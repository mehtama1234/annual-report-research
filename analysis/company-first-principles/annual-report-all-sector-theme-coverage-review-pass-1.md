# Annual Report All-Sector Theme Coverage Review Pass 1

## Purpose

This pass reviews whether the annual-report work really covers the broader `500+` company folder.

The question is:

`Have we only written up the strongest capital-flow themes, or have we covered sectors, industries, consumer trends, industrial trends, cultural trends, financial questions, and deeper first-principles themes across the full company set?`

The structured companion table is:

`analysis/company-first-principles/data/annual-report-all-sector-theme-coverage-review-pass-1.csv`

## Short Answer

We have broad coverage, but not equal deep coverage.

The folder contains:

| Layer | Count | Meaning |
|---|---:|---|
| Company packets | `519` | Broad extracted annual-report packet universe. |
| All-company triage rows | `519` | Every packet was classified into a primary capital-flow or signal lane. |
| High-signal triage rows | `190` | Companies strong enough to revisit for deeper extraction. |
| Lane-queue candidates | `71` | Practical first queue for source extraction. |
| Deep company-analysis files | `50` | Selective first-principles company notes, not full coverage of all packets. |

So the honest answer is:

`We did not deeply write up every one of the 519 companies. We did triage the full set, then built deep themes from the highest-signal clusters.`

That is fine for a strong article, but it is not the same as a full all-sector atlas.

## What We Covered Well

### 1. Power, Grid, And Utilities

This is one of the strongest areas.

We covered electricity as a growth constraint, utility capital plans, load growth, tariffs, energy service agreements, recovery mechanisms, FPL, AEP, Duke, NextEra, contractor beneficiaries, and customer-cash proof limits.

The first-principles question is:

`Who gets permission to spend capital, who pays through bills or contracts, and who earns from the asset after it enters service?`

### 2. Capital Platforms And Private Markets

This is also strong.

We covered big money platforms such as BlackRock, Blackstone, Apollo/Athene, KKR/Global Atlantic, Brookfield, Ares, and Carlyle. We also built a proof standard for AUM, legal entities, statutory schedules, borrower rows, credit vehicles, private funds, and named-cash proof.

The first-principles question is:

`Who has money, what wrapper carries it, where does it go, and what cash comes back?`

### 3. Industrial Uptime

This is strong.

We covered companies that keep physical work moving: equipment rental, MRO supply, electrical distribution, HVAC distribution, construction execution, waterworks, and project backlog.

The first-principles question is:

`Who prevents work from stopping when equipment, parts, technical support, or field execution becomes scarce?`

### 4. Healthcare Access

This is reasonably strong as a reader theme.

We covered payers, pharmacy/service routing, dialysis, infusion, home care, hospice, and specialized care access.

The first-principles question is:

`Who organizes care when patients, families, employers, providers, payers, labor, and reimbursement collide?`

The remaining gap is cash and quality proof.

### 5. Value Retail

This is strong as a consumer behavior theme.

We covered Costco, Walmart, Target, Dollar General, Dollar Tree, Burlington, and Ollie's around the idea that pressured consumers split spending by job and regret type.

The first-principles question is:

`Where can a household spend without feeling careless?`

## What Is Undercovered

### 1. Services And Cultural Consumption

This is the biggest missing broad theme.

The corpus has lodging, restaurants, casinos, apparel stores, advertising, transportation, publishing, entertainment, and air freight. The final article only lightly uses this material.

Potential subthemes:

- travel as postponed release and status repair
- restaurants as convenience, habit, and social infrastructure
- casinos and resorts as experience spending and regional demand
- apparel as identity under budget pressure
- advertising as attention allocation under weak demand
- logistics and delivery as time-saving infrastructure

First-principles question:

`How do people allocate money, time, attention, status, and convenience when budgets feel tight?`

### 2. Consumer Goods And Household Identity

The corpus has packaging, personal products, toys, apparel, food, home-related products, and household goods, but the final article does not fully promote them.

Potential subthemes:

- beauty and personal care as affordable control
- packaging as hidden demand infrastructure
- toys and games as family spending under pressure
- food and staples as habit, trust, and price architecture
- brands as identity shortcuts
- private label versus brand trust

First-principles question:

`What do households keep buying because it helps them feel clean, competent, safe, normal, entertained, or in control?`

### 3. Basic Materials And Input Scarcity

The corpus has steel, copper, lumber, chemicals, fertilizer, gold, packaging materials, and industrial metals. Some of this appears through capex and built-environment themes, but it is not its own full theme.

Potential subthemes:

- copper and electrical metal demand from electrification
- steel and fabrication from infrastructure
- lumber and engineered wood from housing repair and building cycles
- fertilizer and agricultural chemicals from food productivity
- gold and precious metals as financial stress/real-asset demand
- specialty chemicals as hidden industrial inputs

First-principles question:

`Which physical inputs become scarce when power, housing, food, infrastructure, electronics, and defense demand rise?`

### 4. Real Estate And Scarce Locations

Real estate is underdeveloped.

The corpus includes specialty REITs, lodging REITs, towers, property infrastructure, and real-asset platforms.

Potential subthemes:

- towers as scarce wireless locations
- data-center land/power/interconnection as location scarcity
- lodging as travel demand plus asset ownership
- logistics property as delivery infrastructure
- rent burden and shelter affordability
- real estate as financing collateral

First-principles question:

`Who controls locations, leases, access rights, or physical sites that other people need?`

### 5. Labor And Service Capacity

Labor is present inside healthcare, contractors, restaurants, lodging, services, and industrial execution, but it is not pulled into one theme.

Potential subthemes:

- skilled field labor
- caregiver labor
- restaurant and hospitality staffing
- technician availability
- training and retention
- productivity tools that make scarce labor go further

First-principles question:

`Who can organize scarce labor into reliable service?`

### 6. Trust, Safety, And Control

Trust appears in digital security, healthcare, retail, insurance, utilities, and public infrastructure. It could become a cross-sector theme.

Potential subthemes:

- digital security and identity
- healthcare trust and authorization
- financial trust and custody
- brand trust in consumer goods
- infrastructure reliability
- insurance promises
- food/product safety

First-principles question:

`Who gets paid because other people need a trusted gatekeeper?`

This tells us something important.

The broad corpus is not only about consumer narratives or software narratives.

It is heavily about:

- companies spending capital
- companies absorbing physical buildout
- companies buying other companies
- companies refinancing obligations
- companies turning assets into collateral
- companies routing money through legal wrappers

That is why the final article naturally leaned toward bottlenecks and money movement.

## The Honest Coverage Verdict

The current package is strong enough for:

`a bounded article about annual reports as maps of economic bottlenecks`

It is not yet complete enough for:

`a full sector-by-sector atlas of all 519 companies`

The missing broad-sector themes are not fatal. They are optional expansion chapters.

The best additions would be:

1. services and cultural consumption
2. consumer goods and household identity
3. basic materials and input scarcity
4. real estate and scarce locations
5. labor and service capacity
6. trust, safety, and control
