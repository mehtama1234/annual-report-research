# Annual-report energy affordability and supply-route deep dive — pass 1

Research date: 2026-09-17

## Plain-English thesis

People and businesses do not consume crude oil or gas in the ground. They
consume gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, electricity, heat, fertilizer, plastics,
shipping, and manufactured goods. A molecule or electron must move through
production, processing, transport, storage, refining, export, delivery,
contracts, billing, and payment.

The first-principles question is:

> Who controls the route from energy supply to usable energy, and who absorbs
> the cost when that route becomes expensive or unreliable?

This pass uses Energy Transfer, Cheniere, PBF, and Devon as four distinct
routes: midstream logistics, LNG export conversion, refining, and upstream
production plus depletion replacement. It is not a commodity-price ranking.

## What the annual reports reveal

### 1. Energy affordability is a route problem

Energy Transfer shows why supply is not useful until it can be gathered,
processed, transported, fractionated, stored, and exported. Cheniere shows the
same principle at the LNG boundary: local gas becomes globally usable only
after it passes through liquefaction trains, contracts, cargo scheduling, and
debt-financed infrastructure.

PBF turns crude into products households and businesses can actually use.
Devon shows the upstream starting point, where production must replace decline
before a molecule can enter any downstream route.

### 2. Contract visibility does not eliminate physical or financial burden

Cheniere’s roughly 90% contracted anticipated production and Energy Transfer’s
capacity and agreement disclosures make demand visibility stronger. But
contracts do not remove construction, maintenance, gas procurement, debt,
counterparty, or completion risk.

The same discipline applies to Devon. A large operating-cash number can fund
capex, acquisitions, debt repayment, and shareholder payouts in one period,
but a treasury-level bridge does not show which source funded which asset or
whether payout remains covered under lower commodity prices.

### 3. Refining is the conversion and volatility layer

PBF exposes the difference between crude supply and usable energy. Throughput
and gross margin can be strong while turnaround, outage, insurance, working
capital, and refinancing needs consume cash. The correct question is not simply
whether crack spreads are high. It is whether a named refinery converts
feedstock into cash after maintenance, environmental, financing, and
replacement burdens through a cycle.

## QoE and financial-shenanigans controls

1. Separate production, throughput, cargoes, capacity, contracted volume, realized price, and cash collection.
2. Reconcile tariff or contract terms to billed volume, utilization, shipper commitments, customer credit, and settlement.
3. Separate maintenance, growth, turnaround, environmental, and expansion capital before calling free cash flow durable.
4. Test commodity price, crack spread, gas cost, basis, hedge, inventory, and working-capital effects.
5. Treat debt refinancing as a source/use event; reconcile coupon, fees, redemption, make-whole, maturity, and pro forma interest.
6. Keep project, train, refinery, basin, and partnership ownership separate from parent-level EBITDA, DCF, or OCF.
7. For Devon, separate acquired merger cash, divestiture proceeds, treasury drawdowns, operating cash, and financing from asset-level production cash.
8. Normalize insurance recoveries, outage effects, tax timing, depletion, impairment, and “one-time” integration costs.
9. Do not treat contracted capacity, cargo counts, production volume, or refining margin as proof of project return or common-owner cash.

## Owner-cash and valuation handoff

- **Energy Transfer:** distributable cash after maintenance and growth capex, debt, partnership claims, taxes, project funding, and counterparty obligations; named projects require customer cash and contribution evidence.
- **Cheniere:** cash after gas procurement, train maintenance, debt service, sustaining capital, completion cost, customer credit, taxes, and common claims; contracted revenue is a forecast input.
- **PBF:** refinery cash after crude/feedstock, turnaround, environmental, working capital, insurance, debt, taxes, and replacement capital; crack spreads must be normalized.
- **Devon:** cash after decline-replacement capex, LOE, acquisitions, merger integration, debt, taxes, hedges, and payouts; a commodity-stress and reserve-backed model is required.

Damodaran-style valuation must make the price pay for production decline,
throughput, contracted volume, utilization, realized margin, maintenance
capital, debt, and terminal asset life. Lyn Alden-style stress must test fuel
and food affordability, inflation, rates, geopolitics, export constraints,
customer liquidity, energy transition policy, and capital-market access.

## Filing-based thesis breakers

| Company | Breaker observation |
| --- | --- |
| Energy Transfer | Growth capex and named capacity rise without contracted/billed throughput, utilization, project contribution, or sufficient DCF after debt and partnership claims. |
| Cheniere | Cargoes and contracted production rise while train completion, gas cost, debt service, sustaining capital, or customer credit weaken free cash conversion. |
| PBF Energy | Throughput and gross refining margin weaken while turnarounds, insurance normalization, working capital, refinancing cost, or environmental capital consume cash. |
| Devon Energy | Production decline and commodity stress overwhelm capex, merger contribution, debt-service, and payout coverage; acquired assets fail to produce durable cash. |
