# Annual-report healthcare care-infrastructure deep dive — pass 1

Research date: 2026-09-17

## Plain-English thesis

Healthcare demand is not enough to explain the economics. The difficult
question is how human need becomes staffed, scheduled, reimbursed, and
delivered care. In many cases the control point is not a patented treatment.
It is a network of caregivers, clinics, pharmacies, nurses, logistics, payer
relationships, and operating density.

The first-principles question is:

> Who organizes care when medical need, payment rules, labor limits, and
> physical capacity collide?

This pass extends the repository beyond medical devices and drug distribution
into care-site migration, mandatory chronic treatment, and aging-in-place
labor infrastructure.

## What the annual reports reveal

### 1. The care site is an economic variable

Addus, Option Care, and BrightSpring all show that moving care into homes and
communities can lower friction or cost, but only if the operating system works.
The site shift requires staffing, scheduling, pharmacy, authorization,
supplies, data, compliance, and payer settlement.

The customer is therefore buying reliable execution at a lower-friction site.
The patient may value the home; the payer may value avoided institutional cost;
the provider must still deliver safe care. “Home-based” is not a business model
by itself. The control point is the network that makes home delivery repeatable.

### 2. Mandatory demand can still produce weak returns

DaVita demonstrates the strongest recurring-demand case. Dialysis cannot be
deferred like a restaurant visit or home remodel. But durable need does not
eliminate reimbursement, labor, supplies, center utilization, or medical-cost
risk. Risk-based arrangements add an additional burden: the operator may be
paid to manage total cost and may absorb losses if execution fails.

This is a useful correction to demographic investing. Aging and chronic disease
can create stable volume while also creating political pricing pressure and
labor intensity.

### 3. Labor is the hidden scarce asset

Addus exposes the labor layer most clearly. The business depends on caregivers
who can be recruited, retained, scheduled, verified, and paid enough to cover
the reimbursement rate. BrightSpring and Option Care add pharmacists, nurses,
clinicians, and coordinators.

The financial model must therefore treat labor availability as a capacity
constraint, not just an expense percentage. Revenue growth without staffing,
quality, and retention can weaken the service promise and generate compliance,
rework, or payer risk.

## QoE and financial-shenanigans controls

1. Separate patient count, treatment count, prescriptions, census, visits, and revenue per unit.
2. Reconcile payer mix, reimbursement rates, denials, authorization, receivables, and collection timing.
3. Test labor hours, wage rates, clinician/caregiver retention, overtime, agency use, and service quality against margin.
4. Separate acquired volume from organic growth and include purchase price, integration, contingent consideration, debt, and dilution.
5. For risk-based care, separate revenue from medical-cost risk, reserves, shared savings, and actual patient-cost outcomes.
6. For infusion and pharmacy, test drug acquisition costs, rebates, inventory, patient assistance, and pass-through economics.
7. Treat adjusted EBITDA and adjusted EPS as management measures requiring reconciliation to OCF, maintenance capital, leases, debt, taxes, claims, and diluted shares.
8. Keep regulatory settlements, quality events, recoupments, accreditation, and “one-time” integration costs visible until recurrence is tested.

## Owner-cash and valuation handoff

- **Addus:** cash after caregiver wages, branch infrastructure, compliance, acquisitions, maintenance capital, taxes, debt, leases, and dilution. Value depends on local density and sustainable reimbursement.
- **DaVita:** cash after center maintenance, supplies, clinical labor, risk-based medical costs, debt, taxes, capital needs, and common claims. Treatment volume is not the numerator.
- **Option Care:** cash after drug and supply costs, nurse/pharmacy labor, authorization, payer settlement, branch and infusion-site capital, taxes, debt, and dilution.
- **BrightSpring:** cash after pharmacy acquisition and dispensing costs, provider labor, integration, leverage, working capital, capex, taxes, and common claims.

Damodaran-style valuation must make the price pay for volume, reimbursement,
labor productivity, site-of-care conversion, medical-cost control, and
reinvestment. Lyn Alden-style stress must test inflation, wages, rates,
government budgets, payer liquidity, policy changes, drug prices, labor
shortages, and household affordability.

## Filing-based thesis breakers

| Company | Breaker observation |
| --- | --- |
| Addus | Caregiver supply or state reimbursement fails to keep pace with wages, while acquisitions add volume without profitable density or cash conversion. |
| DaVita | Treatment or risk-based volume grows but reimbursement, clinical labor, supplies, or medical-cost risk compresses cash and common residual. |
| Option Care | Site-of-care growth slows, therapy mix worsens, payer cuts reimbursement, or nurse/pharmacy capacity and service intensity consume the expected margin. |
| BrightSpring | Prescription and patient volume rise without durable EBITDA-to-cash conversion, or leverage, reimbursement, labor, and integration burdens overwhelm coordination benefits. |

## Decision and boundary

Status: healthcare-care-infrastructure; organized-access-and-site-of-care-theme;
article-grade; labor-and-reimbursement-visible; owner-cash-and-outcome-open;
no-ranking.

The healthcare lane now extends from tools and distribution into the operating
infrastructure that makes care possible. Its strongest insight is that
demographics create need, but labor, reimbursement, coordination, and site
selection determine whether need becomes durable cash.
