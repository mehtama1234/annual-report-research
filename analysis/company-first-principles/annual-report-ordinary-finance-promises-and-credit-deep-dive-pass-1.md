# Annual-report ordinary finance: promises and credit deep dive — pass 1

Research date: 2026-09-17

## Plain-English thesis

Finance becomes real when a household or business makes a promise: a deposit
must remain accessible, a card balance must be repaid, a loan must be serviced,
an insurance claim must be paid, or a payment must authorize and settle.

The first-principles question is:

> Whose promise is being transformed into someone else’s funding, spending
> power, protection, or payment?

This pass uses JPMorgan, American Express, and Capital One as a bounded
ordinary-finance cohort. They represent broad bank intermediation, premium
card/membership spending, and consumer-credit stress. It is not a bank
valuation ranking and does not claim that large financial platforms create
productive or durable returns automatically.

## What the annual reports reveal

### 1. Finance is a time bridge

A deposit stores purchasing power. A card lets a household spend before
repayment. A loan pulls future income into the present. A payment network lets
commerce settle. A bank or card company is paid for operating that time bridge,
but the bridge becomes fragile when funding costs, credit losses, or regulation
rise.

JPMorgan is useful as a broad macro sensor because it touches many forms of
ordinary money movement. American Express shows the premium-spend and
membership side. Capital One shows the point where spending power becomes
delinquency, charge-off, reserve, and funding-cost pressure.

### 2. Spending growth and credit quality are different observations

American Express can report large billed business and strong consumer spend
while the analyst still needs repayment, rewards, and merchant economics.
Capital One can report card loans and revenue while provisions and charge-offs
consume a large part of pre-provision earnings.

The distinction matters socially. A household may keep spending because it is
healthy, because prices are higher, or because credit is bridging a budget
shortfall. The annual report cannot answer that from purchase volume alone.
Delinquencies, payment rates, vintage performance, charge-offs, and reserves
are the next proof objects.

### 3. Deposits are both an asset and a customer product

JPMorgan and Capital One demonstrate that deposits are not merely cheap
funding. Customers choose whether to keep money at a bank based on access,
rate, trust, convenience, and perceived safety. If the bank raises the rate
paid to retain deposits, funding cost changes. If deposits leave, liquidity and
asset funding change.

The correct analysis therefore separates deposit balance, average balance,
rate paid, beta, maturity, liquidity, and regulatory capital. A large deposit
base is a starting point for analysis, not proof of durable funding.

## QoE and financial-shenanigans controls

1. Separate loan growth, purchase volume, billed business, deposits, fees, and net interest income from actual repayment and collection.
2. Reconcile interest income with funding cost, deposit beta, credit provisions, charge-offs, reserves, and capital requirements.
3. Test delinquencies, vintage performance, payment rates, recoveries, collateral, and allowance coverage before normalizing earnings.
4. Separate premium card fees, merchant discount revenue, rewards, benefits, acquisition spending, fraud, and servicing costs.
5. Keep market and investment gains separate from recurring banking, payment, or card economics.
6. Treat pre-provision earnings as a before-loss measure; it is not owner cash after credit losses.
7. Separate Discover integration cost, network economics, loan migration, and synergy from Capital One’s legacy card and auto books.
8. Treat CET1 and capital return as constraints and allocations, not evidence of stress-cycle safety.
9. Keep AUM, deposits, billed business, loan commitments, fair value, and gross transaction volume distinct from legal-entity cash.

## Owner-cash and valuation handoff

- **JPMorgan:** earnings and capital generation after credit losses, deposit funding cost, operating expense, technology, regulatory capital, preferred claims, and common distributions. Markets revenue requires cycle normalization.
- **American Express:** network and card economics after rewards, benefits, merchant costs, funding, credit losses, acquisition, service, taxes, and diluted shares. Billed business is an activity denominator, not owner cash.
- **Capital One:** loss-adjusted card/auto spread and payment economics after provisions, charge-offs, deposit cost, integration, marketing, rewards, capital, taxes, and dilution.

Damodaran-style valuation must make the price pay for net interest margin,
credit losses, deposit cost, fee growth, payment volume, capital requirements,
and normalized market revenue. Lyn Alden-style stress must test rates,
inflation, household affordability, unemployment, deposit competition,
liquidity, regulation, refinancing, collateral values, and nominal claims.

## Filing-based thesis breakers

| Company | Breaker observation |
| --- | --- |
| JPMorgan | Deposit cost and credit losses rise while loan growth or market revenue weakens; capital requirements and technology/regulatory burden reduce common-owner residual. |
| American Express | Premium spend or billed business slows, fee-paying retention weakens, rewards and benefits rise faster than fees, or delinquencies and write-offs undermine the premium network economics. |
| Capital One | Charge-offs, delinquencies, and provisions remain elevated while NIM compresses or deposits become more expensive; Discover integration fails to add network economics after capital and execution costs. |
