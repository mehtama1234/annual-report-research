# Annual Report Power Scarcity Chapter Polish Pass 1

## Purpose

This pass turns the selected first chapter into a writing-ready artifact.

The chapter is not trying to prove that every power project has already produced named cash receipts. It is trying to explain a bigger finding from the annual-report work: electricity is becoming a binding operating constraint, and companies are reorganizing capital, contracts, regulation, procurement, and customer access around that constraint.

## Chapter Thesis

The power chapter should open with a simple point:

`AI, cloud computing, electrification, manufacturing reshoring, and reliability needs are turning electricity from a background utility expense into a front-line growth constraint. Annual reports show that the scarce asset is not just generation. The scarce asset is the ability to deliver dependable power through generation, transmission, interconnection, customer contracts, regulatory recovery, turbines, construction labor, and balance-sheet capacity.`

In plain words: the economy wants more electricity than the system can easily deliver. That makes power capacity, grid connection, dispatchability, and rate recovery economically important.

## What The Annual Reports Show

The strongest evidence is not one perfect project-return model. The strongest evidence is that many different company types are pointing to the same bottleneck.

Constellation shows the clean-reliable-generation side. It has a large nuclear-heavy fleet, raised 2026 earnings guidance, and signed new long-term clean-power PPAs. That says large customers are willing to contract for dependable clean supply.

Vistra shows the dispatchable-power side. Its adjusted EBITDA and commercial availability in extreme heat show that controllable generation has value when the grid is stressed.

NRG shows the customer-access side. Its retail customer base and generation position create a route from end-user demand into contracted power supply, including power arrangements tied to large data-center style demand.

Exelon shows the wires side. Almost 11 million customers and a large capital plan show that transmission and distribution are part of the bottleneck, not passive infrastructure in the background.

Duke shows the regulated-load-growth side. Its data-center ESAs, high-confidence pipeline, gas-turbine procurement, and 2026-2030 capital plan show that large-load demand can become a utility capital program.

AEP shows the grid-gateway side. Its disclosed new-load pipeline, larger capital plan, secured turbine capacity, and large-load tariff mechanics show the bridge from data-center demand into utility obligation, study, reimbursement, and capital investment.

NextEra and FPL show the regulated-recovery side. FPL's SPPCRC evidence shows a route from approved cost category to factor authority and aggregate clause revenue. NextEra Energy Resources adds the developer backlog and origination side.

Sterling and MasTec show the contractor and implementation side. Their backlog, revenue, adjusted EBITDA, operating cash flow, contract-liability, credit, L/C, and surety evidence show that the power buildout becomes work for construction and infrastructure contractors before it becomes final utility earnings.

## Proof Ladder

The chapter should use a proof ladder instead of a single overclaim.

Level 1 is demand visibility: data centers, electrification, reliability, and customer growth are visible.

Level 2 is obligation visibility: PPAs, ESAs, tariffs, letters of agreement, study fees, reimbursement mechanics, and backlog are visible in places.

Level 3 is capital deployment visibility: utility capital plans, turbine orders, approved projects, contractor backlog, and capex are visible.

Level 4 is recovery or cash-proxy visibility: regulated recovery, adjusted EBITDA, DCF, revenue, contract liabilities, and operating cash flow are visible in some rows.

Level 5 is named-cash proof: customer receipt, billing determinant, debt waterfall, legal-entity cash movement, collateral certificate, and project-return model. The current power chapter mostly does not reach Level 5.

That boundary is the key discipline. The chapter can say the power bottleneck is real in company reporting. It cannot say every named customer dollar has been traced to a named asset return.

## Reader Takeaway

The reader-facing takeaway is:

`The annual reports do not just say power demand is high. They show the money path forming around power scarcity: large customers create demand, utilities and developers turn demand into contracts and capital plans, suppliers and contractors turn plans into physical work, regulators decide how costs are recovered, and investors get paid only if that chain converts into bills, receipts, and earned returns.`

## Investment And Business Takeaway

The useful investment takeaway is not simply "buy power stocks."

The useful takeaway is that power scarcity creates multiple choke points:

- clean dependable generation
- dispatchable generation
- retail customer access
- transmission and distribution
- interconnection and load-study control
- turbine and equipment availability
- construction capacity
- regulatory recovery
- customer billing and receipt collection

Companies exposed to these choke points can benefit, but the proof standard changes by company type. A merchant generator needs power-price and availability evidence. A regulated utility needs load, capex, approval, recovery, and earned-return evidence. A contractor needs backlog conversion and cash collection evidence. An asset manager needs source/use, collateral, waterfall, and return evidence.

## Safe Claim

`Annual reports show power scarcity becoming a broad economic control point. The evidence is strongest as a cross-company mechanism: customer demand moves into contracts, tariffs, capital plans, procurement, construction, regulatory recovery, and company cash proxies. The current evidence does not yet prove full named-customer receipt, legal-entity cash movement, debt waterfall, collateral support, or project-level return for every asset.`
