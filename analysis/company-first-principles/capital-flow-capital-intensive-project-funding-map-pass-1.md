## Funding Evidence Types

| Evidence type | Meaning | Claim strength |
|---|---|---|
| Company cash capacity | Operating cash, DCF, or cash conversion can support investment. | Useful, but not project-specific. |
| Facility capacity | Revolver, ABL, securitization, or credit facility availability exists. | Funding capacity, not use-of-proceeds proof. |
| Direct funding split | The company discloses equity-funded or debt-funded growth capital. | Stronger, but still needs project/entity mapping. |
| Debt stack context | Long-term debt, entity debt, maturities, and leverage frame the buildout. | Necessary risk context, not proof of project economics. |
| Funding gap | Capacity/backlog/project evidence exists, but source-of-funds is not mapped. | Explicitly below project-and-cash-grade. |

## Theme And Subtheme Interpretation

This funding pass sits inside the broader theme:

`capital-intensive real-economy buildout`

The reason this theme matters is simple: the most useful capital-flow evidence is often found where money has to become physical capacity. Pipelines, LNG trains, export terminals, rental fleets, site-development work, data-center civil work, and utility-scale infrastructure cannot be built with narrative alone. They require cash, debt, revolvers, project finance, customer commitments, retained earnings, securitization capacity, or some mix of these.

The current subthemes are:

| Subtheme | What It Tracks | Companies In This Pass | What The Evidence Currently Says |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operating cash support for growth capex | Whether recurring cash generation is large enough to support the investment program. | ET, URI, Sterling | ET's adjusted EBITDA and DCF are large relative to growth-capex guidance; URI's operating cash flow exceeds H1 gross rental capex; Sterling generates operating cash in excess of company capex. |
| Facility and liquidity backstop | Whether committed credit capacity exists around the buildout. | ET, URI, Sterling | ET has unused five-year credit-facility capacity, URI has liquidity through ABL and AR securitization capacity, and Sterling expanded its revolver to support a larger platform. |
| Direct funding split | Whether the company explicitly separates equity-funded, debt-funded, or otherwise funded growth capital. | Cheniere | Cheniere is the cleanest current example because it discloses equity-funded growth capital in Q2 and H1 2026. |
| Debt-stack dependency | Whether the buildout is being carried on a large balance sheet that needs maturity, covenant, and refinancing analysis. | ET, Cheniere, URI, Sterling | All four need credit-agreement and maturity-ladder work before the funding story is project-and-cash-grade. |
| Customer/project funding chain | Whether backlog, customer demand, or physical output can be tied to the customer or project owner that funds the work. | Sterling, ET, Cheniere | This remains the biggest gap for Sterling backlog and for named ET/Cheniere projects. Company capacity exists, but project owner funding is not fully proven. |
| Asset conversion | Whether funded capital becomes measurable output such as cargoes, volumes, fleet OEC, backlog conversion, or completed infrastructure. | Cheniere, URI, ET, Sterling | Cheniere has the strongest physical-output denominator through DOE LNG vessel rows and terminal volumes; URI has OEC and fleet productivity; ET has NGL/export denominator work; Sterling has backlog and awards but needs project-level conversion. |

In other words, the subtheme ladder is:

`cash generation -> credit capacity -> funding split -> debt stack -> project/customer source-of-funds -> physical output`

That ladder is useful because it prevents us from overstating the claim. A company can have strong cash flow and still fund a specific project with debt. A company can have backlog and still depend on a customer, municipality, hyperscaler, utility, or energy producer to provide the true project funding. A company can report capex and still leave the use-of-proceeds ambiguous.

The next analytical standard should therefore be:

`Do not call something fully funded unless we can identify the cash source, debt facility, customer commitment, project owner, or project-finance vehicle behind the activity.`

## Simple Bottom Line

In simple words:

`The funding story is now partly visible. ET and URI look like cash-generating platforms with credit-facility support. Cheniere gives the cleanest direct funding split because it tells us how much growth capital was equity-funded. Sterling has company-level cash and a much larger revolver, but its real funding question is upstream: who is funding the customer projects inside backlog?`

The important boundary:

`Company funding capacity is not the same as project funding proof.`
