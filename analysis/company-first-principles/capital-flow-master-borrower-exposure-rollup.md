## Simple Read

Across the completed borrower cases, the strongest visible pattern is services-heavy lending.

The money is showing up in:

- facility services and commercial services
- roadway safety and infrastructure-adjacent services

- insurance brokerage roll-ups

## What This Says

In simple words: private credit is not just lending to abstract financial vehicles. In these cases it is funding ordinary operating-company platforms.

But the evidence is uneven.

For several companies, SEC schedules show actual holder-dollar exposure. For Jiffy, the transaction value is strong but the debt-holding evidence is still missing. For Atwell, the prior bank facility is known, but the replacement claim is not proven.

That gives us a disciplined thesis:

Private credit is visibly financing services and industrial operating-company platforms, especially sponsor-backed roll-ups. The strongest claims are borrower-level holder exposure claims. The weaker claims are bank-displacement claims unless we find payoff, termination, amendment, or credit-agreement evidence.

## What We Need To Do Next

1. Build a holder-level source-of-funds map.

   For each borrower, classify the visible lender/holder as BDC, private fund, insurance-linked vehicle, or other credit vehicle.

2. Add quarterly movement.

   Compare Q1 2026, Q2 2026, and the next available quarter for each borrower. Track new positions, increases, paydowns, exits, maturity changes, rate changes, and new unfunded commitments.

3. Hunt denominators.

   For every borrower, search for credit agreements, lender presentations, rating reports, UCC records, sponsor releases, and legal-advisor financing releases that disclose total debt package size.

4. Test bank displacement narrowly.

   Do not ask whether banks are disappearing. Ask whether this exact borrower had bank debt that was repaid, terminated, amended, subordinated, or retained alongside private credit.

5. Add operating use-of-proceeds.

   Classify each financing as acquisition finance, refinancing, dividend recap, growth capex, working capital, restructuring, or unknown.

6. Expand from nine cases to a repeatable sample.

   Run the same table for the next wave of Ares, ARCC, OBDC, BXSL, FSK, GSBD, K-FITS, ASIF, and insurance-platform filings.

## Evidence Standard

| Claim Type | Strong Enough Evidence |
|---|---|
| Holder-dollar exposure | SEC schedule row with borrower name, holder, period, fair value, and instrument. |
| Transaction value | Company announcement, 8-K/proxy, sale agreement, or credible transaction release. |
| Financing role | Lender, sponsor, company, legal-advisor, or rating-agency source naming arranger/agent/lender role. |
| Facility size | Credit agreement, rating report, debt commitment letter, lender deck, or audited borrower disclosure. |
| Bank displacement | Termination, payoff, amendment, refinancing, or before/after debt-source evidence. |

## Next Best Research Questions

- Which industries show repeated borrower-level private-credit exposure rather than isolated one-offs?
- Which borrowers have visible unfunded commitments, suggesting capital available for add-ons or growth?
- Which sponsor-backed roll-ups are financed by credit vehicles connected to insurance liabilities?
- Which cases have enough evidence to prove bank replacement rather than just private-credit participation?
- Which BDC schedules reveal the same borrower across multiple vehicles, suggesting broader syndication?
- Which transaction announcements name private-credit arranger roles but have not yet appeared in SEC holder schedules?

## Current Answer

The big picture is this:

Private credit appears to be funding the consolidation layer of the real economy. It is showing up behind service networks, maintenance providers, infrastructure contractors, insurance brokers, wealth advisers, and industrial supply-chain platforms.

The next step is not more broad narrative. It is a borrower-by-borrower proof system: dollars, holder, source of funds, use of proceeds, denominator, and bank-status outcome.
