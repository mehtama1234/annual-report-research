# Integrated oil and gas Q2 2026 cash-quality refresh

Research date: `2026-09-17`  
Status: `qualified; cycle-and-replacement-open; no-ranking`

## Finding

The current ConocoPhillips and Exxon Mobil filings show why integrated oil and
gas must be analyzed as a commodity-exposure and replacement system rather
than as a simple operating-cash screen. Both companies produced large first-
half cash surpluses before shareholder distributions, but the surplus is
exposed to realized prices, field decline, project spending, refining and
chemical margins, geopolitical disruption, reserves replacement, and claims
or other exceptional items. The evidence supports a physical-asset and
integrated-control-point thesis; it does not yet support a normalized
common-owner ranking.

Mechanical H1 OCF less the principal reported capital line is approximately
`$5.757B` for ConocoPhillips (`$11.729B - $5.972B`) and `$19.263B` for Exxon
Mobil (`$32.260B - $12.997B`). These are diagnostic residuals, not owner-cash
denominators: they do not allocate maintenance versus growth capital, exclude
all claims, normalize prices, or prove reserve replacement.

## Control point and burden map

ConocoPhillips controls upstream acreage, reserves, reservoir execution, LNG
investments, and global marketing. Exxon adds a larger integrated system of
upstream production, refining, chemicals, specialty products, trading, and
logistics. The control point is the ability to convert scarce reserves,
processing capacity, and advantaged projects into delivered molecules and
products.

The burden sits in decline and depletion, exploration and development capital,
royalties and taxes, refining turnarounds, energy and feedstock costs,
environmental and abandonment obligations, geopolitical interruptions,
working-capital swings, debt and interest, partner/NCI claims, and the
possibility that a high-return project requires spending before cash arrives.

## QoE and financial-shenanigans diagnostics

1. **Price versus volume:** ConocoPhillips states that the H1 increase in
   operating cash was driven by higher commodity prices, partly offset by
   working-capital timing. Its H1 production fell primarily because of normal
   field decline. Exxon reports H1 upstream earnings benefited from higher
   crude realizations and advantaged volume growth, while Middle East
   disruption, divestments, and depreciation reduced the result. A price-led
   cash improvement is not the same as a durable volume or cost improvement.
2. **Capitalization and replacement:** DD&A, PP&E additions, exploration,
   appraisal, and development must be reconciled to the barrels and product
   capacity that remain after the period. A mechanical OCF-less-capex figure
   can overstate owner cash when current production consumes reserves or when
   project spending is deferred.
3. **Exceptional and derivative effects:** Exxon separately identifies losses
   from financial reserves, impairments, supply disruptions, and derivative
   timing. The right test is not to erase every identified item: it is to
   determine whether the item is recurring, cash-paid, operationally embedded,
   or a genuine one-time transfer between periods.
4. **Working-capital timing:** Exxon reports a `$3.857B` H1 operational
   working-capital use. ConocoPhillips attributes part of the OCF comparison to
   working-capital timing. Inventory, payables, receivables, derivatives, and
   advances therefore need period-matched reversal testing before a margin or
   cash-quality conclusion.
5. **Acquisition and contingent claims:** ConocoPhillips' final `$81M` Surmont
   contingent payment is a concrete example of acquisition consideration that
   becomes cash after the headline transaction. Exxon also reports contingent
   consideration payments and a post-redomiciliation legal-entity transition;
   per-share and legal-entity comparisons must preserve those boundaries.
6. **Capital returns:** Repurchases and dividends are uses of cash, not proof
   that cash generation is recurring. The denominator must first survive
   maintenance/replacement capital, taxes, debt service, environmental claims,
   and a lower-price scenario.

## Owner-cash and valuation workbench

The next defensible screen is:

`realized commodity price × production × netback`
`+ refining / chemical / trading margin`
`- operating cost - royalties / taxes - maintenance and replacement capital`
`- interest - environmental / abandonment claims - dilution`
`= normalized common-owner residual`

Damodaran-style valuation should model price, volume, margin, reinvestment,
reserve life, terminal growth, and cost of capital as explicit assumptions.
The present filings provide strong observed inputs, but not a company-disclosed
maintenance/replacement schedule or a through-cycle reserve-backed cash
denominator. The correct output remains an expectation screen and sensitivity
range, not an intrinsic-value conclusion.

Lyn Alden-style stress should test lower nominal commodity prices, higher
service and construction costs, stronger dollar effects, rates and debt
refinancing, geopolitical supply interruptions, and whether shareholder
returns continue to depend on balance-sheet liquidity. Exxon’s working-capital
use and identified disruption/reserve losses make that stress surface visible;
ConocoPhillips’ production decline, LNG commitments, and development program
make replacement and project timing the decisive durability questions.

## Promotion consequence

`physical-asset-qualified; cycle-and-replacement-open; no-ranking`

The next upgrade requires a same-period, company-specific schedule that joins
production and reserve replacement to maintenance/development capital,
realized prices and netbacks, claims, debt, and diluted common-owner cash. Do
not promote production growth, integrated segment earnings, mechanical
OCF-less-capex, dividends, or repurchases into normalized owner cash.
