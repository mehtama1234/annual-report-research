# McDonald's Corporation First-Principles Analysis

Date baseline: 2026-08-30

Company: McDonald's Corporation  
Ticker: MCD  
Sector: Services  
Industry: Restaurants  
Main question: why does McDonald's still make so much cash when U.S. visits are weak, and what do loyalty, the `$5 Meal Deal`, restaurant property, store technology, and the Grinch socks tell us?

## Core First-Principles Read

### Plain answer

McDonald's still makes a lot of cash because it gets paid in several ways from many busy restaurants.

### Why

Customers buy the food. Franchisees run many of the stores. McDonald's also collects rent and fees. The app and promotions try to bring customers back.

### Main doubt

The parent company can look strong while customers visit less and franchisees earn less.

### What we need to check

We need to separate four things: visits, prices, store profit, and parent-company cash.

McDonald's is not just a burger seller. It is a company that controls busy restaurant sites, charges franchisees rent and fees, uses the app to bring people back, and uses famous campaigns to make people show up.

Start with the place. A McDonald's on the right road or corner gets first shot at people who are hungry, busy, and nearby. That matters before the menu matters. Many franchisees run the restaurants, but McDonald's still gets paid through rent and fees.

Then look at speed. A restaurant has limited kitchen space, workers, drive-thru space, and customer patience. If more orders move through the same restaurant with less waiting and fewer mistakes, the same building can make more money.

That is why the app and store technology matter. McDonald's says Ready on Arrival uses phone location to tell the crew when a mobile-order customer is getting close. It also says this can cut wait times by more than 50%. The Google Cloud work matters because McDonald's wants more computing power inside restaurants, not only in a faraway data center.

The loyalty question is simple: does joining the program make people come more often? McDonald's says U.S. MyMcDonald's Rewards members more than double their visits in the first year after joining. That is the key claim. The next question is whether the app caused those extra visits, or whether frequent customers were the ones most likely to join.

The `$5 Meal Deal` is also not just a cheap meal. It is McDonald's telling customers, "fast food can still be worth the price." That matters because many people came to feel fast food had become too expensive.

The Grinch socks were not random. Sherwood reported that McDonald's had its biggest sales day ever and sold about 50 million pairs of socks globally. That shows McDonald's can make a meal feel like an event or collectible, not only lunch.

## Customer Insight

### Plain answer

McDonald's is selling an easy decision as much as it is selling food.

### Why

People often want food that is nearby, familiar, fast, and cheap enough. The app and the value menu reduce the effort and worry involved in choosing a meal.

### Main doubt

Discounts may create visits that do not make much money.

### What we need to check

We need repeat visits after the discount ends, the number of extra items in the order, and profit per order.

The customer is not always thinking, "I want McDonald's." Often the thought is simpler: "I need food fast, I know what it will cost, and I do not want to think too hard."

That is why small price differences matter. McDonald's is not only competing with Burger King or Wendy's. It is competing with eating at home, skipping the meal, buying gas-station food, or grabbing prepared food from a grocery store.

The `$5 Meal Deal` tells the customer there is still a cheap option. The app lets McDonald's give one person a deal without lowering every price for everyone.

The risk is that customers learn to wait for deals. If a person buys only the `$5` meal and stacks another app coupon on top, the visit may hurt restaurant profit. That is why the next pass must ask: did the deal bring back normal visits, or only discounted visits?

### Deeper Why Chain

Why do people return when the food is easy to copy? Because the decision is familiar. People know the menu, taste, speed, and price range before they arrive.

Why does a `$5` meal matter? Because it changes the fairness question. The customer may not be asking, "Can I afford it?" They may be asking, "Is this fast-food meal a rip-off?"

Why does loyalty matter? Because McDonald's can see who came, what they bought, what offer they used, and whether they returned.

Why might loyalty look better than it is? Because app sales can rise when old customers move into the app. The hard proof is more visits, not just more app orders.

Why do socks matter? Because 50 million sock pairs sold through a meal deal show that McDonald's can create demand beyond normal food hunger.

## Operating Insight

### Plain answer

McDonald's can keep parent-company cash high even when running a store becomes harder.

### Why

Franchisees pay rent and fees. They also carry much of the cost of workers, food, discounts, remodels, and technology.

### Main doubt

The system may be moving pressure from McDonald's to its franchisees.

### What we need to check

We need store-level profit, franchisee debt, closures, new openings, and willingness to pay for remodels and technology.

McDonald's makes money in two linked ways. First, restaurants sell food. Second, the parent company collects rent and fees from many franchised restaurants.

That is why parent-company profit can look strong even when restaurant work is hard. Labor, food cost, discounts, remodels, and technology spending may hit franchisees first.

The important question is: are franchisees still making enough after rent, labor, discounts, remodels, and tech spending? If not, parent cash flow can look better than the health of the whole system.

### Where The Pressure Goes

When customers resist prices, the cost can move to different places:

- Customers pay higher prices and some stop coming.
- Franchisees accept lower profit to keep prices attractive.
- McDonald's gives more support or accepts less profit.
- Suppliers or workers get squeezed.

For McDonald's, the danger is that the parent company keeps reporting strong cash while franchisees carry more of the problem.

### The Store Tech Question

Ready on Arrival is easy to understand. If the restaurant knows a customer is nearby, it can start the order sooner. The customer waits less. The kitchen can plan better.

Edge computing is the same idea inside the restaurant. More data can be handled locally. That may help with order timing, equipment problems, kiosks, drive-thru speed, and kitchen flow.

The test is not whether the technology sounds advanced. The test is whether it cuts wait time, reduces mistakes, prevents equipment downtime, and lowers labor needed per order.

## Investment Read

### Plain answer

McDonald's is a strong cash business, but the latest sales growth does not yet prove stronger customer demand.

### Why

U.S. sales rose in Q2 2026 while guest counts fell. Higher prices or larger orders can produce that result.

### Main doubt

If visits keep falling, the company may be using price increases and discounts to hide weaker demand.

### What we need to check

We need the next quarters split into guest counts, price, order size, and profit after discounts.

McDonald's is a strong cash business, but that does not automatically make the stock attractive at any price. FY2025 operating cash flow was about `123%` of net income. Free cash flow before dividends and buybacks was roughly `$7.186B`.

The strength is simple: people get hungry often, the brand is familiar, the locations are convenient, and the app can bring people back.

The risk is also simple: U.S. visits were weak in Q2 2026. If growth comes mostly from price, mix, discounts, and one-time campaigns, the business is less healthy than the headline numbers suggest.

Questions for an investor:

- How much cash does McDonald's make after the spending needed to keep restaurants modern?
- Are sales rising because more people come, or because each visit costs more?
- Did loyalty create more visits, or just move existing visits into the app?
- Are franchisees making enough money after rent, wages, food, discounts, remodels, and tech?
- Were buybacks done at a good price?

### Tests

Test 1: The app works if members keep visiting more often after the first year, not only right after joining.

Test 2: The `$5 Meal Deal` works if people come back later and buy normal meals, not only discounted bundles.

Test 3: The property model works if franchisees can still make enough money after paying rent and fees.

Test 4: Store technology works if wait times, mistakes, downtime, and labor per order fall.

Test 5: Grinch-style events work if they bring new or returning customers who come back after the event ends.

## Deep Questions

### 1. Did loyalty actually make people come more often?

McDonald's says U.S. rewards members more than double visits in the first year after joining. That sounds powerful, but the sentence does not prove cause by itself.

The clean question is: what did those same people do before they joined? If they were already the heaviest McDonald's users, the app may be counting loyal customers rather than creating them. If their visits rose after joining, and similar non-members did not rise, then loyalty likely changed behavior.

What would prove it:

- visits per member in the 12 months before joining and 12 months after joining;
- a comparison group of similar customers who did not join;
- repeat visits after the first-year offer rush ends;
- order size after rewards are redeemed.

What would weaken it:

- most new members were already frequent customers;
- app sales rose while total U.S. visits stayed weak;
- members visited more only when large discounts were offered.

### 2. Did the `$5 Meal Deal` repair trust, or did it only buy cheap visits?

The `$5 Meal Deal` matters because many customers had started to feel fast food was too expensive. The meal says, in plain language: "you can still get a full meal here for a price that feels fair."

But a cheap meal can do two very different things. It can remind customers that McDonald's is worth visiting, or it can train customers to wait for discounts. Those outcomes look similar on day one and very different three months later.

What would prove it worked:

- customers who bought the `$5` meal came back later for normal-price items;
- average order size rose because people added fries, drinks, desserts, or extra sandwiches;
- U.S. guest counts improved after McValue without a large profit drop.

What would weaken it:

- customers bought only the `$5` meal;
- app coupons were stacked on top of the deal;
- franchisees saw more orders but worse profit per order.

### 3. Who pays when McDonald's promises value?

"Value" sounds customer-friendly, but somebody pays for it. The cost can land on the franchisee, the parent company, suppliers, workers, or customers through higher prices elsewhere.

This matters because McDonald's parent company can still report strong cash while franchisees carry more of the burden. If the parent collects rent and fees while operators absorb discounts, remodel costs, wage pressure, and technology spending, the parent numbers can look better than the store reality.

What would prove the system is healthy:

- franchisee profit stays strong after discounts and wage increases;
- remodel and technology spending does not raise operator debt too much;
- store closures do not rise;
- franchisees keep opening and renewing restaurants.

What would weaken it:

- franchisee profit falls while parent cash stays high;
- operators push back on required spending;
- stores need more discounts to keep traffic from falling.

### 4. Are U.S. sales rising for the right reason?

Q2 2026 U.S. comparable sales rose `0.8%`, but McDonald's said guest counts were down. That means sales rose even though fewer visits came through the restaurants.

That can happen when prices are higher, people buy a different mix of items, or discounts pull in enough dollars without raising visits. For a mature restaurant chain, more visits are better evidence than higher dollars per visit, because there is a limit to how much price can rise before customers leave.

What would prove improvement:

- guest counts turn positive;
- sales rise without relying mainly on price;
- low-income customers return;
- peers with similar customers do worse, showing McDonald's gained share.

What would weaken it:

- traffic stays negative;
- sales depend on higher checks;
- value deals grow but normal-price visits do not.

### 5. Does Ready on Arrival actually improve the restaurant?

Ready on Arrival is useful only if it changes the work inside the store. If the restaurant knows the customer is close, it can start the order at the right time. That should mean less waiting, fresher food, fewer angry pickup customers, and better use of workers.

The Google Cloud edge work should be judged the same way. It is not important because it sounds technical. It is important only if the restaurant handles more orders with fewer mistakes and less downtime.

What would prove it:

- wait time falls in normal busy restaurants, not only test stores;
- order accuracy improves;
- equipment downtime falls;
- labor minutes per order falls;
- drive-thru and pickup lines move faster.

What would weaken it:

- wait-time claims do not show up in broad store data;
- the system adds cost and training burden without better service;
- franchisees pay for technology before seeing store-level benefits.

### 6. Did the Grinch socks create real demand or just a one-day spike?

The sock story matters because Sherwood reported McDonald's biggest sales day ever and about 50 million sock pairs sold globally. A restaurant chain briefly became a sock seller. That is strange enough to study.

The question is whether the event brought new or lapsed customers back into the system, or whether it only pulled forward one visit from people who already planned to go. A big campaign day is not enough. The follow-up behavior matters.

What would prove it mattered:

- new or lapsed customers came in during the campaign;
- those customers came back after the socks were gone;
- the meal and socks added profit after product, labor, and marketing costs;
- social attention led to measurable store visits.

What would weaken it:

- sales spiked for one day and faded;
- the campaign mostly attracted existing heavy users;
- the socks created buzz but little profit.

### 7. Are buybacks helping owners?

McDonald's returned a lot of cash through dividends and buybacks. That can be good, but only if the stock is bought at a sensible price.

The plain test is this: after McDonald's pays the money needed to keep restaurants modern, how much owner cash is left per share, and what price did the company pay to retire shares? A buyback helps when each remaining share owns more future cash at a fair cost. It hurts when the company overpays.

What would prove buybacks helped:

- shares were bought at a strong owner-earnings yield;
- per-share cash earnings rose for reasons beyond accounting;
- buybacks did not crowd out needed store investment.

What would weaken it:

- repurchases happened at a high price while U.S. visits were weak;
- store investment needs rose after cash was returned;
- debt rose mainly to fund shareholder returns.

## Deeper Insights

1. McDonald's wins because it gives people a known answer when they are hungry and busy.
2. Loyalty matters only if it makes people come more often.
3. The `$5 Meal Deal` is a test of whether customers still believe McDonald's is worth the price.
4. High parent-company profit comes partly from rent and fees, not just selling food.
5. Store technology matters because seconds, mistakes, and broken equipment cost real money at McDonald's size.
6. The Grinch socks showed that McDonald's can make a meal feel like a collectible event.
7. The biggest warning sign is positive U.S. sales with negative U.S. guest counts.
8. The stock question is not "is McDonald's good?" It is "how much future visit growth and pricing power is already in the stock price?"

## What Would Prove This Wrong

- Loyalty members do not keep visiting more often after the first year.
- Loyalty sales mainly replace non-app sales.
- `$5 Meal Deal` customers do not return for normal-price orders.
- Franchisee profit weakens after rent, labor, discounts, remodels, and technology costs.
- Ready on Arrival does not cut wait time or mistakes.
- Grinch-style campaigns create one-day spikes but no repeat visits.
- U.S. traffic stays negative while sales depend on price increases.

## Reflection And Next Questions

This is now a better McDonald's pass, but it still needs more proof.

Next questions:

- What happened to U.S. traffic after McValue and the `$5 Meal Deal`?
- How many new loyalty members kept visiting after the first year?
- What was the average order after a `$5 Meal Deal` redemption?
- How much did franchisees pay for discounts, remodels, and technology?
- Did Ready on Arrival improve wait time in normal busy stores, not only pilot stores?
- Did Grinch meal buyers come back after the campaign?

Next companies to compare:

- Starbucks: does loyalty raise visits, or mostly reward existing regulars?
- Domino's: does digital ordering change repeat orders better than McDonald's?
- Yum Brands: how does a mostly franchised system look without McDonald's property strength?
- Restaurant Brands: does a multi-brand franchisor have weaker customer data?
- Chipotle: what happens when a restaurant chain is less franchised and more premium-priced?
