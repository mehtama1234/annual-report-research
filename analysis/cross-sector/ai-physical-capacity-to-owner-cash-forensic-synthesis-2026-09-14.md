# AI Physical Capacity: From Compute Demand to Owner Cash

Date: `2026-09-14`

## The question

AI demand is not one product category. It is a connected system that includes
compute architecture, semiconductor manufacturing, network movement, testing,
security, power, cooling, real estate, and field deployment.

The investment question is not “which companies benefit from AI?” It is:

`which company controls a necessary step, who pays for the step, who carries
the physical and liquidity burden, and what cash remains after replacement and
growth investment?`

This page combines the archive's semiconductor, optical/network, security,
validation, AI-capacity, electrical, and infrastructure work. It is a
cross-company proof page, not a recommendation.

The layers are economically different. A semiconductor tool maker can have
high gross margins but remain tied to customer fab budgets. A contractor can
have a large backlog but must first pay labor and suppliers and carry project
estimate risk. A data-center owner can have contracted demand but must fund
power and construction before lease-up. A software platform can show strong
cash while its stock compensation, inference cost, and required infrastructure
are rising.

This bridge makes the burden transfer visible. NVIDIA and the process-control
companies capture high-value technical control but must fund supply, R&D,
qualification, and customer concentration. Vertiv and the contractors sit
closer to the physical build and therefore show larger capital, labor, and
working-capital claims. Equinix and Digital Realty can have signed demand but
must fund power and construction before revenue is fully available. Security
and software platforms have lighter physical assets but carry serving cost,
trust, SBC, and remediation claims.

The important question is not which screen is numerically largest. It is which
company can maintain the control point through a customer-capex slowdown while
still funding the replacement, support, and balance-sheet obligations that
make the relationship valuable.

These are different businesses, so the table is not a ranking. NVIDIA's
commitment perimeter can make an architecture company more exposed to customer
liquidity and infrastructure utilization than direct PP&E suggests. Vertiv's
backlog can be valuable while acquisition and working-capital demands absorb
the cash needed to fulfill it. Equinix's recurring-capex screen is useful only
alongside the much larger total property investment and debt burden. Quanta's
RPO is visibility into future work, not cash that can be distributed before
labor, retainage, claims, equipment, and acquisitions are funded.

The common falsifier is a widening gap between visibility and owner cash:
backlog or commitments rise, but collections slow, inventory or contract assets
rise, margins fall, capital spending accelerates, or guarantees and debt grow
faster than the cash produced by the completed work.

## The order-to-cash chain

The same AI project can pass through the following sequence:

1. A cloud or enterprise customer decides that more compute capacity is worth
   funding.
2. Architecture and software determine how efficiently the workload runs.
3. Semiconductor suppliers manufacture, inspect, package, and test the devices.
4. Optical, switching, and security layers move and protect the data.
5. Power, cooling, backup generation, and electrical interfaces make the site
   usable.
6. Real-estate owners provide powered space and interconnection.
7. Contractors install the physical system and connect it to the grid and
   network.
8. The customer must then run the system at enough utilization to pay for
   depreciation, financing, maintenance, and replacement.

Demand at step one is not cash at step eight. Each step can delay, reject,
reprice, or finance the next step.

## Who controls the valuable relationship?

### Architecture and software

NVIDIA reaches developers, cloud operators, system builders, and end users.
Its software ecosystem can make the architecture difficult to replace. The
company also increasingly coordinates supply and cloud capacity, which puts
more commitments and partner risk around the platform.

F5, Microsoft, Amazon, and the security platforms control different software
workflows. Their recurring economics depend on usage, renewal, application
importance, and the cost of serving that workload. Contracted revenue or ARR
is only valuable if customers continue using the service at a price above
cloud, AI-processing, support, and stock-compensation costs.

### Semiconductor process and validation

ASML, KLA, Applied Materials, and Lam are qualified inside manufacturing
processes. The customer is not merely buying a machine; it is buying expected
yield, productivity, and production reliability. Qualification creates a
stronger relationship than a general equipment sale.

Teradyne and Keysight sit later in the chain. They provide proof that a device,
system, or network is ready for production or deployment. That proof can be
hard to remove, but test budgets can still be delayed and product mix can
change.

### Network, security, power, and site

Arista controls switching architecture and software in important networks.
Ciena controls a different optical transport and data-movement layer. Corning
and Amphenol supply physical content that can be qualified into the system but
usually have more manufacturing and acquisition burden.

CrowdStrike, Palo Alto, Zscaler, and F5 control security policy, telemetry,
access, or application delivery. Security has strong recurring need, but a
material incident can damage the trust that supports renewal.

Vertiv, nVent, Eaton, Hubbell, and Generac supply qualified interfaces for
power, cooling, backup, and protection. The control point can be attractive
when failure is expensive and replacement requires qualification. It is less
clean when the company must expand factories, carry inventory, or guarantee
project delivery.

Equinix and Digital Realty control powered space and interconnection density.
They capture a recurring property and network relationship but fund the most
visible physical burden in the chain.

Contractors control execution capacity when customers cannot obtain enough
qualified crews. Their relationship is valuable during a buildout, but the
customer can renegotiate, defer, or re-source projects after the capacity
shortage eases.

## Cash and earnings-quality tests

### Backlog, RPO, and orders are not cash

For every company, distinguish:

`order or commitment -> contract -> delivery/acceptance -> invoice ->
collection -> margin -> replacement and growth capital -> owner cash`

Backlog can be canceled, repriced, delayed, or costly to execute. RPO can be
fulfilled at a lower margin. Orders can be placed to reserve capacity before a
customer's own financing is secure.

The next filing should reconcile backlog or RPO changes with revenue,
deferred revenue, customer advances, receivables, inventory, gross margin, and
cash—not just cite the headline growth rate.

### Cash can be strong while required capital is rising

Operating cash flow must be reduced for the capital required to keep the
control point viable. Relevant burdens include:

- R&D and process qualification;
- factories, tooling, and test systems;
- inventory and customer acceptance delays;
- servers, data centers, power, and cooling;
- recurring property renewal and development capex;
- skilled labor, equipment, safety, and project working capital;
- acquisition cash and integration cost;
- stock compensation and the shares needed to settle it; and
- debt, preferred claims, guarantees, and partner or JV interests.

The right denominator varies. A semiconductor tool company may use operating
cash less capex and R&D-adjusted replacement spending. A REIT requires
normalized FFO less recurring property renewal and financing claims. A
contractor requires collected gross profit after working-capital investment.
A software platform requires cash after serving cost, capitalized software,
SBC, and infrastructure commitments.

### Accounting and financial-engineering questions

1. Does deferred revenue represent profitable future service or a costly
   obligation?
2. Are customer advances a sign of pricing power, or a timing benefit that
   reverses when acceptance and delivery occur?
3. Does receivable factoring or securitization change the apparent cash quality?
4. Are inventory increases supporting real demand or hiding obsolescence and
   acceptance risk?
5. Are acquisition integration, amortization, cyber response, warranty,
   restructuring, or quality costs recurring consequences of the model?
6. Does SBC reduce diluted-share cash even when adjusted earnings exclude it?
7. Are buybacks funded from normalized cash, or do debt, guarantees, or partner
   financing preserve a high per-share claim while the risk moves to owners?
8. Do guarantees and commitments place infrastructure or utilization risk on a
   platform that is valued as if it were asset-light?

## Macro and liquidity lens

AI demand can be structurally important and still cycle. Higher rates, weaker
cloud customers, inventory corrections, export controls, and power shortages
can delay the cash conversion of a large technical project.

The most important macro distinction is between:

- durable workload growth and temporary customer-capex acceleration;
- higher process intensity and higher total industry spending;
- scarce power today and profitable power after new supply arrives;
- qualified technology and technology that can be substituted at lower cost;
- customer commitments and customer liquidity; and
- reported cash and cash that remains after replacement capital.

Lyn Alden's durability question is whether the company can survive a funding
and utilization reset without issuing equity, taking uneconomic debt, or
cutting the investment needed to remain qualified. A durable AI trend does not
make every supplier liquid or every valuation reasonable.

## Valuation expectations

The market can capitalize a control point too early. A useful valuation screen
asks what normalized cash must be earned for the current price to make sense.

| Business type | Correct valuation input | Common mistake |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Architecture/software | Cash after R&D, supply commitments, serving cost, SBC, and ecosystem investment | Capitalizing peak growth while ignoring infrastructure and customer concentration |
| Semiconductor tools | Mid-cycle cash after R&D, service infrastructure, facilities, and cycle normalization | Treating backlog or service revenue as independent of fab spending |
| Validation and measurement | Cash after customer-capex normalization, R&D, acquisitions, and SBC | Treating a high test quarter as a permanent utilization rate |
| Network and security | Recurring cash after hosting/processing, support, incident costs, acquisitions, and dilution | Treating ARR, deferred revenue, or gross margin as owner cash |
| Power/cooling/electrical | Through-cycle cash after factories, warranty, tariffs, inventory, and qualification | Capitalizing a project backlog without charging capacity expansion |
| Data-center real estate | Normalized NOI/FFO after recurring capex, development, debt, leases, JVs, and dilution | Treating contracted capacity as cash before it is powered and leased |
| Contractors | Collected gross profit after labor, working capital, equipment, claims, and acquisitions | Treating backlog as earned margin |

Damodaran's test is whether the reinvestment required to sustain the narrative
earns an adequate return. If AI growth requires more capital per dollar of
revenue, the terminal value must reflect that lower conversion even if the
revenue forecast is correct.

company can occupy a necessary AI layer and still be priced above the cash its
filings currently support.

## Current synthesis

The archive supports the following qualified conclusions:

1. AI is creating a connected physical and software system, not one isolated
   product market.
2. The strongest control points generally combine qualification or switching
   cost with a high customer cost of failure.
3. Scarcity alone is not enough. The owner must fund R&D, capacity, inventory,
   power, cooling, property, labor, or customer commitments.
4. The cleanest economics may sit in process control, qualified interfaces,
   high-value network architecture, or recurring security workflow, but each
   has a different cycle, concentration, and replacement burden.
5. The companies carrying the physical build can show the largest backlogs and
   the weakest near-term cash after growth capital.
6. The correct investment conclusion is company-specific: identify the control
   point, subtract the full burden, normalize the cycle, and compare the
   resulting per-share cash with the price-implied expectation.

## Filing-based falsifiers

The AI physical-capacity thesis weakens if filings show:

- orders, RPO, or backlog rising while cancellations, acceptance delays,
  receivables, inventory, or project claims rise faster;
- service revenue falling with tool utilization despite installed-base claims;
- AI demand increasing process intensity but not enough to offset lower unit
  volumes or customer-capex cuts;
- export restrictions permanently reducing addressable demand without an
  economically equivalent replacement market;
- security incidents reducing renewal, increasing remediation cost, or
  weakening pricing power;
- power, cooling, networking, or construction capacity expanding faster than
  customer utilization and collected cash;
- data-center lease-up or project delivery requiring repeated equity, debt, or
  partner support;
- contractors reporting backlog growth while gross-margin estimates,
  receivables, claims, or working-capital needs deteriorate;
- SBC, acquisitions, guarantees, or capitalized costs rising faster than
  common-owner cash per diluted share; or
- market valuation requiring years of peak margins and growth that the filings
  have not yet demonstrated through a normalized cycle.
