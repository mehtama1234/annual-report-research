# Combined end-to-end forensic research system

Date: 2026-09-13  
Repository: `annual-report-research`

## The meaty end-to-end goal

Turn annual reports, 10-Qs, earnings materials, calls, and source ledgers into
a repeatable cross-sector investment-research system. For every priority
company, move through the same chain:

`economic control point -> durability and macro context -> narrative translated into numbers -> GAAP-to-cash reconciliation -> full reinvestment and financing burden -> incentive and presentation test -> valuation expectations -> filing-based thesis breaker`

The goal is not to find the company with the most attractive story. It is to
identify where a scarce relationship, workflow, standard, network, or physical
asset is controlled; determine who funds the system's hardest costs; and test
whether that control becomes durable owner cash at the price being paid.

The written output has two linked layers. The company memo records the filed
facts, calculations, unresolved questions, valuation assumptions, and next-filing
tests for one issuer. The cohort essay explains the underlying business model
across several issuers: what customers pay for, how revenue becomes cash, which
costs are easy to miss, and why the same accounting test differs between sectors.
The essay points back to worked company cases, and each company memo points to
the relevant essay. This prevents a reader from having to infer the business
mechanics from a table of ratios.

The governing lenses are complementary:

- **Lyn Alden:** durability, liquidity, monetary conditions, balance-sheet
  resilience, and whether a business can keep functioning through stress.
- **Aswath Damodaran:** narrative-to-numbers translation, lifecycle,
  reinvestment, incremental returns, capital structure, and valuation.
- **Forensic earnings quality:** reported versus adjusted profit, cash
  conversion, working-capital timing, capitalized costs, recurring “one-time”
  items, acquisition accounting, incentives, and falsifiers.

This is a repeatable research process, not a recommendation list or a finding of
fraud. A “shenanigan” flag means a reconciliation question that requires direct
filing evidence; it does not imply intent or misconduct.

## Writing standard for a reader without a finance background

Each company memo must be technically complete without assuming that the reader
already knows accounting or investing vocabulary. The memo should:

1. State what the company sells, who pays, what must happen before revenue is
   collected, and which party carries the main operating, financial, regulatory,
   or execution risk.
2. Define terms at first use. For example, operating cash flow means cash
   generated by the reported business before capital investment; owner cash is
   the remaining cash after the recurring investment needed to keep or expand
   that business. The exact definition can change by sector, and the memo must
   show the calculation.
3. Show the path from reported earnings to cash in plain language: reported
   profit, non-cash items, working-capital movements, capitalized costs,
   acquisitions, recurring replacement spending, financing, and dilution.
4. Separate facts from judgments. Filed numbers and management disclosures are
   evidence; conclusions about durability, incentives, accounting quality, or
   valuation are judgments that must identify the evidence supporting them.
5. Explain every concern as a testable question. A possible earnings-quality
   issue is not called fraud; it becomes important only when later filings fail
   a defined reconciliation or operational check.
6. End with the valuation denominator, the assumptions required to support it,
   and a measurable filing-based thesis breaker. The reader should be able to
   reproduce the conclusion from the cited filing tables and the scenario input.

The archive therefore contains three connected artifacts: the company memo, the
first-principles cohort essay that explains the economics across companies, and
the scenario register that makes the valuation assumptions comparable while
preserving sector-specific denominators. The [deep-dossier scenario
register](../valuation/deep-dossier-scenario-inputs-2026-09-13.csv) is a
screening tool, not a recommendation or a claim that all companies can be
valued with one multiple.

### Reader-facing cohort writeup

After the company-level evidence is assembled, write a first-principles essay
for its cohort. Begin with the transaction sequence in ordinary language: who
pays, what must happen before collection, which costs are paid before the
customer pays, and who carries the residual risk. Define each technical term at
first use. Then compare at least one cleaner-control company with one burdened
or contradiction company. Explain the accounting differences rather than
ranking unlike businesses on a single margin or cash-flow multiple.

The essay must include:

- a defined revenue-to-cash chain;
- control point versus burden carrier;
- reported profit versus owner cash, including working capital, capitalized
  costs, acquisitions, financing, and dilution;
- the Alden durability and liquidity test;
- the Damodaran narrative, lifecycle, reinvestment, and valuation test;
- earnings-quality questions stated as testable questions, not accusations;
- a comparison table that preserves sector-specific denominators; and
- filing-based falsifiers that can change the conclusion.

Use simple everyday language while retaining the actual accounting terms. Do
not use analogies, slogans, or unexplained finance jargon. A reader should be
able to follow the reasoning without already knowing what free cash flow,
working capital, stock compensation, backlog, RPO, rate base, or adjusted
earnings means.

## The common company work product

Every deep memo should answer the following in order.

### 1. Define the control point

State the customer dependency in one sentence. Name the specific mechanism:
availability, standard, workflow embedment, installed base, switching cost,
network density, brand/loyalty interface, regulatory permission, or scarce
physical capacity. Separate what the company controls from what it merely
benefits from.

### 2. Translate management's narrative

Convert each important narrative claim into an observable metric and a required
economic consequence.

| Narrative claim | Operating evidence | Economic proof still required |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Recurring” | Renewal, retention, repeat usage, installed-base pull-through, or contracted volume | Stable gross profit and cash after service, support, working capital, and maintenance capital |
| “Higher value mix” | Segment mix, price, volume, gross margin, and customer behavior | Incremental margin and return on invested capital after integration and selling cost |
| “Backlog / pipeline visibility” | Orders, RPO, cancellations, revenue recognition, and delivery timing | Collected cash after inventory, contract assets, labor, claims, and equipment |
| “Digital / AI adoption” | Transactions, penetration, utilization, capacity, and customer retention | Lower unit cost or higher willingness to pay after hardware, power, labor, SBC, and cybersecurity |
| “Asset-light” | Owned versus outsourced assets and reported capex | Health of the counterparty that funds the displaced labor, property, inventory, or maintenance |
| “Adjusted earnings growth” | Full adjustment schedule and year-over-year recurrence | GAAP profit, operating cash, capex, acquisitions, dilution, debt service, and owner cash |

### 3. Build the cash and capital bridge

Use a minimum three-year period plus the latest quarter where available:

`revenue -> gross profit -> GAAP operating income -> adjusted operating income -> net income -> operating cash flow -> maintenance capex -> growth capex -> acquisitions/investments -> owner cash`

Then separately show receivables, inventory, payables, contract assets/liabilities,
deferred revenue, customer deposits, and supplier financing. A working-capital
release is cash today, not necessarily repeatable earning power.

Classify cash as earned, timing-supported, growth-funded, or claim-bound. The
classification matters more than a single reported free-cash-flow number.

### 4. Test presentation and incentives

Locate the exact reconciliation. Check recurring restructuring, integration,
remediation, litigation, acquisition amortization, stock compensation and
dilution, capitalized software/development, inventory step-up, deferred tax,
leverage, guarantees, securitizations, pension/insurance obligations, REIT
structures, NCI, TRAs, and off-balance-sheet commitments. Also ask whether the
compensation scorecard rewards revenue, AUM, backlog, adjusted EPS, acquisitions,
or unit growth before cash returns and balance-sheet risk.

### 5. Price the expectations

State what the current price requires:

`market value -> normalized owner cash assumption -> implied cash yield -> growth required to justify the price`

Use bear/base/bull cases with explicit revenue growth, incremental margin,
reinvestment rate, tax/financing burden, share count, and terminal assumptions.
Keep sector-specific measures beside the common bridge rather than replacing it.

### 6. Write the falsifier

End every memo with a filing-based break condition that is measurable,
time-bound, and tied to the control-point claim. Examples include receivables
and inventory growing faster than gross profit, backlog converting without cash
or margin, acquisition cohorts failing to cover incremental capital, recurring
“one-time” charges, weak franchisee or customer health, or maintenance capex,
leases, debt, and legal cash consuming supposedly asset-light owner cash.

## Cross-sector comparison without flattening sectors

The common denominator is not EBITDA or free cash flow alone. It is the burden
stack required to keep the control point productive.

| Economic layer | Cleaner evidence | Hidden burden to test | Contradiction signal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Relationship/workflow owner | Retention, repeat usage, pricing, switching cost, and low incremental service cost | Counterparty health, service labor, compliance, technology, and support | Relationship metrics rise while cash per relationship falls |
| Distributor/channel | Gross profit, turns, fill rate, local density, and customer retention | Inventory, receivables, payables timing, branches, fleet, and acquisitions | Sales rise on price or mix while margin and cash conversion deteriorate |
| Project/physical executor | Backlog, RPO, utilization, realized margin, and repeat awards | Labor capacity, equipment, claims, contract assets, working capital, and financing | Backlog rises faster than collected cash and incremental returns |
| Platform/installed base | Active systems, usage, consumables, service, and renewal | Placement subsidies, training, maintenance, customer budgets, and R&D | Placements or users grow without pull-through or return on the next dollar |
| Financial intermediary | Flows, fee rate, spread, clearing activity, retention, and capital efficiency | Marks, credit, collateral, insurance liabilities, regulation, leverage, and compensation | Adjusted distributable earnings rise while GAAP/cash/credit quality weakens |
| Franchise/brand system | Royalty growth, unit economics, repeat visits, digital retention, and franchisee returns | Labor, food, rent, remodels, debt, closures, and brand support | Corporate fees grow while franchisee health and unit returns weaken |
| Property owner | Occupancy, pricing, NOI, and location scarcity | Sustaining capex, labor, insurance, taxes, interest, leases, and refinancing | AFFO/NOI looks stable because maintenance or asset sales are deferred |

## Definition of done

The combined goal is complete for a company when its memo has:

- a named economic control point and burden carrier;
- at least three years of GAAP, adjusted, cash, capital, and working-capital evidence;
- a narrative-to-numbers bridge with alternative explanations;
- a management-adjustment, dilution, leverage, and incentive review;
- a normalized owner-cash and price-implied-expectations scenario set;
- evidence grades distinguishing packet fact, filing fact, inference, and unresolved question;
- a next-filing watchlist with at least one measurable thesis breaker;
- links back to the source ledger and relevant cross-sector comparison.

At the archive level, the goal is complete when every priority lane has both a
clean-control example and a contradiction example, the comparable valuation
table is populated, and the watchlist is maintained from new filings rather
than narrative momentum.

## Current limits

This archive has reached a meaningful cross-sector synthesis, but it is not
financial signoff. Most companies remain packet-backed rather than full deep
exemplars, comparable bridges are incomplete for many names, and the current
flags identify questions rather than prove wrongdoing. The next work should
deepen source reconciliation and valuation rather than broaden the roster.

The AI cohort now includes [AI physical capacity and control layers](ai-physical-capacity-forensic-comparison-2026-09-13.md),
which places Amazon's cloud demand and capital bill beside process control,
application control, power and cooling, powered real estate, regulated
generation, and field execution. It establishes a specific cross-company test:
strong AI demand is not enough; the owner-cash conclusion requires incremental
returns after the physical system and financing are charged.
Alphabet adds the search-and-cloud incumbent case to that same test through its
[deep dossier](../deep-company-pages/alphabet-inc.md): mature Search and YouTube
cash fund Cloud, AI models, and technical infrastructure, but the return on the
next infrastructure dollar remains unproven until revenue, depreciation, cash,
leases, and dilution are tracked together.
