# Who keeps the cash? What different industries teach us about business quality

Date baseline: 2026-09-13  
Purpose: consolidate the company and sector investigations into a
single economic map.

## Three different reasons a strong headline can mislead

The research returns to one practical question: after a customer pays, what must the company spend or retain before shareholders benefit? The answer differs by business. A restaurant pays for food and labor. A medical-device manufacturer funds instruments, equipment and product development. An exchange holds resources to support trading obligations that must remain separate from ordinary corporate cash. Comparing these businesses requires understanding those differences before comparing a profit ratio.

### Customer activity and profit need not improve together

[Chipotle's study](../deep-company-pages/chipotle-mexican-grill.md) records that customer transactions fell 2.9% in FY2025 while spending per order rose 1.2%. Comparable sales fell 1.7%. The distinction matters because higher spending per order can partly offset fewer transactions without demonstrating stronger customer demand.

The same study records an improvement in transactions in Q2 2026 alongside a decline in restaurant-level margin. That margin measures the share of store sales left after specified restaurant costs, before corporate overhead and other claims. Improving visits and weaker margins can both be true. To judge whether the business is improving, we need to know whether additional customer activity leaves enough profit after the costs of serving it.

This question extends beyond restaurants. A distributor can ship more product without keeping more profit per shipment. A contractor can sign more work without earning the expected margin. Activity is evidence of demand; it is not a substitute for measuring the result after costs.

### Repeat purchases are valuable, but maintaining the product still costs money

[Intuitive Surgical's study](../deep-company-pages/intuitive-surgical-inc.md) records that instruments and accessories supplied approximately 60% of FY2025 revenue, compared with approximately 25% from systems. The equipment sale is therefore only part of the business. Continued clinical use supports further instrument purchases and service revenue.

That helps explain why adoption and utilization matter. A system placed in a hospital creates an opportunity; repeated use provides stronger evidence that the opportunity is being realized. But the manufacturer still funds engineering, production, inventory, training and support. Repeat sales do not remove those obligations. The investment question is whether the resulting cash, after necessary spending and share dilution, is sufficient for the price paid for the shares.

The same distinction appears in cooling equipment, industrial machinery and software. A continuing customer relationship can reduce the need to win the sale again from the beginning. It does not establish the cost of retaining that relationship or the return on future investment.

### Money held by a company is not always money it can distribute

[CME's study](../deep-company-pages/cme-group-inc.md) records $159.7 billion of performance bonds and guaranty fund contributions on its 2025 balance sheet, with a corresponding liability. These resources support the obligations of clearing participants. They cannot be added to a valuation as though they were unrestricted cash earned by shareholders.

The useful analysis separates transaction and data fees from collateral-related income and obligations. That principle extends to banks and insurers, although their accounting and risks differ: deposits create obligations to customers, and insurers hold assets to support policyholder claims. Large balances can be necessary to operate the business without being available for dividends.

## Compare mechanisms, not isolated rankings

These cases do not establish that one sector is always superior. They demonstrate different checks on the same reasoning. Separate customer activity from profit, profit from collected cash, and collected cash from amounts available after investment and obligations. Then state the assumptions used to value the remaining cash.

The figures above are historical observations from the linked studies, not live comparisons or predictions. The broader tables below are research questions and provisional classifications. A company can move between stronger and weaker outcomes as pricing, costs, financing, management decisions and the price of its shares change.

## The archive-level question

The most important distinction across the research is not “good company versus
bad company.” It is:

`who controls the valuable relationship -> who captures the gross profit -> who funds the hard operating burden -> who ultimately keeps the cash`

A company can own a powerful interface and still be a poor equity investment if
the labor, inventory, capital, regulatory, or financing burden absorbs the
surplus. A company can also appear asset-light because it has transferred the
burden to franchisees, suppliers, customers, project partners, or debt markets.
That transfer may be economically intelligent, but it must be tested for system
health and recurrence.

### New worked cases: three different burden locations

The recent PepsiCo, American Express, and Eaton dossiers make the same
principle visible in three different accounting systems.

| Company | What it controls | Where the burden appears | Current contradiction test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| PepsiCo | Branded food and beverage choice, product portfolio, and distribution reach | Ingredients, packaging, plants, labor, retailer support, inventory, acquisitions, and debt | 2025 operating cash fell to `$12.087B`, while free cash flow reached `$8.2B` after lower capital spending and `$528M` of property-sale proceeds; volume and acquisition returns must confirm durability |
| American Express | Card-member and merchant relationships, fee products, spending data, and issuing relationship | Rewards, receivables, funding, credit losses, liquidity, regulatory capital, marketing, and technology | 2025 billed business reached about `$1.67T` and net income `$10.8B`, but customer growth must be tested after rewards, lifetime credit losses, and required capital |
| Eaton | Electrical distribution and protection interfaces serving data centers, grids, industry, and aerospace | Factories, inventory, unbilled receivables, PP&E, acquisitions, debt, and separation costs | 2025 post-capex/acquisition residual was about `$2.063B` while dividends and repurchases were about `$3.49B`; backlog must convert with margin and collection rather than only support the narrative |

These cases should not be compared by headline margin. PepsiCo's question is
whether brand scale protects physical volume and recurring cash. American
Express must retain capital against a growing financial asset base and future
claims. Eaton must fund capacity and acquisitions before backlog becomes cash.
The control point is valuable in each case, but the timing and location of the
burden determine the common shareholder outcome.

This table is a starting map, not a ranking of stocks. The cleaner candidates
still need valuation discipline; the contradiction cases can become attractive
if the burden normalizes and the price does not already assume recovery.

### Cloudflare versus F5: adjacent control points, different burdens

Cloudflare and F5 both sit between an application and the traffic reaching it,
but the economic evidence is not interchangeable. F5's control point is more
closely tied to application delivery and security products installed in
enterprise environments. Cloudflare operates a distributed network that also
handles traffic, access, security, developer execution, and newer payment and
machine-traffic functions. Cloudflare therefore has a broader platform claim,
but it must keep funding global network capacity, bandwidth, servers, technical
labor, and security operations while its GAAP operating result remains negative.

The comparison should use different tests. For F5, examine product renewal,
services mix, hardware replacement, and recurring software attachment. For
Cloudflare, examine RPO and contract-liability conversion, gross margin after
network cost, stock compensation relative to operating cash, restructuring,
diluted shares, and cash after infrastructure investment. Both have a trust
requirement; Cloudflare carries more direct distributed-network burden, while
F5's narrower application-control position may produce a cleaner cash profile
if renewal and support economics persist.

The conclusion is conditional. Cloudflare's wider control surface is valuable
only if platform attachment improves cash per diluted share rather than merely
increasing traffic, usage, or bundled features. The next filings should be read
side by side for retention, module adoption, gross margin, GAAP operating
leverage, stock compensation, and required network investment.

### JPMorgan versus American Express: payment control with different balance-sheet claims

JPMorgan and American Express both benefit from payment relationships, customer
data, and recurring transaction activity, but their owner denominators differ.
JPMorgan's 2025 platform included about `$2.559T` of deposits, `$4.425T` of
assets, `$182.447B` of total net revenue, and `$57.048B` of net income. Its
earnings are spread across net interest income, payments, cards, markets,
investment banking, and asset management. American Express reported about
`$1.67T` of billed business, `$72.2B` of revenue, and `$10.8B` of net income,
with a stronger concentration in its card-member, merchant, rewards, and
receivables system.

The cleaner economics cannot be decided from net margin alone. JPMorgan has a
broader funding base and more diversified revenue, but it carries large
technology, compensation, compliance, market, credit, and regulatory-capital
requirements. American Express has a more focused premium relationship and can
capture merchant fees and annual fees, but rewards, receivables, funding,
credit-loss, and capital needs sit closer to the same customer proposition.

The filing tests should remain separate:

| Test | JPMorgan | American Express |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Funding | Deposit mix, deposit pricing, wholesale funding, liquidity, and withdrawals | Deposits, debt, securitization, receivable growth, and funding cost |
| Credit | Consumer, commercial, card, and wholesale portfolio reserves and charge-offs | Card and loan vintages, reserve coverage, rewards-linked customer behavior, and write-offs |
| Revenue quality | Through-cycle net interest income plus normalized markets, payments, fees, and investment banking | Billed-business conversion after rewards, annual-fee benefits, merchant economics, and credit losses |
| Capital return | CET1 and stress requirements after balance-sheet and market-risk growth | Required capital after receivable growth and customer acquisition |
| Incentive risk | Revenue, EPS, ROTCE, growth, and risk/conduct balance | New accounts, billed business, fees, retention, credit-adjusted return, and diluted shares |

The shared conclusion is that payment volume is a leading indicator, not owner
cash. The company that controls the transaction still has to fund the balance
sheet, preserve trust, absorb losses, and retain capital before returning cash
to common shareholders.

### Synchrony versus American Express: two card relationships, different customers and risk tests

Synchrony and American Express both connect merchants, consumers, and credit,
but they do not represent the same economic model. Synchrony is concentrated
in private-label, co-brand, promotional, installment, and buy-now-pay-later
financing distributed through merchant and provider partners. American Express
has a more integrated premium card-member, merchant, fee, rewards, and lending
relationship. Synchrony reported `$182.3B` of 2025 purchase volume, `$103.8B`
of year-end loan receivables, `$3.552B` of net earnings, and a `5.65%` net
charge-off rate. American Express reported about `$1.67T` of 2025 billed
business and `$10.8B` of net income. These volumes cannot be compared directly:
Synchrony is showing a merchant-linked lending book, while American Express is
showing a broader premium spending and fee system with its own receivables and
rewards burden.

| Test | Synchrony | American Express |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Distribution control | Merchant and provider partners can renew, renegotiate, or replace the issuer | Card-member and merchant relationships are more integrated but require ongoing rewards and service investment |
| Main asset and liability burden | Loan receivables, deposits and other funding, allowance, charge-offs, promotions, and capital | Card receivables, deposits and debt, rewards, credit losses, marketing, and capital |
| Growth quality | Purchase volume and new accounts must produce acceptable lifetime return after partner economics and product-level losses | Billed business and customer growth must remain profitable after rewards, acquisition cost, credit losses, and funding |
| Current contradiction | Q2 2026 purchase volume rose `8%` while ROA fell to `2.9%`; higher activity did not by itself improve return | Large billed-business growth must be reconciled to rewards cost, receivable quality, and capital per customer |
| Falsifier | Volume grows while payment rates weaken, reserves fall ahead of loss emergence, or partner concentration rises | Billed business grows while rewards, losses, dilution, or required capital consume the incremental economics |

The useful conclusion is not that one model is automatically superior. Synchrony
may earn a high spread because it carries more direct household credit risk and
depends on partner access. American Express may retain more of the total
customer relationship, but it also funds rewards, service, receivables, and
credit capacity. Both must be valued on risk-adjusted common earnings after the
full burden, not on purchase or billed-business volume.

### MetLife versus American Express: financial relationships with different residual claims

MetLife and American Express both earn from trusted customer relationships and
financial assets, but the timing of their claims is different. American Express
funds receivables and absorbs credit losses as card members spend and repay.
MetLife receives premiums and fees, invests assets, and must preserve those
assets against future insurance and retirement benefits. In both cases, assets
shown on the balance sheet are not automatically distributable to common
shareholders.

MetLife's FY2025 adjusted earnings were `$5.986B` against `$3.173B` of net
income, with about `$22.559B` of net investment income and nearly `$4.4B` of
shareholder returns. American Express reported `$10.8B` of net income and about
`$1.67T` of billed business, but carries rewards and credit-cycle claims. The
figures cannot be ranked by a conventional free-cash-flow margin. MetLife must
be tested through reserves, statutory capital, asset-liability duration,
reinsurance, and private-asset realizations. American Express must be tested
through receivable funding, rewards, write-offs, deposit or debt funding, and
capital ratios.

The shared forensic question is whether adjusted earnings remove normal costs
of the business. For MetLife, recurring market remeasurement, reserve changes,
and variable investment income cannot be treated as irrelevant if they affect
capital release. For American Express, rewards, credit losses, and funding
costs cannot be separated from the payment relationship that generates fees.

### ServiceNow versus Cloudflare: workflow control versus edge control

ServiceNow and Cloudflare both sell a layer that customers may treat as part of
their operating control, but the customer dependency is different. ServiceNow
records and routes organizational work: incidents, approvals, security events,
and employee or customer processes. Cloudflare controls traffic handling,
access, application protection, and edge execution between applications and the
people or machines reaching them.

ServiceNow's FY2025 operating cash flow was `$5.444B` against `$868M` of
property purchases, while stock compensation was `$1.955B`. Cloudflare's
FY2025 operating cash flow was `$603M` against `$261M` of company-defined free
cash flow, while stock compensation was `$451M`. ServiceNow has the lighter
physical burden and stronger current GAAP earnings profile, but it still has to
fund cloud services, support, research, deferred commissions, acquisitions, and
dilution. Cloudflare has a broader distributed-network burden and remained in a
GAAP operating loss, so its platform thesis requires a larger future margin
improvement.

The evidence should therefore be read through different denominators. For
ServiceNow, test renewal, module expansion, cRPO conversion, AI ACV
incrementality, subscription gross margin, deferred commissions, acquisitions,
stock compensation, and cash per diluted share. For Cloudflare, add traffic
quality, network cost, infrastructure investment, restructuring, and GAAP
operating leverage. Both businesses control important software interfaces; the
one with the cleaner economics is the one that converts that control into cash
after the costs required to keep the interface trusted and useful.

Snowflake adds a third software denominator. Its FY2026 product revenue was
`$4.472B`, operating cash flow was `$1.222B`, free cash flow was `$1.120B`, and
stock compensation was `$1.600B`. Unlike ServiceNow's mainly subscription
contract model, Snowflake's consumption model can expand quickly from existing
customers but can also contract when customers optimize queries, reduce idle
usage, or move workloads. Deferred revenue from prepaid capacity supplied
`$755M` of cash, while third-party cloud, GPU, inference, support, and security
costs remain part of product delivery.

The cohort conclusion is therefore more precise: ServiceNow tests whether
workflow centrality supports renewal and module expansion; Cloudflare tests
whether distributed edge control earns operating leverage after network cost;
Snowflake tests whether governed data consumption remains profitable after
public-cloud cost and dilution. All three can report strong contract or usage
signals while the common-owner denominator remains different.

### Software valuation expectations

The registered scenarios show why recurring revenue is not one valuation
denominator. Adobe uses a mature subscription cash screen; ServiceNow uses a
workflow-platform cash screen; Snowflake uses consumption cash after public-
cloud and stock-compensation claims; Cloudflare requires a much larger future
margin improvement after network and dilution costs.

| Company | Current equity-value screen | Bear / base / bull implied value | What the base case requires |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Adobe | `$101.5B` | `$91.0B / $144.0B / $231.0B` | ARR renewal, seat and price growth, AI monetization, R&D, SBC, buybacks, Figma effects, and competitive position support durable cash |
| ServiceNow | `$137.1B` | `$100.0B / $165.0B / $262.5B` | Subscription renewal, cRPO conversion, AI ACV, cloud and support costs, deferred commissions, acquisitions, SBC, and dilution remain favorable |
| Snowflake | `$114.9B` | `$36.0B / $99.0B / $195.0B` | Consumption growth and net retention offset public-cloud, GPU, inference, prepaid-capacity, deferred-commission, SBC, and dilution claims |
| Cloudflare | `$108.6B` | `$40.0B / $135.0B / $300.0B` | Edge and security attachment produces GAAP operating leverage after bandwidth, network investment, restructuring, leases, SBC, and dilution |

The market is asking Adobe and ServiceNow to prove durable renewal and
incremental product value, Snowflake to prove that consumption growth is not
temporary prepaid or optimization-driven usage, and Cloudflare to prove that
network control can become profitable at much greater scale. High recurring
revenue does not remove the need to charge delivery cost, employee dilution,
and reinvestment.

## Five recurring economic patterns

### 1. Relationship ownership is more valuable when it reduces the next decision

Fastenal's replenishment footprint, McDonald's loyalty and store system, CME's
clearing network, Intuitive Surgical's installed procedure workflow, F5's
application/security position, and Equinix's interconnection density all reduce
customer coordination cost. This is the common control-point mechanism.

The test is whether the relationship lowers customer friction without requiring
the company to add equal labor, capital, or support cost for every incremental
customer. A rising user, AUM, restaurant, system, or interconnection count is
not enough if contribution per relationship falls.

### 2. Asset-light economics can hide a burden-transfer dependency

McDonald's does not run every restaurant, but its parent cash depends on
franchisees being able to pay rent and royalties while funding labor, food,
remodels, technology, and debt. Apollo can earn fees while insurance and spread
businesses carry balance-sheet risk. BlackRock can report fee economics while
private-market marks and consolidated products complicate the denominator.

The right question is not whether the company owns the asset. It is whether the
counterparty can keep funding it without weakening the network or demanding
support that eventually returns to the parent.

### 3. Backlog is strongest when it is closest to collection and weakest when it is closest to a financing promise

F5's RPO and deferred revenue are closer to contracted service conversion than
WESCO's project inventory or Quanta's estimate-based RPO. Digital Realty's
lease backlog is real demand but has a construction and commencement lag.
Generac's hyperscale backlog is promising but still requires qualification,
inventory, packaging, warranty, and factory capacity.

The archive's [backlog-to-cash synthesis](backlog-visibility-to-cash-realization-forensic-synthesis-2026-09-13.md)
formalizes this denominator problem. The filing test is always revenue,
margin, collection, required capital, and final owner cash.

Oracle makes the financing distinction unusually clear. Its FY2026 RPO reached
$638B while free cash flow was negative $23.7B. Customer prepayments and
customer-supplied GPUs reduced the company’s immediate funding requirement, but
Oracle still had to provide facilities, power, networking, operations, and
contracted service. Microsoft’s FY2026 commercial RPO reached $678B while
property-and-equipment additions reached $115.9B. The shared conclusion is that
RPO is evidence of demand visibility, not evidence that the contract will earn
an adequate return after the capacity required to fulfill it.

ServiceNow supplies the lighter comparison. Its FY2025 operating cash flow was
$5.444B against $868M of property purchases, while stock compensation was
$1.955B. Its Q2 2026 AI ACV exceeded $1B, but the same conversion test applies:
AI contract value must become durable revenue and cash after cloud usage,
support, deferred commissions, acquisitions, and dilution. This is a cleaner
capital model than Oracle’s, not a burden-free model.

Snowflake adds the consumption-based data layer. FY2026 free cash flow was
$1.120B, but stock compensation was $1.600B and deferred revenue increased
$755M from prepaid capacity agreements. Product cost includes third-party cloud
infrastructure, GPUs, AI inference, support, availability, and security. The
data-control position may be valuable without Snowflake owning the data center,
but the cloud provider still claims part of the economics and customers can
optimize usage.

### 4. Adjusted earnings become dangerous when the adjustment is the operating model

Across the dossiers, recurring categories include amortization from acquisitions,
stock compensation, restructuring, legal and quality costs, digital-transformation
spending, FFO depreciation exclusions, insurance marks, and working-capital
benefits. None automatically proves manipulation.

The forensic question is whether management's preferred measure removes a cost
that the business must continue to incur to preserve the relationship. If the
answer is yes, the measure may still describe operating performance, but it is
not owner cash and should not receive an unqualified valuation multiple.

### 5. The cleanest demand story can still have the wrong lifecycle

KLA's process-control demand, Vertiv's AI infrastructure backlog, Quanta's
grid RPO, CAVA's openings, Digital Realty's powered capacity, and Generac's
hyperscale demand all benefit from structural narratives. Damodaran's lifecycle
test asks whether the company is in a reinvestment phase, a mature cash phase,
or a temporarily elevated cycle.

The same demand can support very different valuation outcomes:

- mature control point with low reinvestment and high cash conversion;
- high-return reinvestment phase where growth creates value;
- capital-heavy expansion where growth consumes cash before returns appear; or
- peak-cycle demand where backlog and margins are being pulled forward.

The cloud-control cohort adds another practical state: a mature software
franchise financing a new physical-capacity cycle. Microsoft and Oracle can
have durable enterprise relationships and still deliver weaker common-owner
cash while servers, data centers, power, depreciation, leases, debt, and
customer-funded equipment arrangements scale faster than operating profit.

Broadcom adds the intermediate case. It owns silicon and networking designs
that can be deeply embedded in AI systems while also owning infrastructure
software with recurring contracts. Its semiconductor layer carries customer,
manufacturing, and cycle risk; its software layer carries renewal, pricing,
acquisition, and debt risk. Consolidated margin and cash therefore cannot be
used to infer that both layers have the same durability.

Intel is the heavier-burden contrast. Its process technology, CPU franchise,
and prospective external foundry can be strategically important, but the
company must fund fabs, equipment, substrates, labor, R&D, inventory, and
process transitions before the customer cash is proven. Intel’s FY2025 adjusted
free cash flow was negative after net capital expenditure, and Q2 FY2026
adjusted free cash flow remained negative after capex, incentives, partner
contributions, and finance leases. A technical milestone therefore remains a
hypothesis until qualified volume, utilization, margin, and owner cash appear.

Dell carries a different burden: it integrates and delivers the complete AI
system, but its AI growth requires components, inventory, receivables, customer
financing, supplier commitments, and support. Its adjusted free cash flow
explicitly removes the operating cash impact of financing receivables and
equipment under operating leases. The comparison with Broadcom and Intel is
therefore useful: Broadcom can own a high-value design layer, Intel owns the
capital-heavy manufacturing layer, and Dell owns the customer-facing delivery
and financing layer. The cleanest economics depend on which layer retains
pricing power without absorbing the next dollar of capital burden.

### Architecture control versus complete-system delivery

Broadcom, Cisco, and Dell all benefit from the same AI infrastructure spending,
but they sit at different points in the chain. Broadcom supplies custom
accelerators, networking silicon, and infrastructure software. Cisco combines
installed network architecture, security, observability, and support with a
material hardware and supply-chain business. Dell integrates complete systems
and adds delivery, services, and customer financing. The demand headline is
shared; the cash burden is not.

Q3 FY2026 AI semiconductor revenue was `$16.7B`, while semiconductor solutions were 70% of quarterly revenue and infrastructure software 30%. | Determine whether custom-AI design wins remain valuable after customer concentration, product transitions, foundry and packaging commitments, VMware retention, acquisition debt, SBC, and dilution. Low PP&E makes reported free cash flow look unusually clean; acquisition and integration capital must stay visible. |
| Cisco | FY2025 revenue was `$56.654B`, subscription revenue about `$31.5B`, operating cash flow `$14.193B`, and company-defined free cash flow `$13.288B`; SBC was `$3.641B` and total debt `$28.093B`. Cisco reported `$5.3B` of hyperscaler AI infrastructure orders through Q3 FY2026 and raised its expectation to `$9B`. | Test whether orders convert into revenue, margin, collections, software and support renewal after Splunk integration, hardware replacement cycles, inventory, receivables, debt, and SBC. Subscription revenue is not one economic category until hardware, software, support, and contract obligations are separated. |
| Dell | FY2026 revenue was `$113.5B`, operating cash flow `$11.185B`, reported free cash flow `$8.555B`, and adjusted free cash flow `$11.508B`. FY2027 Q2 AI orders were `$60.9B`, AI backlog `$95B`, and `$6.667B` of the quarterly adjusted-cash adjustment came from financing receivables. | Test whether Dell is earning an integration and service return or financing a low-margin hardware cycle. Backlog must be reconciled to margin, inventory, collections, financing receivables, debt, warranty, support, and customer concentration. Adjusted cash is not free of credit and funding risk. |

The comparison produces a useful burden order. Broadcom is closest to the
architecture-control case: it can capture high value from a narrow design layer
without owning the full data-center build, but its acquisition and customer
concentration claims are large. Cisco sits between architecture and delivery:
its installed network and security relationships can support recurring revenue,
while AI orders still pass through hardware, supply-chain, and acquisition
tests. Dell owns the customer-facing integration point, which can be valuable
when customers need a complete system, but it funds more inventory,
receivables, financing, warranty, and support.

The falsifier is not a slowdown in AI demand by itself. It is demand growth that
requires increasingly weak terms: Broadcom revenue rising with concentration,
commitments, or dilution; Cisco orders rising without acceptable margin,
collection, or Splunk retention; or Dell backlog and adjusted cash rising only
because customer financing and working-capital exposure expand. The next
filings should keep the three denominators separate: design-layer cash,
installed-network recurring cash, and system-integration cash after financing.

Fortis adds a different form of recurring control. The valuable relationship is
the regulated service territory and the right to recover prudent infrastructure
investment. The burden is funded before recovery: construction, maintenance,
interest, equity issuance, reliability, environmental compliance, weather, and
customer affordability. Rate-base growth is therefore a leading indicator, not
an owner-return result, until projects enter service and produce per-share
earnings and cash without weakening credit quality.

Edison is the stress case inside the same regulated category. Its service
territory and rate base provide a recurring control point, but wildfire claims,
insurance, the Wildfire Fund, regulatory assets, and affordability can make the
cash outcome discontinuous. A utility can report strong core EPS and still have
weaker owner economics if catastrophe costs, financing costs, or unrecovered
resilience spending consume the return. The comparison with Fortis makes the
burden visible: geographic diversification reduces single-jurisdiction risk,
while Edison’s California concentration increases the importance of one
regulatory and catastrophe system.

MetLife adds the balance-sheet-heavy financial version of the same question.
The valuable relationship is the policyholder promise and the ability to invest
premiums over long periods. The burden is the reserve and capital requirement:
assets supporting future benefits are not all distributable to common owners.
Adjusted earnings must therefore be separated into underwriting, contractual
spread, variable investment income, market marks, reserve changes, and capital
release. The cleaner economics are the earnings that remain after liabilities,
reinsurance, statutory capital, and liquidity needs are funded.

Exelon and AEP show why utility growth claims require a recovery bridge. Exelon
has the cleaner established-wires case when its recovery coverage and in-service
assets remain reliable. AEP has more large-load and capital-plan upside, but
signed demand is not energized demand, and a capital plan is not current common
cash. The decisive evidence is whether projects enter service at acceptable
cost, receive recovery, obtain customer credit support, and produce earnings
after interest, equity issuance, and affordability constraints.

## A common evidence ladder

Every company conclusion should be assigned to one of these levels:

| Level | What it proves | What it does not prove |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Narrative | Management's claimed control point or growth mechanism | That customers pay, margins persist, or capital returns are attractive |
| Leading indicator | Traffic, backlog, RPO, AUM, loyalty, installed base, bookings, or utilization | That the indicator converts into cash without burden |
| Accounting result | Revenue, GAAP profit, adjusted profit, FFO, or EPS | That earnings are durable or cash is distributable |
| Cash result | Operating cash after working-capital effects and capex | That growth capital, acquisitions, debt, dilution, or support can be ignored |
| Owner result | Cash after sustaining and growth capital, claims, financing, and dilution | That the current valuation leaves an adequate return |
| Thesis breaker | A measurable future filing outcome that would weaken the claim | That the claim is permanently true |

The archive should promote a company to a strong conclusion only when the
evidence moves down this ladder. A narrative plus leading indicator is a
research hypothesis; it is not a financial conclusion.

## Lyn Alden, Damodaran, and forensic integration

### Alden: durability through liquidity and stress

Ask whether the relationship survives a period of tighter liquidity, weaker
customer budgets, higher rates, commodity/input inflation, and constrained
financing. Balance-sheet resilience is not a side section. It determines who
can continue funding inventory, labor, power, leases, claims, and expansion.

### Damodaran: narrative translated into lifecycle-adjusted returns

Translate every strategic claim into revenue, margin, reinvestment, financing,
and terminal economics. Estimate the incremental return on the next dollar of
factory capacity, store openings, acquisition, powered capacity, technology,
or working capital. Then compare that return with the cost of capital and the
price-implied expectation.

### Forensic framework: presentation, incentives, and reconciliation

Reconcile GAAP earnings to cash; separate recurring from exceptional items;
trace working-capital timing; identify capitalized costs; test acquisition
returns; count SBC and dilution; examine debt, leases, NCI, preferred claims,
JVs, and insurance/property obligations; and ask whether management is paid on
the same denominator used to present performance.

## Valuation discipline across unlike denominators

The archive does not use one universal free-cash-flow multiple. The relevant
denominator changes with the claims that must be funded before common owners can
receive cash.

| Company | Current screening denominator | Why the denominator is specific | Main price-implied expectation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| PepsiCo | Reported free cash flow, adjusted for property-sale proceeds and normal capital spending | Physical staples operations, retailer support, acquisitions, and dividends must be funded | Volume stabilizes while cash recovers without permanent price or debt support |
| American Express | Normalized common earnings after rewards, credit losses, funding, and capital | Receivables and rewards obligations make industrial FCF misleading | Premium membership and spending growth remain profitable through the credit cycle |
| Eaton | Operating cash less PP&E and acquisitions, before financing and normalization | Backlog and capacity growth require cash before the order becomes owner return | Several years of high growth and successful acquisition/capacity returns are already assumed |
| ServiceNow | Operating cash less property purchases, then adjusted for dilution, acquisitions, and recurring product investment | Subscription cash is strong but stock compensation and deferred commissions are material | AI ACV and workflow expansion become durable cash per diluted share |
| Cloudflare | Free cash flow after PP&E and capitalized software, with a large dilution and infrastructure adjustment | Network operations and stock compensation are central to the platform cost | High traffic and RPO growth turn into GAAP leverage and materially higher owner cash |
| Snowflake | Free cash flow after public-cloud cost, prepaid-capacity normalization, capitalized software, and dilution | Consumption revenue can expand or contract, and cloud providers claim part of gross profit | Data and AI usage expands while gross margin and stock compensation improve |
| NVIDIA | Operating cash less PP&E, intangible investment, and acquisitions, with supply, customer, and dilution claims kept visible | Architecture cash is unusually strong, but receivables, inventory, cloud-service commitments, customer concentration, and ecosystem investment sit around it | Data-center growth remains exceptional while commitments, working capital, R&D, SBC, and diluted shares stay controlled |
| State Street | Institutional-servicing earnings after technology, cyber, liquidity, and bank-capital requirements | AUC/A and AUM measure client scale, not company assets or unrestricted cash | Custody and servicing fees remain durable after fee pressure, market-versus-flow effects, and control spending |
| Exelon | Risk-adjusted common earnings tied to rate-base growth and recovery | Utility construction cash is funded before approved assets earn a return; ordinary post-capex FCF is the wrong screen | Approved wires investment enters service, recovery remains high, and financing and dilution do not absorb the return |
| Brookdale | Operator cash after occupancy, care labor, leases, maintenance, acquisitions, debt, and resident affordability | Senior-housing demand can improve occupancy without producing distributable cash | Occupancy and rates normalize the current weak cash base while labor, leases, and maintenance remain manageable |
| CAVA | Operating cash less PP&E, with store openings, leases, labor, food, technology, SBC, and growth capital charged | Restaurant-level profit is not parent cash while the company is opening units | Same-store demand and mature-store returns fund growth without a persistent capital or dilution gap |

These figures should not be ranked mechanically. A bank, insurer, card issuer,
consumer company, and software company do not have interchangeable owner-cash
definitions. The valuation question is whether the current price requires a
future operating outcome that is stronger than the filing evidence supports.
Every scenario should therefore state: the numerator, the claims excluded, the
maintenance and growth investment included, the share-count assumption, and
the next filing that could invalidate the case.

## Cross-sector watchlist

| Filing signal | What it would confirm | What it would weaken |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Traffic/utilization/retention improves with stable margin | Relationship is deepening economically | Growth is price, mix, or discount-driven |
| Backlog converts with stable gross profit and cash collection | Visibility is real operating control | Backlog is timing, customer financing, or project optimism |
| Working-capital intensity normalizes | Growth is becoming self-funded | Company is financing customers or suppliers are financing company |
| Sustaining capex stabilizes while incremental returns rise | Lifecycle is becoming more cash generative | “Asset-light” or growth claim understates maintenance burden |
| Adjusted-to-GAAP gap narrows without lost growth | Earnings quality is improving | Preferred metric depends on recurring adjustments |
| Debt declines without underinvestment or dilution | Balance-sheet resilience is strengthening | Cash returns are debt-funded or future capital needs are deferred |
| Counterparty/franchisee/customer health remains strong | Burden transfer is durable | Parent economics depend on a weakening network |

T. Rowe Price adds the liquid-public-markets version of the asset-management
question. FY2025 ending AUM rose to $1.776 trillion, but net client outflows
were $56.9 billion; market appreciation was the larger driver of the increase.
In Q2 FY2026, AUM reached $1.893 trillion while net outflows were still $6.5
billion and the effective fee rate fell to 38.1 basis points. The required
bridge is starting AUM plus market movement plus net flows, followed by average
AUM times fee rate less compensation, distribution, technology, and other
operating costs. Record AUM by itself does not establish client retention or
durable owner economics.

The optical and network-control cohort makes the same principle visible across
different layers. Corning and Amphenol control qualified physical content but
must fund plants, inventory, and acquisitions. Ciena controls an active system
and has substantial backlog, but backlog still requires production, acceptance,
collection, and working capital. Arista controls a higher-level fabric and
operating workflow with much lower property investment, but deferred revenue,
hyperscaler concentration, stock compensation, and architecture changes remain
real risks. The cleanest economics are not determined by being physical or
software alone; they depend on what the company controls and what it must fund
to deliver the promise.

### Caterpillar versus Eaton: two physical bottlenecks, different owner burdens

Caterpillar and Eaton both benefit from infrastructure and power demand, but
they sit at different points in the physical system. Caterpillar sells machines,
power equipment, parts, service, and financing into an installed fleet. Eaton
sells electrical distribution, protection, controls, and related systems into
buildings, grids, data centers, and aerospace. Both can benefit from replacement
and qualification, but Caterpillar carries dealer inventory, Cat Financial
receivables, warranty, and equipment-cycle risk, while Eaton carries more
unbilled receivables, factory capacity, acquisition, and separation risk.

Caterpillar's FY2025 operating cash flow was `$11.739B` against `$2.821B` of
PP&E capex and `$47M` of acquisitions, producing an `$8.871B` mechanical screen.
Eaton's operating cash flow was `$4.472B` against `$919M` of PP&E capex and
`$1.490B` of acquisitions, producing about `$2.063B`. These are not directly
comparable owner-cash results because Caterpillar's finance subsidiary and
dealer system create balance-sheet claims, while Eaton's acquisition program
and unbilled-revenue growth create a different burden.

The correct filing tests are therefore distinct. Caterpillar requires dealer
inventory, service attachment, finance-receivable aging, allowance coverage,
warranty, used-equipment values, and buyback timing. Eaton requires backlog
margin, billed versus unbilled receivables, inventory, acquisition-cohort cash,
capacity utilization, debt, and Mobility separation costs. The cleaner physical
control point is the one that converts demand into cash after its own burden,
not the one with the larger backlog or higher headline margin.

### Intel versus KLA: manufacturing ownership versus process-control ownership

Intel and KLA both sit near the technical bottleneck in semiconductor
manufacturing, but they own different parts of the economics. Intel owns the
product design and fab system and must fund process development, factories,
equipment, packaging, labor, inventory, suppliers, and utilization before the
customer cash is proven. KLA sells inspection and metrology tools and services
that help many manufacturers protect yield. Its control point is narrower, but
the capital burden is lighter and service revenue provides a recurring layer.

Intel's FY2025 operating cash flow was `$9.697B` against `$11.204B` of net
capital expenditures, producing negative adjusted free cash flow of about
`$1.612B`. KLA's FY2026 operating cash flow was `$4.143B` against `$376M` of
capex, producing about `$3.767B` before buybacks, debt, stock compensation, and
required technology reinvestment. This is not proof that KLA is cycle-proof or
that Intel cannot recover. It shows why owning a manufacturing bottleneck can
require much more capital than selling the control tools used by many fabs.

The filing tests differ accordingly. Intel requires 18A yield, external-foundry
volume, utilization, gross margin, capital commitments, inventory, partner
funding, government terms, dilution, and owner cash. KLA requires service
renewal, product placements, customer capex, gross margin, technical labor,
stock compensation, debt, and the persistence of process complexity. The
strategic importance of a layer does not determine its common-owner economics;
the required capital and timing of cash do.

The security-control cohort adds a different form of relationship ownership.
CrowdStrike and Palo Alto try to own the data, detection, and operating
workflow used during a security event. Zscaler owns the access and policy path;
F5 combines application control with more hardware and services burden. ARR,
RPO, and deferred revenue show contracted visibility, but the durable test is
renewal and expansion after cloud processing, specialized labor, incident
remediation, acquisition integration, support, research, and SBC. A control
platform is economically strong only when customers continue to trust it and
the provider converts that trust into cash per diluted share.

## Research standard from here

The next research pass should not broaden the roster simply to create more
names. It should strengthen the current map by:

1. replacing packet-only facts with direct filing evidence where available;
2. finishing normalized owner-cash bridges for the remaining priority names;
3. tying each valuation case to a measurable operating denominator;
4. updating the common watchlist from new 10-Ks, 10-Qs, IR materials, and calls;
5. separating allegations from reconciliation questions; and
6. recording what would make each live conclusion wrong.
