# Cross-sector economic-control and forensic watchlist

Date: 2026-09-13

## Purpose

This is the decision layer above the completed sector comparisons. It is not a
price-based ranking and it is not an allegation of misconduct. It ranks the
next research questions by how much they can change the archive's central
hypothesis:

> A company is attractive when it controls a scarce, repeated relationship or
> workflow and converts that control into durable owner cash after the full
> labor, inventory, maintenance, regulatory, financing, and reinvestment
> burden.

The map preserves the distinction between strategic control and economic
profit. A backlog, AUM balance, installed base, digital transaction count,
franchise count, or adjusted EPS result is an input to the investigation, not
its conclusion.

## How to read the priorities

| Priority | Meaning | Required evidence before a stronger conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — clean-control test | The company appears to own a scarce control point with relatively observable cash economics; the main unresolved question is durability, reinvestment, or valuation | Multi-year organic growth, incremental margin, cash conversion, capital intensity, incentives, and a price-implied return bridge |
| 2 — control-with-burden test | The relationship may be valuable, but the company or its counterparties carry a material physical, balance-sheet, project, or regulatory burden | Cash after maintenance capital and working capital, burden-carrier health, financing terms, and evidence that returns exceed the burden |
| 3 — contradiction / normalization test | Reported visibility or growth is particularly vulnerable to mix, timing, marks, leverage, add-backs, or weak conversion | Direct GAAP-to-adjusted-to-cash reconciliation and a filing-based falsifier before relying on the narrative |

These are research priorities, not buy/sell labels. “Clean” means cleaner
relative economics within this framework; it does not mean cheap or risk-free.

The cross-lane pattern is strongest where the controller owns a repeated
workflow but does not need to fund every unit of physical capacity. That creates
an important asymmetry: asset-light economics may be genuinely superior, or
they may simply transfer the hardest costs to franchisees, hospitals,
manufacturers, customers, insurers, or property owners. The next filing must
show whether the counterparty remains healthy enough to keep funding the
network.

The cleanest current *economic-control* candidates are Intuitive Surgical,
JPMorgan, CME, Aon, Fastenal, Grainger, F5, KLA, and Marriott. The strongest
*contradiction* cases are Baxter, Stryker, Core & Main, WESCO, Quanta, Apollo,
T. Rowe Price, Host, and the heavily franchised restaurant systems. This is a
research posture, not an investment ranking: several clean-control candidates
carry high valuation expectations, while several contradiction cases may be
cheap precisely because the burden is visible.

## The highest-value contradiction tests

### 1. Control point versus burden carrier

The strongest apparent control businesses often sit beside a burdened
counterparty. Marriott, RBI, Yum!, and Wingstop can collect fees without
operating every restaurant, but the franchise thesis fails economically if
franchisees cannot earn enough to maintain the network. Aon and Apollo can own
valuable financial relationships, but their clean-looking fee layers must be
tested beside debt, insurance, marks, credit losses, and incentive payments.

The next evidence is therefore not another customer-count metric. It is the
health of the party that must keep funding the system.

### 2. Visibility versus conversion

Vertiv, WESCO, Sterling, and Quanta demonstrate why backlog is a useful
question but a weak conclusion. The relevant sequence is:

`order/backlog -> recognized revenue -> gross profit -> working-capital cash -> maintenance capital -> owner cash`

Any break in that chain should lower confidence even if the demand narrative
remains intact. The same logic applies to AUM, installed systems, restaurant
openings, and digital transactions.

### 3. Recurring revenue versus recurring capital

Intuitive Surgical, Equinix, Digital Realty, Stryker, and KLA may have strong
recurring or hard-to-defer demand, but recurrence does not eliminate the cost
of keeping the system useful. The next filing must identify maintenance versus
growth capital, inventory or capacity required to serve growth, and the return
on the next dollar invested.

### 4. Adjusted earnings versus owner economics

Apollo, Baxter, Generac, Aon, and restaurant franchisors are especially useful
normalization cases. The work must preserve the bridge:

`GAAP profit -> management adjustments -> operating cash flow -> capex and acquisitions -> debt/equity claims -> owner cash`

An adjustment is not automatically improper. It becomes decision-relevant when
it recurs, requires cash, tracks the operating model, or changes the apparent
valuation multiple.

## Portfolio-level research sequence

1. **Finish the clean-control set:** F5, KLA, CME, Aon, Fastenal, Grainger,
   Marriott, and Intuitive Surgical. Establish normalized incremental margins,
   cash conversion, capital intensity, and price-implied returns.
2. **Stress the burden set:** Vertiv, Equinix, Digital Realty, Apollo, Stryker,
   BlackRock, RBI, and Yum!. Test whether the control point survives a real
   capex, credit, volume, or franchisee stress.
3. **Resolve contradiction cases:** Baxter, Generac, WESCO, Sterling, Quanta,
   CAVA, T. Rowe Price, and Host. Do not extrapolate headline growth until the
   normalization bridge is complete.
4. **Build a common valuation worksheet:** revenue growth, incremental margin,
   reinvestment rate, normalized tax/financing burden, owner cash, terminal
   economics, and market-implied expectations. Keep sector-specific metrics
   alongside—not instead of—the common bridge.
5. **Update only on falsifying evidence:** next annual reports, quarterly cash
   flows, segment disclosures, debt maturities, SBC, impairments, inventory,
   receivables, contract assets, franchisee health, and capex disclosures.

## Skeptical Reader Test

- Does the posture distinguish a control point from a cash-return conclusion?
- Does every burden claim name who funds labor, inventory, maintenance,
  regulation, financing, or failure?
- Does every “clean” claim have a multi-year cash and reinvestment test?
- Does every “shenanigan” concern identify a specific reconciliation rather
  than imply intent?
- Does the watchlist tell us what a future filing could prove wrong?
