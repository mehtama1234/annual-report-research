# Financial intermediation and property: forensic comparison

Date: 2026-09-13

This lane compares JPMorgan, American Express, Aon, CME Group, BlackRock,
Apollo, T. Rowe Price, Marriott, and Host Hotels. The companies do not share one accounting model, so the
comparison is about control points and burden transfer rather than headline
margin ranking. The packet evidence is read through the Alden durability lens,
the Damodaran value/reinvestment lens, and a forensic lens for marks, flows,
adjustments, leverage, and capital requirements.

Annaly adds the most leveraged carry case to this lane. It does not own a
deposit franchise or a fee-only workflow; it packages Agency-MBS exposure for
common shareholders through repo financing, hedges, servicing, and dividends.

## The lane question

When capital, risk, travel, and institutional complexity increase, who owns
the relationship and who carries the balance-sheet or property burden?

| Company | Control point | Cleaner economics | Burden that can be hidden by headline metrics |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| JPMorgan | Deposits, payments, credit, and capital-markets relationship | Diversified spread and fee income supported by scale and low-cost deposits | Credit losses, deposit repricing, regulatory capital, technology, market-cycle revenue, and conduct |
| American Express | Premium card-member, merchant, and closed-loop payment relationship | Merchant discount, card fees, data, and membership economics in one system | Rewards, marketing, funded receivables, credit losses, funding, and capital |
| Aon | Risk-advisory and insurance-broker workflow | Fee income without warehousing underwriting risk | Client retention, compensation, acquisitions, and organic-growth quality |
| CME | Regulated exchange, clearing, and benchmark data | Network toll on risk transfer and volatility | Regulation, technology, contract competition, and cycle-sensitive volumes |
| BlackRock | ETF, institutional, private-market, and technology platform | Scale, recurring fees, and multiple monetization layers | Market beta, fee compression, acquisition integration, and private-mark valuation |
| Apollo | Asset management plus Athene insurance/spread platform | Fee-related earnings plus insurance-linked spread income | Credit underwriting, duration, leverage, and mark/realization dependence |
| T. Rowe Price | Public-market asset management and distribution | Capital-light recurring fees when flows and fee rates hold | Persistent outflows, fee compression, and product repositioning costs |
| State Street | Institutional custody, administration, asset servicing, and related technology | Recurring servicing and management fees with embedded workflow switching cost | Market-level and fee-rate exposure, technology and control investment, collateral, liquidity, and operational failure |
| Marriott | Brand, loyalty, franchise, and co-brand ecosystem | Asset-light fees and customer relationship control | Franchisee health, brand standards, developer pipeline, and travel cycle |
| Host Hotels | Premium hotel property ownership | Scarce locations and operating real estate | Renovation, insurance, labor, occupancy, and property capex |
| Annaly | Mortgage portfolio, repo, hedge, and housing-finance exposure | Liquid Agency-MBS carry and servicing income | Repo rollover, collateral, duration, prepayment, spread, hedge, book-value, and dilution risk |

## The important distinction: control is not the same as economic profit

Aon can control a recurring client workflow without taking insurer balance-sheet
risk. CME controls clearing and benchmark infrastructure. Marriott controls a
brand and loyalty system while franchisees and property owners fund much of the
physical network. These are potentially clean control points.

BlackRock, Apollo, and T. Rowe Price control capital-allocation relationships,
but their earnings remain exposed to market levels, product mix, client flows,
fee rates, and in Apollo's case, insurance and credit risk. Host owns the
physical asset and can benefit from scarcity, but it also bears the costs that
asset-light brands avoid. A proper comparison therefore asks what costs are
outside the parent-company income statement or excluded from the preferred
metric.

## Packet evidence and forensic interpretation

### Annaly: a strong carry quarter is not a stable owner return

Annaly's Q2 2026 economic return was `5.5%`, book value per common share was
`$20.15`, and earnings available for distribution was `$0.79` per share against
a `$0.75` dividend. Economic leverage was `5.6x`. Repurchase agreements were
`$86.9B`, with a weighted average rate of `3.85%` and weighted average
remaining maturity of `33` days.

The quarter also included `$552M` of net derivative gains and negative `$319M`
of net gains on investments and other. Those figures belong in the economic
return, but they are not recurring coupon spread. Annaly raised about `$447M`
through ATM common-equity sales, and common shares outstanding increased from
`707.0M` at December 31, 2025 to `750.6M` at June 30, 2026. EAD coverage and
book-value growth must therefore be judged after funding rollover, hedge
performance, and dilution. [Annaly Q2 2026 Form 10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1043219/000104321926000058/nly-20260630.htm)
and the [Annaly dossier](../deep-company-pages/annaly-capital-management-inc.md)
carry the detailed bridge.

### Aon: fee economics require retention and acquisition normalization

Aon reported Q2 2026 organic revenue growth of 5%, adjusted operating margin of
28.9%, and free cash flow of $483M. This is attractive evidence for a trust and
workflow intermediary: the company can monetize complexity without directly
warehousing the insured risk.

The forensic test is whether organic growth is genuinely retained-client and
new-business growth, or whether it is primarily rate, acquired revenue, or
favorable insurance-market pricing. Reconcile free cash flow after restructuring
and acquisition payments, track client retention and compensation, and separate
brokerage float or investment effects from recurring advisory economics.

### JPMorgan: scale and deposits do not remove capital and credit claims

JPMorgan's FY2025 net income applicable to common stockholders was `$55.681B`
on `$182.447B` of total net revenue, with `$2.559T` of deposits and provision
for credit losses of `$14.212B`. Q2 2026 reported net income was `$21.2B`, but
net income excluding significant items was `$16.9B`. Credit costs were `$2.5B`,
including `$2.4B` of net charge-offs and a `$149M` net reserve build; average
loans grew `10%` and average deposits grew `7%` year over year.

The bank is the lane's scale and liquidity control case. Low-cost deposits,
payments, underwriting, markets, and technology can produce cleaner economics
than a smaller lender, but net income is not unrestricted cash. The owner-cash
denominator must retain enough earnings for credit losses, loan growth,
regulatory capital, liquidity, technology, compliance, and operational
resilience. The Q2 standard and advanced CET1 ratios were `14.1%` and `14.2%`,
and reported ROTCE was `29%` versus `23%` excluding significant items.

The comparison test is whether recurring net interest income, payments, and
client fees remain strong after deposit repricing and market-revenue
normalization. Visa and equity-investment gains, favorable trading conditions,
reserve timing, and credit-card growth should not be treated as ordinary
earnings without a separate recurrence and capital requirement judgment.

### American Express: closed-loop control carries a concentrated funding burden

American Express reported FY2025 revenue of `$72.2B`, net income of `$10.8B`,
and billed business of approximately `$1.67T`. Q2 2026 revenue was about
`$19.6B`, net income about `$3.1B`, and diluted EPS `$4.53`; management raised
full-year revenue-growth guidance to `10%`.

The closed-loop model gives American Express more control over the member,
merchant, and payment relationship than a fee-only network. It also means the
company bears rewards, marketing, receivable funding, credit losses, fraud,
merchant acceptance, and capital requirements. The Membership Rewards
liability was approximately `$16.5B` at FY2025 year-end, so points and benefits
are an economic obligation rather than excess cash.

The comparison test is whether billed-business and fee growth produce durable
common earnings after rewards and member costs, funding expense, net write-offs,
reserve requirements, and capital growth. JPMorgan has broader balance-sheet
diversification and deposit funding; American Express has a more focused
premium relationship and closed-loop data. Neither can be valued from payment
volume or account additions alone.

### CME: the toll road still has volume and technology dependencies

CME's Q2 2026 average daily volume was 29.8M contracts, revenue was $1.7B,
operating income was $1.1B, and market-data revenue reached a record $238M.
The exchange and clearing model is a powerful network-effect case, with risk
transfer, collateral, contract standards, and data reinforcing each other.
The 2025 filing makes the cash quality unusually visible: $4.277B of OCF
against $83.5M of property purchases, while 2026 first-half OCF was $2.207B
against $45.4M of property purchases. Those figures are cleaner than most
financial intermediaries, but they include the economics of reinvesting client
collateral and must be separated from $159.7B of restricted performance-bond
and guaranty-fund balances.

The forensic question is not whether CME has a moat; it is whether the current
earnings level is normalized across volatility and rate cycles. 2025 investment
income was $5.737B while other non-operating expense was $4.834B, making
collateral interest and related distributions a material denominator issue.
Track contract mix, volume, clearing and transaction fees, market-data pricing,
technology capital, regulatory costs, collateral rates, and competitor or
decentralized-market substitution. High operating margins can conceal the need
to continually maintain resilience, security, and market integrity.

### BlackRock: AUM growth can mask fee-rate and valuation complexity

BlackRock's Q2 2026 AUM reached about $15.3T, revenue was $6.352B, and adjusted
operating margin was 45.9%. The platform combines ETF scale, private markets,
technology services, data, and outsourcing. Technology and subscription revenue
reached $1.981B in FY2025, which makes the company more diversified than a pure
beta-fee story.

The forensic test is to bridge AUM growth into average AUM, net flows, fee rate,
product mix, technology margins, acquisition synergies, and cash conversion.
HPS and Preqin expansion can widen the platform or introduce private-market
valuation and integration risk. Reported asset growth from market appreciation
must not be treated as evidence of new client control.

### Apollo: fee income and spread income must be separated

Apollo's Q2 2026 total AUM was about $1.05T, fee-generating AUM about $858B,
adjusted net income about $1.31B, spread-related earnings $877M, and
fee-related earnings $785M. The integrated Athene model is economically
different from a pure alternative manager because insurance liabilities and
credit spreads are part of the earnings engine.

The forensic question is whether spread income is durable after credit losses,
capital requirements, duration hedging, and funding costs. Track fee-paying AUM
versus total AUM, realized income versus marks, insurance underwriting and
reserve development, asset quality, leverage, and liquidity. “Recurring” spread
earnings should not be treated as fee income merely because management presents
them as a stable engine.

### T. Rowe Price: the clean contrast case is outflows plus fee compression

T. Rowe Price ended 2025 with $1.78T of AUM but $56.9B of net outflows. Revenue
was $7.31B, investment-advisory revenue $6.60B, and the effective fee rate fell
to 39.4 basis points from 41.0 basis points. The contrast is analytically useful:
asset levels can rise while the economic relationship weakens.

The key test is whether alternatives, fixed income, ETFs, retirement, and SMAs
replace lost fee rate and flows without disproportionate distribution and
technology spending. Adjusted EPS was $9.72 versus GAAP diluted EPS of $9.24;
the gap is modest here, but the more important quality risk is secular fee
compression rather than a large one-quarter adjustment.

### State Street: institutional assets are not corporate assets

State Street reported approximately `$11.0B` of FY2025 fee revenue and Q2 2026
fee revenue growth of `13%`, with AUC/A of approximately `$50.7T` and AUM of
approximately `$5.1T`. The custody and administration relationship can be
sticky because records, settlement, reporting, collateral, and compliance
workflows are expensive to move. But AUC/A and AUM are scale measures, not
owner cash: market appreciation can increase them without new flows, and client
fees can compress.

The cleaner denominator is recurring servicing and management fees after
compensation, technology, cybersecurity, control, and regulatory costs. FX,
securities finance, and net interest income need separate treatment because
their market, collateral, and rate sensitivities differ. State Street is less
balance-sheet-heavy than a lender in direct credit exposure, but operational
failure can still create client losses, remediation, regulatory action, and lost
trust. The next filings should show fee rate, net flows, client retention,
technology investment, operational incidents, collateral exposure, and capital
returns.

### Marriott and Host: the brand owner versus property owner split

Marriott's Q2 2026 worldwide RevPAR grew 3.4%, and its development pipeline was
about 629,000 rooms. Its asset-light model controls brand, loyalty, franchise
fees, and co-brand economics while hotel owners fund much of the property base.
Host's Q2 comparable hotel RevPAR was $251.53, up 7.0%, comparable Total RevPAR
was $417.58, and adjusted EBITDAre was $525M. Host captures property scarcity
but carries renovation, insurance, labor, and operating leverage.

This is a clean test of burden transfer. Marriott may show better capital-light
returns, but its economics depend on franchisee solvency, brand standards, and
continued development. Host may own the scarce asset, but the property-level
cash return is exposed to capex and operating costs. RevPAR growth alone cannot
decide which shareholder is better compensated for risk.

## Alden / Damodaran / forensic synthesis

Alden's lens favors businesses that remain necessary when markets become noisy:
risk intermediation, clearing, custody, and operating travel networks can have
stronger demand floors than discretionary asset gathering. But market stress
can also increase balance-sheet and liquidity risk, especially for Apollo and
property owners.

Damodaran's lens separates fee growth from value creation. AUM, pipeline,
volume, and RevPAR are drivers, not value. The relevant variables are net flows,
fee rates, incremental distribution costs, capital intensity, loss reserves,
maintenance capex, and the return on acquired or newly deployed capital.

Our forensic lens asks whether the preferred metric is the right metric. FRE,
ANI, adjusted operating income, distributable earnings, EBITDAre, and adjusted
EPS can be useful, but each can omit a different economic burden. The analysis
must preserve GAAP, cash, balance-sheet, and capital requirements alongside the
management metric.

## Falsifiers and next evidence

The lane thesis weakens if:

- AUM or pipeline rises while net flows, fee rates, or cash conversion deteriorate;
- volatility-driven exchange volume normalizes without durable data or clearing growth;
- insurance spread earnings rise while reserves, credit losses, or capital needs worsen;
- “one-time” integration, restructuring, or mark-related adjustments recur;
- franchise growth leaves property owners economically weaker or unable to fund standards;
- property RevPAR rises but maintenance capex, insurance, labor, or debt service consume the gain; or
- acquisitions add reported scale without improving organic growth and returns on capital.

The next filing bridge should capture average and ending AUM, net flows, fee
rate, realized versus unrealized income, operating cash flow, free cash flow,
capital requirements, leverage, reserve development, client retention, volume,
pipeline conversion, RevPAR, maintenance capex, and shareholder distributions.

The cross-sector conclusion is conditional but useful: the cleanest economics
often belong to the party controlling the relationship or standard, while the
largest hidden risks sit with whoever must fund the balance sheet, property,
technology resilience, or customer-acquisition promise.

The matrix changes the interpretation of “clean economics.” CME and Aon have
the clearest relationship-to-cash path, but CME’s collateral interest and Aon’s
acquisition/debt burden require normalization. Marriott controls a valuable
brand/loyalty layer while franchisees fund much of the physical system; Host
captures the scarce asset but carries the costs Marriott avoids. Apollo is the
most important warning that a fee-related label does not remove insurance and
credit risk. T. Rowe Price is the useful negative control: capital-light cash
conversion can remain strong while the underlying client relationship weakens
through persistent outflows.

## Filing-based cross-sector tests

For the next filings, the comparable question is who funds resilience:

- Aon and T. Rowe Price: does client retention or net flow quality support the
  fee stream before cost cutting and buybacks?
- CME and Apollo: are interest income, marks, and spread earnings being
  capitalized as recurring fee economics without the associated capital and
  credit burden?
- BlackRock: do acquired platforms and private-market capabilities increase
  fee-paying cash returns after integration and compensation?
- Marriott and Host: does room/RevPAR growth remain after franchisee/property
  capex, labor, insurance, loyalty, interest, and debt service?

This preserves the central framework: control identifies where value may sit;
the burden bridge determines who actually earns the return.
