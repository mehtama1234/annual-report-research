# Financial Intermediation: From Client Relationship to Common-Owner Cash

Date: `2026-09-15`

## The question

Financial companies often look asset-light because they do not manufacture a
physical product. That does not mean their earnings are simple or freely
distributable. The company may be managing somebody else's assets, holding
customer deposits, investing policyholder float, clearing collateral, carrying
receivables, or owning a physical property platform.

The correct chain is:

`customer relationship -> transaction, asset, liability, or risk -> reported
revenue -> claims and operating cost -> required liquidity and capital -> cash
available to common owners`

This page combines the archive's bank, payments, exchange, asset-management,
broker, insurer, private-capital, hotel-brand, and property-owner work. The
models are compared by control point and burden, not by headline margin.

## Terms that must remain separate

- **Client assets:** securities or capital belonging to clients, not the
  manager's common shareholders.
- **Deposits:** customer funding that supports a bank's assets but remains a
  withdrawal obligation.
- **Float:** funds held before a future insurance claim or payment; investment
  income can benefit the insurer, but the funds are not unqualified surplus.
- **Collateral:** assets supporting clearing or trading obligations; collateral
  interest can produce income, but default resources and customer claims remain.
- **Required capital:** equity that must remain in the company or regulated
  subsidiary to absorb losses and maintain trust.
- **Owner cash:** the residual after the business funds its obligations,
  operating platform, required capital, maintenance investment, and growth that
  is part of the normal model.

These distinctions address the most common analytical error in this lane:
calling a client asset, collateral balance, payable timing benefit, or
performance mark the same thing as recurring common-owner earnings.

The cleanest economics usually arise when the company controls an important
workflow with limited balance-sheet exposure. That advantage can reverse when
the company adds credit, insurance, property, leverage, or acquisition claims
to preserve growth.

## Bank cohort: earnings recovery is not the same as capital-light cash

The money-center-bank comparison is most useful when the same three-year
questions are asked of each company: did revenue grow, did credit cost rise or
fall, did returns improve, and what happened to the capital buffer? The numbers
below are reported company measures, not a claim that the banks have identical
accounting or business mixes.

| Bank | Revenue, net of interest expense | Net income | Credit-cost measure | Return / capital measure |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| JPMorgan | `$158.1B / $177.6B / $182.4B` in 2023/2024/2025 | `$49.6B / $58.5B / $57.0B` | Provision: `$9.3B / $10.7B / $14.2B` | 2025 standard CET1 about `14%`; 2025 common net income `$55.7B` |
| Bank of America | `$98.6B / $101.9B / $113.1B` | `$26.5B / $27.1B / $30.5B` | Net charge-offs / average loans: `0.36% / 0.57% / 0.50%` | ROTCE: `13.5% / 12.9% / 14.2%`; standardized CET1: `11.8% / 11.9% / 11.4%` |
| Citi | `$78.1B / $80.7B / $85.2B` | `$9.2B / $12.7B / $14.3B` | Provision for credit losses and benefits/claims: `$9.2B / $10.1B / $10.3B` | RoTCE: `4.9% / 7.0% / 7.7%`; CET1: `13.4% / 13.6% / 13.2%` |

The first read is positive but limited. JPMorgan and Bank of America produced
large earnings streams while credit costs increased in 2025. Bank of America's
return improved as its standardized CET1 ratio and allowance ratio moved lower.
Citi's return improved from a low base while its transformation and control
burden remained material. These are not accusations of manipulation; they are
reasons to keep earnings, credit, capital, and payout in one frame.

The useful follow-up is denominator-specific. For JPMorgan, normalize Markets,
investment banking, securities gains, and the Apple Card portfolio. For Bank of
America, test deposit pricing, loan growth, charge-offs, allowance coverage,
risk-weighted assets, and the capital needed to support buybacks. For Citi, test
whether Services and Wealth produce durable returns after transformation and
control spending, while Markets and Banking are brought back to ordinary
conditions. A higher EPS number is not enough if the bank has had to use more
capital or accept more balance-sheet risk to produce it.

M&T adds the regional-bank counterpart to this comparison. Its relationship
deposits, treasury activity, commercial lending, and local credit knowledge can
support a durable franchise, but the owner-cash screen cannot treat net interest
income as unrestricted cash. The relevant tests are deposit repricing, normalized
net interest margin, commercial and real-estate credit losses, securities marks,
fee income, technology and control spending, CET1 capital, stress capacity, and
the amount available for dividends or buybacks after balance-sheet growth. Its
screen is registered separately from the money-center cases because a regional
bank has a different mix of concentration, funding, and credit-cycle risk.
Regions adds a Southeast relationship-bank case with a different test: its
low-cost deposits, treasury and wealth attachment, and commercial-credit mix
must be separated from property concentration, deposit repricing, reserve
coverage, securities duration, CET1, and payout capacity. It should not be
treated as interchangeable with M&T simply because both are regional banks.
WaFd adds the smaller thrift-to-business-bank transition. Its new business-bank
label is not accepted as evidence by itself: transaction deposits, treasury
attachment, NIM, new-loan profitability, legacy mortgage and CRE losses,
transition cost, liquidity, CET1, and buyback effects must appear in the filings.

### Regional-bank comparison: relationship quality after capital and credit

The regional-bank cases can be compared without pretending that a bank has
industrial free cash flow. The useful screen is the combination of earnings,
funding cost, margin, credit, return on tangible equity, and CET1 capital. The
latest figures below are evidence for the next question, not a ranking:

| Bank | FY2025 earnings anchor | Latest return / margin evidence | Latest capital and credit evidence | What still has to be proven |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| M&T | Net income about `$2.85B` | Q2 2026 NIM `3.70%`; net income about `$818M` | CET1 about `10.19%` | Whether relationship deposits and commercial credit produce through-cycle returns after losses and capital retention. |
| Regions | Net income about `$2.1B`; FY2025 ROTCE about `18.25%` | Q2 2026 interest-bearing deposit cost about `1.69%` | CET1 about `10.7%`; annualized net charge-offs about `42` bps | Whether low-cost deposits and treasury/wealth fees remain durable when credit and deposit competition worsen. |
| WaFd | Net income about `$226M`; FY2025 ROTCE about `9.20%` | Q3 FY2026 NIM `2.81%`; ROTCE about `11.0%` | Deposits about `$20.9B`; Q2 nonperforming assets about `0.48%` of assets | Whether the mortgage-to-business-bank transition creates profitable new lending without weakening liquidity or legacy-credit quality. |
| Truist | Net income available to common shareholders `$4.974B` | Q2 2026 ROTCE `15.4%`; noninterest income grew `17%` | CET1 about `10.9%`; 2025 included `$156M` severance and `$130M` legal settlement | Whether fee growth and merger cleanup remain after compensation, integration, credit, and capital costs normalize. |
| U.S. Bancorp | FY2025 net income `$7.570B` | Q2 2026 ROTCE `18.7%`; NIM `2.79%` | CET1 `10.8%`; average deposits up `2.4%` year over year | Whether fee growth, payment-network costs, BTIG integration, fraud, credit provisions, and capital support durable distributable earnings. |
| PNC | FY2025 net income `$6.997B` | Q2 2026 ROTCE `17.9%`; average loans up `4%` sequentially | CET1 `9.9%`; loans/deposits at year-end 2025 `$331.5B`/`$440.9B` | Whether FirstBank growth and capital-markets activity earn above funding, integration, credit, and capital costs. |

The comparison changes the interpretation of “quality.” Regions and U.S.
Bancorp show strong recent tangible-equity returns, but both still depend on
deposit pricing, credit normalization, and capital capacity. M&T has a stronger
recent margin signal, while WaFd offers the clearest transition case and the
lowest current return base. Truist and PNC show why scale and fee growth are
not enough: merger, legal, capital-markets, and risk-weighted-asset effects
must be separated from organic relationship economics.

The common owner-cash test for this cohort is:

`net income after credit and operating costs -> capital retained for loans,
payments, liquidity, technology, and stress -> capital releasable to the parent
-> dividends and repurchases per diluted share`

The falsifier is a rising ROTCE or EPS number accompanied by higher deposit
cost, weaker credit, falling CET1, greater wholesale funding, or payouts that
leave too little capital for the next stress period. That is why the table is a
within-model comparison, not a cross-sector valuation ranking.

### Public asset managers: AUM growth versus fee-relationship quality

Public asset managers have a lighter physical balance sheet than banks, but
their economic asset is not headline AUM. It is the retained fee relationship
after market movement, net flows, fee rates, investment talent, distribution,
technology, acquisitions, and dilution.

| Manager | FY2025 scale and flow evidence | Fee / margin evidence | Owner-cash and forensic question |
|---|---|---|---|
| BlackRock | Revenue `$24.216B`; reported net inflows `$698B`; Q2 2026 AUM about `$15.3T` and net inflows `$192B` | FY2025 adjusted margin `44.1%`; technology/subscription revenue `$1.981B`; FY2025 OCF `$3.927B` less `$375M` capex and `$3.496B` acquisitions left only a `$56M` diagnostic residual before other claims | Can the enlarged platform earn back acquisition cash and `$1.307B` of SBC while fee-paying AUM, technology revenue, and per-share cash grow? |
| T. Rowe Price | FY2025 ending AUM `$1.776T`; net client outflows `$56.9B`; Q2 2026 AUM `$1.893T` but outflows `$6.5B` | Effective fee rate fell from `41.0` to `39.4` bps in FY2025 and `38.1` bps in Q2 2026; FY2025 net revenues `$7.315B` | Did market appreciation hide a weakening client relationship, and can ETFs, SMAs, retirement, and alternatives replace active outflows without damaging contribution margin? |
| Invesco | FY2025 ending AUM `$2.170T`; average AUM `$2.000T`; total net flows `$104.4B`; Q2 2026 long-term inflows `$45.1B` | FY2025 operating revenue `$6.377B`; adjusted operating margin `33.4%`; QQQ AUM `$407.2B`; private-markets AUM `$130.7B`; China JV AUM `$132.5B` | Are flows broad and fee-paying, or concentrated in QQQ and lower-fee products? The `$1.8B` Q4 2025 non-cash impairment also needs to remain visible. |
| Franklin Resources | FY2025 ending AUM `$1.661T`; long-term net outflows `$97.4B`; Q3 FY2026 AUM `$1.792T` and inflows `$18.4B` | FY2025 revenue `$8.771B`; effective fee rate fell from `41.1` to `40.5` bps; Q3 alternatives AUM about `$294.2B` | Is the apparent flow repair broad, durable, and fee-paying, or is it market appreciation and alternatives growth masking legacy active outflows and fixed distribution cost? |

The comparison produces four different readings. BlackRock has the strongest
current flow and scale evidence, but acquisition spending and compensation make
its cash conversion less automatic than its margin suggests. T. Rowe Price is
the clearest example of rising AUM coexisting with client outflows and fee
compression. Invesco has better recent flows but greater concentration and
product-mix risk. Franklin may be repairing flows, yet the repair must survive
fee-rate pressure, Western Asset and legacy-platform costs, and the different
liquidity and performance economics of alternatives.

The common test is:

`average fee-paying AUM × fee rate + recurring technology or distribution fees
- investment talent - distribution - technology - compliance - acquisitions -
SBC and dilution = common-owner cash per share`

Market appreciation can raise AUM without improving the relationship. A manager
therefore fails the durability test if AUM and adjusted EPS rise while net
flows remain weak, fee rates fall, acquisition cash consumes operating cash, or
diluted per-share cash does not improve.

BlackRock's current value is above the base screen because the market is

These are scenario inputs, not price targets. They are most useful as questions:
which manager has already demonstrated the cash needed by the base case, and
which manager requires the most unproven flow, mix, acquisition, or margin
repair?

### Insurance brokers: organic fee engine versus acquisition engine

Insurance brokers avoid most underwriting losses, but they do not avoid the
cost of maintaining the producer and client relationship. The relevant
comparison is organic fee growth after professional compensation and working
capital, plus the cash and financing required to buy additional relationships.

| Broker | FY2025 operating evidence | Acquisition and balance-sheet burden | Owner-cash question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aon | Revenue about `$17.2B`; organic growth `6%`; operating cash flow `$3.481B`; operating margin `25.3%` | Acquisitions `$394M`; capex `$263M`; `$14.660B` long-term debt; 2025 also included a `$1.201B` gain from business sales and `$2.349B` of sale proceeds | Does organic growth remain real after retention, pricing/exposure, compensation, pensions, restructuring, debt, and the exclusion of divestiture proceeds? |
| Gallagher | Revenue `$13.942B`; organic fee growth `6%`; operating cash flow `$1.930B` | Acquisition cash `$15.766B`; goodwill acquired `$9.904B`; long-term debt `$12.873B`; 33 acquisitions closed in 2025 | Can AssuredPartners and later cohorts earn above the cost of acquisition after producer retention, integration, earnouts, interest, goodwill, and dilution? |
| Marsh McLennan | Revenue about `$26.981B`; underlying growth about `4%`; operating cash flow `$5.292B` | Capex `$291M`; acquisitions `$652M`; residual after listed uses about `$4.349B`; long-term debt `$19.587B`; SBC `$394M` | Is the stronger current residual durable after client-fund timing, receivables, talent cost, litigation, recurring M&A, debt, and stock compensation? |

The contrast is not simply “organic good, acquisitions bad.” Acquisitions can
create value when the acquired producers retain clients, generate new business,
and produce cash returns above the hurdle rate. The forensic risk is allowing
acquired revenue, adjusted EBITDA, or goodwill to stand in for that return.
Aon has the cleanest current acquisition burden of the three but also relies on
divestiture gains and carries substantial debt. Marsh produced the strongest
listed residual, but its large debt, talent cost, receivables, and acquisition
history still matter. Gallagher is the clearest stress case because its 2025
acquisition cash exceeded operating cash flow by a wide margin.

The broker falsifier is a widening gap between organic growth and reported
growth, or between adjusted earnings and cash per diluted share. Watch client
retention, producer retention, revenue per producer, receivable collection,
earnout payments, acquisition cohorts after 12/24/36 months, goodwill, debt,
interest, and capital returns together.

## A common liability-and-capital bridge

The models should not share a single free-cash-flow ranking, but they can be
read through one disciplined sequence: identify what the customer or counterparty
funds, identify what remains a company obligation, and then measure the capital
that must stay in the system before common owners can be paid.

| Model | Representative evidence | Starting measure | Claims ahead of common owners | Correct owner-cash question |
|---|---|---:|---|---|
| Deposit-funded bank | JPMorgan FY2025 common net income `$55.681B`; deposits `$2.559T`; provision for credit losses `$14.212B`; CET1 about `14.1%` in Q2 2026 | Common earnings after provision and operating cost | Deposit stability, credit losses, liquidity, technology, conduct, and CET1 capital | How much earnings remain after through-cycle losses and capital needed to support the balance sheet? |
| Regional bank | M&T FY2025 net income about `$2.85B`; Q2 2026 net interest margin `3.70%`; CET1 about `10.19%` | Net interest income and fees after credit cost and operating expense | Deposit repricing, commercial and real-estate losses, securities marks, regional concentration, and CET1 capital | What earnings remain after a regional credit cycle and the capital needed to retain relationship deposits and grow safely? |
| Closed-loop card | American Express FY2025 revenue `$72.2B`, net income `$10.8B`, billed business `$1.67T`, rewards liability `$16.5B` | Discount revenue, interest, and fees after rewards and credit | Receivables funding, charge-offs, rewards, fraud, partner obligations, and capital | Does incremental spend produce risk-adjusted per-share cash after benefits and losses? |
| Exchange and clearing | CME FY2025 OCF `$4.277B`, property purchases `$83.5M`; restricted default and guaranty-fund balances about `$159.7B` | Fee cash after technology and property investment | Default resources, collateral obligations, regulation, controls, and volume cycles | How much fee cash is unrestricted after default protection and technology resilience? |
| Custody and servicing | State Street fee revenue about `$11.0B`; AUC/A about `$50.7T`; AUM about `$5.1T` | Fee revenue on serviced assets | Cyber, settlement, controls, compensation, fee compression, and operational failure | Is fee-paying client activity retained at a sustainable rate, independent of asset appreciation? |
| Public asset manager | T. Rowe Price 2025 AUM `$1.78T`, net outflows `$56.9B`, fee rate `39.4` bps versus `41.0` | Fee-paying AUM × fee rate | Outflows, compensation, distribution, technology, product migration, and SBC | Does fee cash grow after fee compression and client attrition, not merely after markets rise? |
| Insurance broker | Gallagher FY2025 acquisition cash `$15.766B` against OCF `$1.930B`; Aon Q2 FCF `$483M` | Organic fee cash plus acquisition returns | Producer compensation, retention, earnouts, integration, debt, and amortization | Does organic fee cash cover the acquisition strategy and preserve per-share value? |
| Insurance carrier | Chubb, Markel, MetLife, Prudential scorecard below | Underwriting, spread, and fee earnings after liability funding | Claims, reserves, reinsurance, asset-liability mismatch, required surplus, and subsidiary liquidity | What capital can safely leave regulated entities after stress, not what OCF reports? |
| Property / mortgage vehicle | Host hotel owner and Annaly Agency-MBS carry | Normalized FFO/NOI or mid-cycle carry | Renewal capex, debt, repo, hedges, haircuts, book-value changes, and dilution | Does the distribution remain covered after physical or financing claims and per-share value stress? |

The table explains why a large client-asset, deposit, collateral, premium, or
property balance is not automatically an asset of common shareholders. The
control point may be valuable because it organizes the flow, but the burden may
remain as a withdrawal obligation, policy claim, default resource, debt claim,
property renewal requirement, or regulated capital reserve.

The cross-model conclusion is therefore conditional. Exchanges, custody, and
some brokers can produce cleaner cash because they bear less direct loss risk.
Banks, card issuers, insurers, private-capital platforms, REITs, and mortgage
vehicles can still compound capital, but they require a balance-sheet stress
test before their reported earnings are treated as owner cash.

owner economics. P&C underwriting can look strong before catastrophe or reserve
development. Life spread earnings can look stable before asset marks, lapse
changes, or capital strain. The correct comparison is risk-adjusted capital
release per diluted share after the claims and obligations that make the
business possible.

## Six different cash tests

### 1. Banks: earnings after required capital

The bank receives deposits and uses them to fund loans, securities, payments,
and other customer services. The deposit is useful funding but remains a claim
that can leave the bank. A bank's reported net income is not the amount that can
all be paid out because the bank must absorb future credit losses, meet
liquidity rules, support loan growth, and maintain technology and controls.

The correct bridge is:

`net interest income + durable fees - credit losses - funding cost - operating
cost - required capital growth = distributable common earnings`

Track deposit pricing, loan growth, delinquencies, charge-offs, reserve
development, securities duration, capital ratios, liquidity, technology,
conduct, and diluted shares. A favorable reserve release or trading quarter is
not a normal long-term earnings rate without a separate recurrence judgment.

### 2. Payments and card credit: spend after rewards and losses

American Express and Synchrony control merchant and customer relationships,
but they also fund receivables and customer benefits. Billed business or
purchase volume is an activity measure. Common-owner cash is what remains after
rewards, funding, charge-offs, fraud, servicing, marketing, and regulatory
capital.

The bridge is:

`billed business -> discount, fees, and interest -> rewards and operating cost
-> credit losses and funding -> required capital -> common-owner cash`

The test is whether growth in customers and spend improves risk-adjusted cash
per diluted share, not merely whether payment volume rises.

Affirm belongs beside this group, but its denominator is more specialized than
the ordinary card-issuer screen. Its Q3 FY2026 GMV was about `$11.6B` and RLTC
was `$498M`. The platform controls approval and merchant conversion, but it
also funds or sells installment receivables and retains exposure to expected
losses, funding spreads, servicing, warrants, and capitalized stock
compensation. The correct bridge is:

`GMV -> merchant and interest revenue -> funding, expected loss, servicing,
fraud, and operating cost -> required capital and equity claims -> common value`

Affirm's network engine may eventually deserve a payment or software
denominator, but the credit engine must first show positive contribution through
a weaker funding and consumer-credit cycle. This is why its screen uses
normalized fully diluted operating earnings rather than ordinary operating
cash flow.

### 3. Exchanges and custody: client assets are not company assets

CME and State Street benefit from trusted infrastructure and switching costs.
CME earns transaction, clearing, and data fees. State Street earns custody,
administration, management, securities-finance, and related fees. Both handle
large balances that belong to clients or support market obligations.

The analyst must separate fee revenue from collateral interest, securities
finance, net interest, market appreciation, and client asset growth. Then charge
technology, cybersecurity, settlement controls, default resources, regulation,
and compensation. A large asset or collateral balance can make the platform
important without increasing common-owner cash in the same proportion.

### Market infrastructure: clearing liquidity versus custody workflow

[CME Group](../deep-company-pages/cme-group-inc.md) and
[State Street](../deep-company-pages/state-street-corp.md) both operate trusted
financial infrastructure, but they earn from different events. CME earns
trading, clearing, and data fees while managing a clearing system supported by
client collateral. State Street earns custody, administration, management, and
related service fees while operating a bank balance sheet around institutional
assets.

|---|---|---|---|
| CME Group | FY2025 operating cash flow `$4.277B`, property purchases `$83.5M`, and a listed residual of `$4.183B`; first-half 2026 OCF `$2.207B`; market-data revenue `$462.2M` in the first half | About `$159.7B` of performance bonds and guaranty-fund balances were restricted and matched by liabilities; collateral interest contributed to earnings and varies with rates and client positions; technology and clearing resilience remain required spending | Volume and rate per contract through normalized volatility, market-data renewal, collateral balances and rates, default resources, cyber and resilience investment, SBC, and unrestricted cash per share |

strong, but its earnings include a rate-sensitive collateral component and its

broader institutional workflow and recurring fee base, but its economics are
more exposed to fee negotiation, market levels, client activity, bank funding,

The common falsifier is scale without unrestricted owner cash. CME weakens if
volume or collateral interest normalizes while technology and clearing costs
remain fixed. State Street weakens if AUC/A and AUM rise only through markets,
fee rates fall, client activity weakens, or apparent operating leverage comes
from deferred control and cybersecurity spending.

### 4. Asset managers: fee-paying capital versus headline AUM

An asset manager's economic asset is not total AUM. It is the amount of
fee-paying capital that clients retain at a sustainable fee rate, after
compensation and distribution expense. Market appreciation can increase AUM
without adding client control. AUM can also rise while fee rates fall.

The bridge is:

`fee-paying AUM -> fee rate -> management revenue -> compensation, distribution,
technology, and taxes -> common-owner cash`

Performance income, carried interest, realized income, and marks should remain
separate until their durability and capital claims are demonstrated. Ares,
Apollo, Blackstone, and KKR need an additional test for insurance liabilities,
credit exposure, partner/NCI claims, leverage, realizations, and liquidity.

### 5. Brokers and insurers: relationship control versus loss-bearing capital

Aon and Gallagher advise and place risk while generally avoiding the carrier's
underwriting balance sheet. Their cleaner economics can be weakened by
producer compensation and acquisition dependence.

Chubb and other carriers price and retain risk. They receive premiums and
invest float, but policyholders have contractual claims. The carrier bridge is:

`premium -> expected claims and expenses -> reserve development and catastrophe
risk -> investment income -> required surplus -> common-owner cash`

The broker should be valued on organic fee growth and acquisition returns. The
carrier should be valued on normalized underwriting and investment economics
after required capital. The two should not receive the same multiple because
their downside obligations differ.

### 6. Brands and property: fees versus physical renewal

Marriott controls brand, loyalty, distribution, and franchise relationships.
Host Hotels and Ventas own physical property. The brand can earn fees without
funding every building, while the property owner can benefit from scarcity but
must fund renovation, insurance, labor, taxes, and debt.

For property owners the bridge is:

`property revenue or NOI -> operating costs -> recurring renewal -> debt and
preferred claims -> equity issuance -> value per diluted share`

RevPAR, occupancy, or FFO growth is not enough if renewal capital and financing
consume the benefit. For the brand, pipeline growth is not enough if franchisee
returns weaken and the system cannot maintain standards.

## Private capital and leverage require a separate layer

Private-capital platforms can combine management fees, performance income,
insurance spread, principal investments, and monetizations. These earnings do
not share one risk or one cash denominator.

Use at least four separate questions:

1. How much fee-paying capital exists, by product and fee rate?
2. How much performance income is realized rather than marked?
3. What partner, noncontrolling-interest, preferred, and carried-interest
   claims sit ahead of common owners?
4. What insurance, credit, liquidity, or principal-investment burden is carried
   by the parent or regulated subsidiary?

Apollo, Blackstone, Ares, and KKR may have powerful origination and
distribution relationships. That does not make insurance spread, marks,
realizations, or consolidated cash equivalent to recurring management fees.

## Macro and liquidity lens

Financial intermediaries are sensitive to the availability and price of
liquidity. Higher rates can help some deposit or collateral spreads while
harming securities values, property refinancing, private-asset exits, and
borrower credit. A recession can increase exchange activity and insurance
demand while increasing bank losses and reducing asset-management fees.

The durable companies are not simply those with recurring revenue. They are
those that can remain liquid, trusted, and adequately capitalized when client
flows fall, collateral requirements rise, claims arrive, assets reprice, or
refinancing becomes expensive.

## Filing-based falsifiers

The financial-intermediation thesis weakens if filings show:

- bank earnings relying on reserve releases, trading conditions, or payable
  timing while credit and capital needs worsen;
- payment volume rising while rewards, charge-offs, funding, or dilution consume
  the added revenue;
- custody or exchange assets rising through market appreciation while fee rates,
  retention, or control investment deteriorate;
- AUM or fundraising rising while fee-paying mix, net flows, and fee rates fall;
- performance or spread income being presented as recurring despite marks,
  unrealized gains, credit deterioration, or insurance-capital pressure;
- broker revenue growing primarily through acquisitions while organic retention,
  producer productivity, debt, and per-share cash weaken;
- carrier premiums or investment income rising while reserve development,
  catastrophe losses, or required surplus consume the gain;
- hotel or senior-housing NOI/FFO rising through concessions or operator support
  while property renewal, debt, and dilution increase; or
- levered mortgage dividends being maintained while book value, funding
  liquidity, hedge economics, or share count deteriorate.

## Current conclusion

The cleanest financial economics in the archive tend to come from trusted
relationships with limited direct loss-bearing capital: exchanges, custody,
brokers, and some asset-management products. Banks, card issuers, insurers,
alternative managers with insurance or credit platforms, and property owners
can still be excellent businesses, but their earnings require a balance-sheet
and capital-reservation analysis.

The final investment judgment must answer four questions for each company:

1. What relationship or infrastructure does it control?
2. Whose money, assets, or risk is being handled?
3. What claims must remain funded before common shareholders can be paid?
4. Does the current price require a level of normalized per-share cash that the
   filings have not yet demonstrated?
