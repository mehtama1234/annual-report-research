# Formalized Price Architecture And Approved Increase Systems Comparison

Date: 2026-08-11
Repo: `annual-report-research`

## Plain-English Claim

One of the archive's more important patterns is that some businesses do not rely on improvised pricing power.

They rely on structured price architecture.

The useful question is not only whether a company can charge more.

It is whether the increase mechanism is already embedded in regulation, contracts, lease resets, or narrow operating rights before the next pricing conversation even begins.

## Core Question

Which companies build more durable economics because their price or revenue growth is supported by formalized mechanisms such as rate cases, recovery structures, lease-rate resets, or protected fee rights?

## Why These Five Belong Together

These companies look unrelated on the surface.

- American Water grows through rate cases, infrastructure surcharges, and acquisition approvals.
- Exelon grows through approved recovery mechanisms, transmission visibility, and rate-base planning.
- VeriSign can raise `.com` wholesale pricing inside a narrow contract-and-governance framework.
- Digital Realty pushes revenue growth through booked rent, backlog, and renewal lease-rate resets.
- SBA relies on long leases and escalators inside tower rent rather than on consumer-facing repricing.

The stronger conclusion is that one of the cleaner business qualities in the archive is not just generic pricing power.

It is having a formal system that governs how price or revenue can move.

## Exact Mechanism

### 1. American Water turns infrastructure spend into approved revenue growth

American Water is the cleanest regulated example in the set.

In Q1 `2026`, the company said authorized annualized revenues since `2026-01-01` had increased by `$89M`.

The packet also says `2025` capital investment reached `$3.2B`, with six completed rate cases and seven active cases at year-end.

That means the company is not mainly counting on discretionary customer willingness to pay more.

It is building infrastructure and then converting that investment through a formal approval system.

### 2. Exelon shows how recovery coverage itself becomes part of the moat

Exelon makes the same logic visible at larger grid scale.

Q1 `2026` materials highlighted a revised `$41.7B` four-year capital plan, expected rate-base growth of `7.9%`, and an additional `$12B` to `$17B` transmission opportunity beyond the base plan.

More importantly, by late `2025`, management said close to `90%` of rate base was covered by established recovery mechanisms through `2026` to `2027`.

That is the point.

The company is not pitching only asset growth.

It is pitching asset growth with a relatively formalized recovery path.

### 3. VeriSign proves the pattern can exist outside classic regulation

VeriSign is useful because it is not a utility and not a landlord.

But it still has structured monetization.

Q1 `2026` management announced a `.com` wholesale fee increase from `$10.26` to `$10.97`, effective `2026-11-01`.

That sits alongside fiscal `2025` revenue of `$1.6566B`, operating cash flow of `$1.0911B`, and deferred revenue of `$1.3845B` at year-end.

The company does not need mass consumer persuasion to enact that move.

It operates inside a narrow contractual and governance framework that already defines who controls the fee layer.

### 4. Digital Realty shows that lease structure can be the pricing engine

Digital Realty extends the pattern into AI-era physical infrastructure.

Renewal lease rate increases moved from `6.1%` cash in Q4 `2025` to `5.0%` in Q1 `2026` and then to `25.4%` in Q2 `2026`.

At the same time, Q2 `2026` backlog reached `$1.4B` at Digital Realty's share.

That matters because the company is not just hoping demand stays strong.

It is converting digital-infrastructure scarcity into formal rent resets, backlog, and booked annualized rent.

### 5. SBA shows the quieter lease-escalator version of the same logic

SBA is the simpler tower-rent case.

The `2025` annual materials said the company operated a `46,328`-site portfolio and that site leasing contributed `97.9%` of total segment operating profit.

The packet also says long leases and escalators help support recurring economics, even though customer concentration and churn still matter.

That means the rent stream is not rebuilt from scratch every quarter.

The economic architecture already contains a path for recurring monetization as long as the network dependence remains real.

## The Stronger Conclusion

The stronger conclusion is that some of the archive's better businesses are not only businesses with pricing power.

They are businesses with pricing architecture.

In plain English:

- the operator does not renegotiate value from zero every time
- the path for higher revenue is partly embedded in rules, contracts, leases, or approved recovery systems
- that makes the economics more legible and often more durable than ordinary price-led narratives

This is why rate cases, surcharges, recovery mechanisms, renewal lease rates, escalators, and protected fee rights deserve to be treated as strategic assets rather than as small accounting or legal details.

The common pattern is that these companies do not simply hope the market accepts a higher price.

They operate inside systems that already define how higher revenue can be justified or captured.

## Why This Pattern Matters

This pattern matters because it changes how defensibility should be read.

A company with generic pricing power can still lose that power quickly if demand weakens or competition rises.

A company with formalized price architecture often has a sturdier economic process:

- capital or service need is documented
- the increase mechanism is already recognized
- the next revenue step is more procedural than improvisational

That does not eliminate risk.

But it changes the quality of the revenue stream.

## Consumer And Cultural Meaning

These packets also support a broader social reading.

- households increasingly live inside systems where essential-service pricing is mediated through public approval rather than open-market choice
- digital life depends on narrow control layers whose fee rights are often invisible to end users
- AI and connectivity demand are pushing more value toward landlords and infrastructure operators whose contracts can absorb scarcity through renewal or escalator structures

The larger societal point is that many important price increases now happen inside rules systems, not only inside ordinary retail behavior.

## Industrial And Operating Pressures

This pattern is valuable because it stabilizes monetization, but it never removes burden.

- American Water and Exelon still need regulatory approval and large capital execution.
- VeriSign still depends on governance legitimacy and continued relevance of the domain layer.
- Digital Realty still needs power, capital, and development conversion.
- SBA still depends on carrier budgets, tenant health, and land control.

The larger lesson is that formalized revenue architecture can improve earnings visibility, but only if the physical, contractual, and regulatory system underneath it keeps functioning.

## Disconfirming Evidence And Limits

This pattern would weaken if:

- regulators turn less constructive and slow recovery enough to break the capital-growth logic
- lease-rate resets stop converting scarcity into actual rent growth
- long leases and escalators fail to offset churn, concentration, or funding weakness
- narrow fee rights lose legitimacy or face political or contractual pushback

There is also a conceptual limit:

formalized monetization is not the same as frictionless monetization.

Sometimes the approval process is slow, politically constrained, or economically overwhelmed by the burden it is meant to recover.
