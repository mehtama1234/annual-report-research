# Healthcare, Aging, and Owner Cash: From Repeated Need to Economic Capture

Date: `2026-09-14`

## The question

An aging population and rising chronic-care needs create more healthcare
activity. That does not identify the best business or the cleanest investment.
The activity can be captured by a hospital, home-care operator, diagnostic
workflow, equipment supplier, drug distributor, payer, or property owner.
Each layer receives a different payment and carries a different cost.

The correct chain is:

`patient need -> care setting -> workflow control -> payment and collection ->
labor/property/product burden -> maintenance and growth capital -> shareholder
cash per diluted share`

This page combines the archive's outside-hospital, medical-instruments,
healthcare-distribution, payer/provider, and aging-property work. It is a
forensic synthesis, not a recommendation or a claim that every company below
has the same quality.

## Plain-language definitions

- **Repeated need:** treatment, monitoring, supplies, testing, or support that
  occurs again after the first episode.
- **Workflow control:** a position through which patients, clinicians,
  suppliers, or payers repeatedly route activity.
- **Economic capture:** the part of collected payment left after the costs
  required to keep the service available and the relationship trusted.
- **Owner cash:** cash remaining after ordinary working capital, maintenance
  investment, required technology or quality spending, acquisitions treated as
  part of the growth model, financing claims, and dilution are considered.

These definitions prevent a common error: treating more patients, more sales,
more members, or more revenue as proof of better shareholder economics.

## The system has six different layers

| Layer | Worked companies | What customers are buying | Main burden carried |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Care facilities and direct treatment | HCA, DaVita, Option Care, Brookdale | Staffed capacity, treatment cadence, clinical service, local access, and supported living | Clinical or care labor, facilities, supplies, payer or resident mix, quality, regulation, and direct execution |
| Home and aging-in-place support | Addus, AdaptHealth, Brookdale | Caregiver hours, hospice/home-health visits, equipment, documentation, resupply, and residential support | Labor availability, reimbursement or affordability, scheduling, delivery, property operations, and service quality |
| Diagnostics and procedure tools | Quest, Abbott, Intuitive Surgical, Stryker, Thermo Fisher | Tests, monitoring, instruments, devices, procedure platforms, consumables, and evidence | Reimbursement or customer budgets, clinical adoption, product quality, R&D, inventory, and replacement capital |
| Product-flow and practice access | McKesson, Cencora, Cardinal Health, Henry Schein | Medicine, specialty-product, medical-supply, and practitioner procurement continuity | Thin spreads, inventory, receivables, vendor terms, customer credit, regulation, acquisitions, and debt |
| Payer and care coordination | UnitedHealth, Cigna | Premium administration, claims, networks, pharmacy, benefits, and care coordination | Medical-cost trend, claims timing, reserve changes, regulation, cyber risk, trust, and required capital |
| Healthcare property | Ventas, Welltower, Brookdale | Communities, beds, senior-housing capacity, and physical setting | Occupancy, operator health, labor at the property, renewal capital, financing, and resident affordability |

The layers can benefit from the same demographic trend while producing very
different returns. The party with the largest revenue may retain the smallest
percentage of payment. The party with the highest margin may carry scientific,
regulatory, or product-replacement risk. The party with the most recurring
demand may carry the most labor.

## A common bridge across the six healthcare layers

The companies cannot be put into one P/E or free-cash-flow ranking, but they
can be tested in the same order. The table below makes the denominator and the
claim ahead of common owners explicit.

The bridge shows why repeated need is not a sufficient investment conclusion.
HCA and Addus must pay people at the time care is delivered. Intuitive must
turn placements into recurring procedure use. McKesson must finance product
flow before customers and suppliers settle. UnitedHealth's reported cash
includes a large medical-cost payable movement. Welltower's FFO excludes the
physical renewal and operator claims needed to preserve the property income.

The cleanest comparison is therefore not “which company has the highest
margin?” It is “which company converts its control point into per-share cash
after the burden that cannot be outsourced or ignored?” That question is
portable across the cohort without pretending the denominators are identical.

The HCA dossier and scenario register now make the provider denominator
explicit: consolidated operating cash is reduced for PP&E spending, hospital
acquisitions, and noncontrolling-interest distributions before the valuation
screen is applied. This keeps HCA's payment-policy and collection timing
exposure visible beside its admissions and staffed-capacity growth.

### Direct-care cohort: staffed hospital throughput versus chronic treatment

[HCA](../deep-company-pages/hca-healthcare-inc.md) and
[DaVita](../deep-company-pages/davita-inc.md) both benefit from repeated medical
need, but the unit of economics is different. HCA routes patients through a
large hospital and outpatient network. DaVita delivers repeated kidney
treatments and is extending the relationship into home and risk-based care.

| Company | Current evidence | Main burden ahead of common cash | What would confirm durability |
|---|---|---|---|
| HCA | FY2025 revenue `$75.600B`, net income `$6.784B`, operating cash flow `$12.636B`, and PP&E spending `$4.944B`; Q2 2026 same-facility admissions rose `2.5%` and equivalent admissions `2.7%` | Clinician and support labor, supplies, collection concessions, policy-dependent supplemental payments, facility renewal, `$46.492B` of debt, acquisitions, and `$827M` of 2025 NCI distributions | Admissions and equivalent admissions convert into collected revenue after payer mix, labor, maintenance capex, interest, NCI, and policy effects; tax and supplemental-payment timing normalizes rather than drives cash |
| DaVita | Q2 2026 revenue `$3.554B`, operating income `$579M`, and operating cash flow `$490M`; Q1 U.S. treatments averaged about `91,650` per day; Q2 risk-based arrangements covered about `64,900` patients and `$5.8B` of annualized medical spend | Clinic labor, technicians, drugs, supplies, facility and equipment costs, reimbursement, quality, home-dialysis support, and medical claims or settlement risk in the risk-based population | Treatment contribution remains strong after labor and maintenance capital, while risk-based medical cost, reserves, quality results, and settlement cash are reported separately from dialysis revenue |

HCA has more facility and collection exposure. Its 2025 operating-cash
increase included a fourth-quarter payment of about `$1.3B` of taxes deferred
after severe weather, so the annual figure needs timing analysis. DaVita has a
more concentrated treatment cadence, but its risk-based population introduces a
different denominator: `$5.8B` of annualized medical spend is exposure and
opportunity, not revenue or free cash.

The common falsifier is activity growth without better shareholder economics.
For HCA, that means admissions rising while payer-adjusted collections,
staffing, concessions, policy payments, or maintenance returns deteriorate. For
DaVita, it means treatments or risk-based patients rising while cost per
treatment, claims, quality events, reserves, or required capital consume the
benefit. The two companies should be compared on cash after their distinct
burdens, not on healthcare demand alone.

### Procedure platforms: installed use versus acquisition-led breadth

[Intuitive Surgical](../deep-company-pages/intuitive-surgical-inc.md) and
[Stryker](../deep-company-pages/stryker-corporation.md) both sit inside hospital
procedure workflows, but their reinvestment profiles are materially different.
Intuitive's 2025 revenue was led by instruments and accessories, while Stryker
used most of its 2025 operating cash after capital expenditure to fund
acquisitions.

| Company | Current evidence | Reinvestment and earnings-quality burden | Next proof of durable owner cash |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intuitive Surgical | FY2025 revenue `$10.065B`: instruments and accessories `$6.019B`, systems `$2.474B`, services `$1.572B`; operating cash flow `$3.031B`, PP&E purchases `$539.8M`; Q1 2026 instruments and accessories growth was `23%` | Inventory used `$1.063B` of cash in 2025; SBC was `$788.2M`; first-half 2026 included `$419.4M` of SBC, `$528.2M` of acquisition/IP/other investing, and material inventory and deferred-tax movements | Procedures per system, instruments per procedure, service revenue, system utilization, inventory turns, contract collections, maintenance versus growth capacity, and cash per diluted share after SBC |
| Stryker | FY2025 net sales `$25.116B`, operating cash flow `$5.044B`, acquisitions `$4.960B`, PP&E capex `$761M`; reported EPS `$8.40` versus adjusted EPS `$13.63`; Q2 2026 sales were about `$6.6B` after a Q1 cyber incident | OCF less acquisitions and capex was approximately negative `$677M`; amortization was `$732M`, stock compensation `$243M`, inventory step-up `$173M`, and goodwill/other impairments `$170M` in 2025; debt financing supported the acquisition program | Acquisition-cohort incremental cash return, organic procedure growth, inventory and implant turns, cyber and quality costs, amortization and integration recurrence, debt, SBC, and diluted cash per share |

Intuitive has stronger evidence of a repeat-use engine because instruments and
accessories supplied roughly 60% of 2025 revenue. That does not make the model
asset-light: inventory, manufacturing capacity, training, service, usage-based
leases, and SBC remain real claims. Stryker has a broad and valuable clinical
workflow, but its 2025 cash profile makes the acquisition question central.
Adjusted EPS cannot substitute for post-deal cash return when acquisition,
amortization, inventory step-up, integration, debt, and stock compensation are
recurring consequences of the strategy.

The common falsifier is a widening gap between procedure growth and cash per
share. For Intuitive, it is installed systems rising without utilization,
instrument pull-through, or acceptable inventory and support returns. For
Stryker, it is reported growth and adjusted EPS rising while acquisition cash,
impairments, cyber remediation, debt, or dilution prevent acquired businesses
from producing returns above the cost of capital.

McKesson currently has the cleaner listed cash screen, but it remains a thin-

gross profit and cash. For McKesson, watch whether receivables, inventory,
payables, rebates, or legal timing provide an increasing share of operating
cash. For Cencora, watch whether specialty growth offsets lower-margin GLP-1
volume and whether LIFO credits, settlement gains, or acquisition adjustments
remain visible rather than being treated as recurring distribution economics.

## Senior-housing cohort: operating recovery, property cash, and financing claims

Brookdale, Welltower, and Ventas participate in the same demographic demand but
make different economic claims. Brookdale operates communities and carries the
staffing and service burden directly. Welltower and Ventas own or finance
properties and increasingly participate in senior-housing operating performance,
but they fund growth through property investment and capital markets.

| Company | Current operating evidence | Starting denominator | Claims that remain ahead of shareholder cash | Next proof |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Brookdale | FY2025 same-community RevPAR up `7.6%`; Q1 2026 occupancy `80.0%`; Q1 adjusted EBITDA `$112.2M`; FY2025 OCF `$218.0M` less `$201.5M` capex and `$311.0M` asset acquisitions | Resident fees and community operating income, with a FY2025 listed-use screen of approximately `$(294.5M)` | Care labor, food, maintenance, leases, debt, compliance, resident affordability, and the gap between adjusted EBITDA and common earnings | Separate maintenance from development capital, reconcile community cash and lease claims, and show whether acquired communities add cash after integration |
| Welltower | Q2 2026 normalized FFO `$1.60`/share; total same-store NOI up `15.5%`; senior-housing operating NOI up `20.5%` | Normalized FFO and property NOI | Recurring property renewal, operator support, `$11B` of 2025 net investment, debt, equity issuance, preferred/NCI claims, and property-value risk | Operator coverage, maintenance capital by property type, acquisition yields, diluted FFO/share, and cap-rate/NAV movement |
| Ventas | Q2 2026 normalized FFO about `$0.97`/share; SHOP same-store cash NOI up about `16%`; YTD investments about `$3.4B` | Normalized FFO per share and segment cash NOI | About `$12.7B` debt, roughly `4.7x` net debt to further adjusted EBITDA, equity forwards for about `34.7M` shares, operator support, and recurring property capital | Investment returns after stabilization, per-share FFO after forward settlements, tenant/operator funding, and dividend coverage after renewal capital |

The operating recovery is real across the group, but it is not one owner-cash
story. Brookdale's improving occupancy can raise fixed-cost absorption while
still leaving the operator exposed to wages, staffing, and building upkeep.
Welltower's stronger NOI and FFO can reflect operating recovery and investment
volume, but the property platform must keep funding communities and operators.
Ventas shows the financing consequence most clearly: strong SHOP NOI can coexist
with a large investment program and new equity that changes the per-share
return.

The falsifier is not simply lower occupancy. It is a widening gap between
occupancy or NOI and the residual after labor, resident affordability,
maintenance, financing, operator support, and dilution. If total FFO rises while
diluted FFO per share, property returns, or operator coverage weaken, the aging
demand thesis is not producing durable shareholder value.

## The causal chain by care setting

### 1. Hospitals and treatment centers

HCA and DaVita own a physical and staffed point of care. Their relationships
can be durable because treatment cannot be postponed indefinitely, but their
costs move with the patient. More volume requires clinicians, supplies,
facilities, scheduling, quality systems, and payer administration.

The key test is collected revenue per case or treatment after labor, supplies,
facility maintenance, bad debt, interest, and required capital. Admissions,
treatments per day, and revenue are activity measures; they are not owner cash.

### 2. Home care and aging in place

Addus and AdaptHealth show two different outside-hospital models. Addus
provides labor hours and supervision. AdaptHealth provides equipment,
documentation, logistics, and resupply. Brookdale adds the residential
operator: it provides a staffed community, housing, support, and care in one
local setting. Moving care away from a hospital may reduce one facility burden,
but it creates scheduling, reimbursement or affordability, delivery, property,
and service-quality requirements.

The home and residential settings are therefore not free cost reductions. They
change the cost location. Addus carries caregiver availability; AdaptHealth
carries equipment and delivery flow; Brookdale carries buildings and community
labor. The analyst should ask whether each company controls a recurring
relationship or only processes reimbursable or resident volume with limited
pricing power.

### Home-care cohort: paid labor versus equipment and resupply

[Addus](../deep-company-pages/addus-homecare-corporation.md) and
[AdaptHealth](../deep-company-pages/adapthealth-corp.md) both benefit from care
moving into the home, but their cash constraints are different. Addus must fill
and supervise paid hours. AdaptHealth must place equipment, deliver supplies,
collect claims, and keep patients eligible for repeat orders.

| Company | Current evidence | What the metric does not prove | Next proof of durable owner cash |
|---|---|---|---|
| Addus | Q2 2026 revenue `$377.4M`, net income `$27.6M`, adjusted EBITDA `$49.2M`, and operating cash flow `$40.0M`; personal care was `78.4%` of service mix | Revenue can rise through reimbursement or rate support while filled hours, caregiver availability, wage cost, travel, and service quality weaken | Authorized hours versus delivered hours, reimbursement per filled hour, caregiver wage and turnover, branch productivity, payer collection, and cash after acquisitions and debt |
| AdaptHealth | FY2025 revenue `$3.2449B`, adjusted EBITDA `$616.7M`, operating cash flow `$601.8M`, and free cash flow `$219.4M`; Q2 2026 revenue `$740.3M` with `15.9%` organic growth, but Q2 included a `$144.2M` pre-tax goodwill write-down and YTD free cash flow was negative `$48.4M` | Patient count and organic revenue can rise while capitated rollout, equipment cost, manufacturer pricing, inventory, delivery, and impairment reduce shareholder cash | Active patients and resupply frequency, paid claims, contribution after delivery and service, capitated utilization and vendor cost, equipment replacement capital, and cash per diluted share |

The difference matters for valuation. Addus is mainly a labor-capacity and
reimbursement test: a payer authorization has value only when a caregiver can be
found, scheduled, paid, and retained at a margin. AdaptHealth is a working-
capital, equipment, and contract-risk test: a referred patient has value only
when the equipment is delivered, the claim is collected, the patient remains
eligible, and repeat supplies cover product and service costs.

The common falsifier is growth without a stronger cash bridge. For Addus, that
means rising revenue with lower filled-hour productivity, higher wage leakage,
or more payer timing. For AdaptHealth, it means rising patient or revenue
counts with weaker resupply, negative free cash flow, capitated losses, vendor
price pressure, or recurring goodwill impairment. The two companies should not
receive the same multiple merely because both serve aging at home.

### 3. Diagnostics and tools

Quest, Abbott, Thermo Fisher, Intuitive, and Stryker sit closer to workflow
tools than to direct daily care. Their economics can be cleaner when the
product is approved, installed, repeatedly used, and difficult to replace.

The burden is still real: clinical evidence, regulatory review, product
quality, inventory, training, field support, R&D, customer capital budgets, and
replacement cycles. Intuitive's instruments per installed system are more
informative than placements alone. Abbott's organic segment growth is more
informative than acquired reported sales. Quest's requisition and revenue
quality matter more than adjusted EPS by itself.

### 4. Distribution and practice access

McKesson, Cencora, Cardinal, and Henry Schein ensure that drugs, specialty
products, and medical supplies reach pharmacies, practices, and health systems.
Their control point is continuity and access, not necessarily high retained
margin.

Distribution requires a large balance sheet. Inventory and receivables can
grow before cash is collected, while supplier payables can make one period
look unusually strong. A distributor's correct cash measure is after
working-capital growth, acquisitions that are part of the strategy, debt, and
legal or regulatory cash costs.

### 5. Payers and property owners

UnitedHealth and Cigna coordinate payment and benefits. Ventas owns physical
capacity. Neither is a passive beneficiary. Payers bear claim and medical-cost
risk; property owners bear financing, renewal, operator, and occupancy risk.

For a REIT, normalized FFO is a useful operating denominator but not the final
owner-cash measure. The bridge is:

`normalized FFO -> recurring property renewal -> operator/tenant support ->
debt and preferred claims -> equity issuance -> value per diluted share`

For a payer, the bridge is:

`premium and service revenue -> claims and medical costs -> administrative and
technology cost -> required capital -> shareholder cash`

membership growth as owner cash.

## Who controls the relationship and who carries the burden?

| Relationship | Likely control point | Cleaner part of economics | Burden that can reverse the conclusion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

| Aging in place | Addus caregiver network or AdaptHealth equipment/resupply workflow | Recurring local need and reduced dependence on a hospital visit | Wage inflation, recruitment, documentation, payer denial, delivery cost, adherence |
| Diagnostic information | Quest/Abbott testing and monitoring workflow | Repeated testing, installed instruments, data and consumables | Reimbursement, pricing, quality event, utilization, R&D and replacement |

Welltower's valuation screen is now registered separately as a healthcare-property
case. It uses annualized Q2 2026 normalized FFO only as the starting operating
measure, then tests recurring property renewal, operator support, acquisitions,
debt, preferred claims, cap rates, and diluted per-share value. Strong senior-
housing NOI growth is therefore evidence to investigate, not automatic
distributable cash.

The cleanest economics tend to occur where the company controls a repeated,
necessary workflow while another party funds a meaningful portion of the
physical or labor burden. That advantage narrows when the company must fund the
care setting, scientific replacement, or regulatory response itself.

## Earnings-quality and financial-engineering tests

### Do not confuse an activity denominator with cash

Use the denominator that matches the business:

- treatments per day and collected revenue per treatment for dialysis;
- paid care hours, caregiver retention, reimbursement, and cash per hour for
  home care;
- deliveries, resupply, payer collection, and equipment return for home
  medical equipment;
- requisitions, tests per instrument, procedure volume, instruments per system,
  and service revenue for diagnostics and procedure platforms;
- gross profit, inventory turns, receivables, payables, and debt for
  distribution;
- admissions, case mix, collections, labor cost, and maintenance capex for
  hospitals;
- premium, claims, reserve development, and required capital for payers;
- occupancy, property NOI, recurring capex, debt, and diluted shares for REITs.

### Challenge adjusted measures

Recurring acquisition integration, amortization, restructuring, product holds,
cyber remediation, litigation, quality work, stock compensation, and
capitalized software may be non-cash in one period or separately disclosed.
They can still represent the cost of maintaining the business or buying its
growth. The right question is whether the item recurs as a consequence of the
operating model.

### Normalize working capital and liabilities

Healthcare cash flow can be shifted by medical-cost payables, pharmacy
payables, provider receivables, distributor inventory, product receivables,
patient collections, and deferred revenue. A favorable movement is not
automatically improper, but it must be separated from cash created by the
underlying service.

### Test incentives against shareholder value

Management may be rewarded for revenue, adjusted EPS, admissions, members,
procedures, acquisitions, or buybacks. Compare those measures with cash per
diluted share after SBC, acquired intangibles, debt, required capital, and
claims. Growth that increases total earnings while reducing per-share cash or
balance-sheet resilience is not yet proven value creation.

## Macro and liquidity reading

The durable part of healthcare demand is the need for treatment, monitoring,
medicines, supplies, and support. The fragile part is the funding route.
Reimbursement can change. Hospitals can defer capital purchases. Payers can
change coverage or authorization. Families can resist price increases. Higher
rates increase the cost of hospitals, distributors, REITs, and acquisition
programs. Labor scarcity can reduce the amount of care that can actually be
delivered.

This is the Alden-style distinction between durable need and durable cash.
Durable need is not enough if the business cannot fund inventory, labor,
quality, replacement R&D, property renewal, or required capital through a
liquidity shock.

## Valuation discipline

The same multiple should not be applied to all healthcare layers.

- **Direct care:** value collected service cash after labor, supplies,
  facilities, quality, reimbursement, and maintenance capital.
- **Home care:** value recurring hours or resupply after staffing,
  documentation, route density, payer denial, and equipment replacement.
- **Tools and procedure platforms:** value probability-adjusted product and
  procedure cash after R&D, quality, training, inventory, and replacement.
- **Distribution:** value normalized gross-profit cash after working capital,
  acquisitions, debt, legal costs, and customer-credit losses.
- **Payers:** value risk-adjusted common earnings after claims, reserve
  development, required capital, regulation, and trust-repair costs.
- **Healthcare property:** value normalized FFO or NOI after recurring renewal,
  operator support, debt, preferred claims, and dilution.

Damodaran's central test is whether growth earns more than its reinvestment
cost. For every company, estimate the incremental cash invested in people,
inventory, facilities, software, acquisitions, or clinical replacement and
compare it with the durable cash added to common owners.

## Current cohort conclusion

The archive supports five conclusions:

1. Aging and chronic disease create real recurring demand across more settings,
   including homes, alternate-site care, diagnostics, distribution, and senior
   housing.
2. Moving care outside hospitals changes the burden; it does not eliminate it.
   Labor, documentation, logistics, reimbursement, and adherence become more
   important.
3. The cleaner economic positions generally sit in repeated workflows,
   installed tools, and scarce access points, but those positions still need
   evidence of utilization, renewal, quality, and cash conversion.
4. Distributors and direct-care operators can be strategically necessary while
   retaining less economic profit because inventory, labor, reimbursement, and
   physical execution absorb the payment.
5. A healthcare investment thesis is incomplete until it identifies who pays,
   who collects, who must reinvest, who bears the downside, and how much cash
   remains per diluted share.

This is a qualified cohort conclusion, not a sector ranking. The next filing
must decide whether the relationship is becoming more embedded and whether the
cash burden is becoming more manageable.

## Filing-based falsifiers and next checks

The thesis weakens if the next filings show:

- home-care or diagnostic volume growing while reimbursement, collections, or
  service quality deteriorate;
- caregiver, nurse, or technician shortages limiting delivered volume;
- admissions, procedures, or tests rising without better cash after labor and
  maintenance capital;
- distributor revenue growing while gross-profit percentage falls and
  receivables or inventory consume cash;
- payer operating cash relying increasingly on payable or reserve timing while
  medical-cost trend worsens;
- senior-housing NOI rising through concessions or operator support rather than
  durable occupancy and resident pricing;
- procedure-platform growth depending on acquisitions, placements, or price
  concessions without higher utilization and recurring consumables;
- adjusted earnings rising while GAAP earnings, diluted shares, debt, or
  shareholder cash deteriorate; or
- maintenance, quality, remediation, litigation, and replacement spending
  remaining structurally above the returns implied by valuation.

Next checks should be performed from each company's source ledger and current
filing: volume, price, reimbursement, utilization, renewal, labor cost,
inventory, receivables, payables, claims, unbilled services, capex, R&D,
acquisition cash, SBC, debt, diluted shares, and per-share cash after the full
burden stack.
