# Healthcare control points: clinical evidence, workflow access, and owner cash

## Purpose

Healthcare companies can sit at very different points in the same economic
system. Regeneron develops medicines and owns scientific and clinical rights.
Boston Scientific and Intuitive Surgical embed devices in procedures. Abbott
and Johnson & Johnson operate broader portfolios. McKesson, Cencora, Cardinal,
and Henry Schein control distribution and practice access. HCA operates care
facilities. UnitedHealth and Cigna coordinate payment, benefits, pharmacy, and
care services.

The useful comparison is not which company has the largest revenue or the
strongest “defensive” label. It is:

`clinical need -> evidence or access -> treatment workflow -> payment -> cash -> replacement investment -> common owner cash`

Each participant controls a different step and carries a different burden.

## Control-point map

| Model | Companies | Control point | Main burden |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Clinical evidence and biologic franchises | Regeneron | Discovery, clinical evidence, approved labels, IP, and product rights | Trial failure, R&D, manufacturing, reimbursement, patent loss, partner dependence |
| Focused procedure MedTech | Boston Scientific | Physician adoption, hospital approval, device category, repeat procedure use | Clinical evidence, quality, inventory, R&D, acquisitions, regulatory and legal exposure |
| Installed procedure platform | Intuitive Surgical, Stryker | Robotic or implant workflow, training, instruments, hospital relationship | Platform renewal, hospital budgets, quality, acquisitions, service, inventory |
| Diversified medicine and devices | Abbott, Johnson & Johnson | Broad clinical portfolio, diagnostics, devices, drugs, and trust | Multiple product lifecycles, litigation, manufacturing, R&D, acquisitions, quality |
| Healthcare distribution | McKesson, Cencora, Cardinal, Henry Schein | Product availability, specialty access, practice procurement | Thin margins, inventory, receivables, regulation, acquisitions, working capital |
| Care delivery | HCA Healthcare | Local facilities, staffed capacity, service lines, patient routing | Labor, payer mix, uncompensated care, facilities, debt, policy |
| Payer and coordination | UnitedHealth, Cigna | Premium, network, claims, pharmacy, care, and data coordination | Medical cost, provider reimbursement, claims timing, regulation, capital, trust |

The strongest control point is not always the highest-return investment. A
biologic can have very high margins while its future value depends on a small
number of products. A distributor can have a weaker margin but a necessary
relationship, while inventory and working capital absorb most of the cash. A
payer can control payment coordination but carries claims, policy, and trust
risk.

## Clinical evidence and procedure adoption

### Regeneron: current franchise cash must fund replacement

Regeneron reported FY2025 revenue of $14.343B and operating cash flow of
$4.979B. It spent $898M on capital expenditures, $315M on intangible assets,
and $5.850B on R&D. Dupixent and EYLEA remain central, but EYLEA faces
biosimilar competition and the shift to EYLEA HD.

The control point is scientific and clinical, but some commercial control sits
with partners. Sanofi records global Dupixent sales and shares profits with
Regeneron; Bayer records EYLEA sales outside the United States. The cleanest
cash question is whether current products fund the probability-adjusted cost of
the next products before competition reduces the current base.

### Boston Scientific: procedure growth with acquisition burden

Boston Scientific reported FY2025 revenue of $20.074B and operating cash flow
of $4.534B. PP&E and internal-use software used $876M, acquisitions used
$1.593B, and strategic investments and technology acquisitions used another
$254M. The simple residual after PP&E/software and acquisitions was $2.065B.

The company controls focused procedure categories through clinical evidence,
physician adoption, hospital approval, stocking, and repeat use. The cash
question is whether organic growth remains strong when recent acquisitions
reach a full-year comparison and whether the acquired portfolio produces cash
after integration, amortization, quality review, and sales support.

### Intuitive and Stryker: installed workflow versus purchased breadth

Intuitive's robotic procedure platform creates a recurring relationship through
installed systems, instruments, services, training, and surgeon familiarity.
Stryker combines implants, instruments, hospital relationships, and a broad
acquisition-built portfolio. Both can become embedded in procedure workflow,
but the hospital still funds capital budgets and the companies fund product
renewal, quality, inventory, and commercial support.

The difference is lifecycle evidence. Intuitive's recurring instruments and
services can support a platform if procedure volume rises. Stryker's breadth
can improve cross-selling and category access, but its cash bridge must charge
the acquisition spending that makes the breadth possible.

## Distribution and care delivery

### Distribution: necessary access with thin retained economics

McKesson, Cencora, Cardinal, and Henry Schein sit between manufacturers and
providers or practices. Their control point is product availability, logistics,
specialty access, procurement, and sometimes workflow software. Their revenue
can be large while retained margins are thin.

The analyst must track inventory turns, receivables, supplier terms, rebates,
chargebacks, specialty-product concentration, acquisitions, debt, and cash
after working-capital growth. A working-capital release can create strong cash
in one year and reverse when inventory or receivables normalize.

### HCA: facility access with labor and reimbursement burden

HCA controls local hospitals, staffed capacity, service lines, outpatient
routing, and patient access. It carries more direct burden than a distributor:
clinical labor, facility renewal, payer mix, uncompensated care, collection,
technology, debt, and regulatory scrutiny.

The key metric is not admissions alone. It is collected revenue per case after
labor, supplies, payer shortfalls, facility maintenance, interest, and required
capital. A full hospital can still have weak owner economics if staffing costs,
reimbursement, or capital renewal rise faster than collections.

## Payers and benefit coordination

UnitedHealth and Cigna control financial and administrative coordination rather
than the physical treatment itself. Their cash flows can be large because they
receive premiums, manage claims, coordinate pharmacy, and provide services.

UnitedHealth's FY2025 operating cash flow was approximately $19.697B, including
a $5.824B contribution from medical-cost payable movement. It spent about
$3.622B on PP&E and software and $4.509B on acquisitions. Cigna's FY2025
operating cash flow was approximately $9.601B, with cash contributions from
pharmacy/service payables and insurance liabilities.

These payable movements are not automatically improper or non-recurring. They
are part of the operating model. But the analyst must distinguish cash earned
from claims timing, reserve development, premium growth, retained pharmacy
margin, provider reimbursement, and required insurance capital.

The payer controls payment coordination while providers and patients bear much
of the care-delivery friction. The payer still bears medical-cost trend,
regulatory, cyber, trust, and capital risk. Reported scale does not remove the
need to test the claims denominator.

The table is not a ranking. Its purpose is to prevent one healthcare measure
from substituting for another. Revenue, procedures, members, prescriptions,
product sales, and operating cash have different claims attached to them.

### Intuitive, Stryker, and Abbott: three forms of procedure and care control

Intuitive, Stryker, and Abbott all benefit from clinical trust and repeated use,
but they control different parts of the healthcare workflow. Intuitive's system
connects a capital placement to surgeon training, procedures, instruments, and
service. Stryker combines implants, instruments, robotics, and hospital sales
with a much more acquisition-dependent portfolio. Abbott spreads diagnostics,
monitoring, cardiovascular devices, diabetes care, nutrition, and
pharmaceuticals across several product lifecycles.

The 2025 cash evidence shows why the comparison needs more than margin. Intuitive
reported `$3.031B` of operating cash flow and `$539.8M` of PP&E purchases, but
inventory used about `$1.063B` of cash and share-based compensation was about
`$788M`. Stryker reported `$5.044B` of operating cash flow, but acquisitions
used `$4.960B` and PP&E used `$761M`, making cash after those uses negative
before other claims. Abbott reported about `$9.566B` of operating cash flow,
with `$2.171B` of PP&E and about `$105M` of acquisitions, but its diversified
portfolio introduces multiple product-replacement and quality tests.

Intuitive therefore has the clearest installed-procedure pull-through, Stryker
has broader purchased access with the largest acquisition-return burden, and
Abbott has the broadest diversification with more varied lifecycle and
regulatory exposures. These are working conclusions, not rankings. The filing
tests are: procedures and instruments per system for Intuitive; acquisition
cohort cash, amortization, inventory step-up, and procedure adoption for Stryker;
and product-level growth, reimbursement, quality, R&D, and replacement cash for
Abbott.

## Who captures the cleaner economics?

1. **Clinical evidence businesses** can capture high margins when a product is
   differentiated, but they fund scientific failure and must replace products
   before exclusivity ends.
2. **Procedure platforms** can create repeat use and switching friction, but
   hospitals, quality systems, and acquisition programs determine how much of
   the value reaches shareholders.
3. **Distributors** control necessary access but operate with thin spreads and
   large working-capital requirements. Their social importance does not imply
   high incremental returns.
4. **Providers** control physical access and patient throughput but carry the
   heaviest labor, facility, reimbursement, and regulatory burden.
5. **Payers** control the payment and coordination layer and can earn strong
   returns on scale, but medical-cost, claims, policy, capital, cyber, and trust
   risks remain on their balance sheets.

The cleanest economics usually occur where a company controls a repeated
workflow or scarce evidence while another participant supplies much of the
capital. That advantage narrows when the company must fund the physical,
scientific, or regulatory burden itself.

## Earnings-quality tests across the cohort

### Product or service activity versus owner cash

For every model, reconcile the operating metric to collected cash. A product
franchise needs price, volume, and remaining life. A procedure platform needs
units, utilization, and hospital economics. A distributor needs inventory and
receivables. A provider needs collections after labor and facility renewal. A
payer needs claims and reserve development.

### “One-time” costs

Amortization, restructuring, acquisition integration, IPR&D, product recalls,
cyber costs, litigation, and special items should be tracked over multiple
years. A cost can be non-cash in the current period and still represent a real
capital allocation decision. A recurring acquisition strategy can make
acquisition charges a normal cost of growth.

### Working capital and liabilities

Medical-cost payables, pharmacy payables, claims reserves, distributor
inventory, product receivables, deferred revenue, and collaboration settlements
can all move cash across periods. The analyst must ask whether the cash is
earned, timing-supported, growth-funded, or claim-bound.

### Incentives and dilution

Management may be rewarded for adjusted EPS, sales, procedure volume, members,
product approvals, acquisitions, or buybacks. Compare those measures with
common cash per diluted share. SBC, acquired intangible amortization, debt,
buyback timing, and partner claims should remain visible.

## Macro and durability

Medical need provides a durable baseline, but liquidity still matters. Patients
need care while hospitals can defer procedures, payers can change coverage,
providers can reduce staffing, and governments can change reimbursement.
Higher rates lower the value of distant pipeline cash and increase the cost of
hospital, provider, distributor, and payer balance sheets.

Regulated demand is not risk-free demand. The company may face price controls,
clinical evidence requirements, safety rules, privacy, cybersecurity,
reimbursement changes, and public scrutiny. Durability means surviving these
changes while funding the next required investment.

## Valuation discipline

Use the correct denominator for each model:

- Regeneron: probability-adjusted product cash after R&D and manufacturing.
- Boston Scientific and Intuitive: procedure cash after product renewal,
  quality, inventory, and platform reinvestment.
- Distributors: cash after working-capital growth, acquisitions, and debt.
- HCA: collected case cash after labor, supplies, facilities, and interest.
- UnitedHealth and Cigna: risk-adjusted cash after claims, payable timing,
  required capital, policy, and cyber costs.

Do not apply a single “defensive healthcare” multiple to all of them. A high
multiple requires durable control, a credible replacement schedule, and a
cash burden that remains manageable when reimbursement, rates, or utilization
change.

## Cross-company watchlist

| Model | Confirming evidence | Falsifying evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Biotech | New approvals, durable product price/volume, strong partner profit share | Product concentration, biosimilar erosion, R&D without replacement cash |
| Procedure MedTech | Organic units, adoption, hospital approvals, stable quality and margin | Acquisition-dependent growth, recalls, price concessions, rising inventory |
| Distribution | Stable turns, collections, supplier economics, post-acquisition cash | Thin margins, receivable or inventory growth, debt-funded acquisitions |
| Provider | Volume and case mix convert to collections after labor and capex | Staffing cost, payer shortfalls, facility burden, weak cash per case |
| Payer | Premium and service growth exceed medical and pharmacy cost | Claims trend, payable reversal, policy/cyber costs, required-capital pressure |
