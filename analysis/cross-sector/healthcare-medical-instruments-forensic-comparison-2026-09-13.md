# Healthcare medical-instruments lane: forensic comparison

Date baseline: 2026-08-10; latest evidence added through Q2 2026.

This is a packet-backed comparison, not a financial signoff or a valuation
recommendation. It applies the lane question through three complementary
angles: Alden's durability and macro-burden lens, Damodaran's reinvestment and
value lens, and a forensic review of earnings quality, cash conversion, and
management presentation.

## The lane question

Which healthcare middle-layer businesses convert institutional dependence into
durable economic profit, and which are merely benefiting from procedure,
capital-budget, mix, or recovery timing?

| Company | Control point | Cleaner economic feature | Burden or fragility |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| McKesson | Drug, specialty, oncology, and provider-workflow distribution | Essential channel continuity plus specialty and technology services | Thin distribution spreads, working capital, customer credit, litigation, and reimbursement exposure |
| Cencora | Global drug distribution, specialty access, and physician services | Scale, specialty logistics, and provider/manufacturer embedment | Acquisition intensity, payable timing, GLP-1 mix, litigation, debt, and customer concentration |
| Cardinal Health | Pharma, medical-product, at-home, and logistics distribution | Institutional continuity plus broader service attachment | Contract churn, working capital, $5.3B acquisition program, tariffs, litigation, and debt |
| Baxter | IV, infusion, patient-support, and hospital supply workflow | Recurring institutional replenishment and installed equipment | Product holds, inventory normalization, impairments, and portfolio repair |
| Abbott | Diagnostics, cardiovascular devices, diabetes monitoring, nutrition, and established medicines | Broad installed clinical workflows plus recurring tests, sensors, and procedure products | Exact Sciences acquisition, $20B acquisition debt, large amortization, reimbursement, quality, and portfolio-mix risk |
| Henry Schein | Practitioner procurement, distribution, and practice workflow | Dense replenishment network plus specialty, technology, and corporate-brand attachment | Distribution margins, supplier/customer leverage, equipment cyclicality, and integration |
| Intuitive Surgical | Robotic procedure platform and installed base | Procedure-linked instruments, accessories, service, training, and switching costs | Hospital utilization, placement economics, procedure growth, and high expectations |
| Stryker | Procedure-room equipment, implants, robotics, and surgeon workflow | Category breadth and installed procedural platforms | Hospital capital budgets, acquisitions, product execution, and cyber disruption |
| HCA Healthcare | Hospital access, staffed capacity, procedure throughput, and payer collection | Direct capture of facility and procedure economics | Labor, payer mix, implicit price concessions, maintenance capex, debt, and noncontrolling interests |

## What the evidence currently supports

The strongest common finding is not simply “healthcare is defensive.” These
companies sit at different points in a dependency chain. Baxter and Henry
Schein monetize repeated supply flow; Intuitive monetizes use of a platform;
Stryker monetizes procedure-room adoption, implants, and capital equipment.
The closer the company is to an installed workflow, recurring consumables, or
switching costs, the more durable the relationship appears—but durability does
not remove the need to fund inventory, train users, maintain equipment, absorb
quality failures, or win hospital budgets.

The latest packets show strong reported momentum:

- Baxter reported Q2 2026 sales of about $2.96B, up 5%, with $181M of quarterly
  free cash flow, but the packet also records higher-cost inventory from late
  2025 and the prior Novum IQ shipment/install hold.
- Henry Schein reported Q2 sales of $3.5B, up 6.7%, with adjusted EBITDA of
  $288M; specialty products and technology grew faster than core medical
  distribution, supporting management's claim that the model is moving beyond
  low-margin box movement.
- Intuitive reported Q2 revenue of $2.89B and GAAP net income of $818M. Its
  Q1 instruments-and-accessories growth of 23%, alongside more than 11,100 da
  Vinci systems installed at 2025 year-end, is stronger evidence of platform
  utilization than system-placement growth alone.
- Stryker reported Q2 sales of $6.6B and adjusted EPS of $3.69 after a Q1
  cyber-incident disruption. The rebound is encouraging, but it also proves
  that operational continuity is part of the economic asset.

These facts support a durable-demand hypothesis. They do not yet prove durable
shareholder returns, because the packets do not by themselves reconcile full
cash economics, acquisition returns, maintenance capital, or current price.

## Procedure pull-through: platform utilization versus provider throughput

Intuitive, Stryker, and HCA sit in the same clinical chain but collect money at
different points. Intuitive earns when a hospital's installed robotic system is
used and instruments, accessories, and service are consumed. Stryker earns from
implants, equipment, instruments, and acquired procedure platforms; its cash
burden includes a large acquisition program. HCA earns the facility residual
after the procedure is staffed, paid for, and collected, so it carries the
largest direct labor, reimbursement, facility, and debt exposure.

| 2025 filed screen | Intuitive Surgical | Stryker | HCA Healthcare |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Revenue | $10.065B | $25.116B | $75.600B |
| Operating cash flow | $3.031B | $5.044B | $12.636B |
| Visible PP&E capex | $540M | $761M | $4.944B |
| Acquisitions | $13.9M of acquisitions, intellectual property, and other investing | $4.960B | $397M |
| Simple OCF less PP&E capex and stated acquisitions | Approximately $2.477B before other claims | Approximately negative $677M | Approximately $7.295B before NCI distributions, interest, taxes, and other claims |
| What must be proven next | Procedures per system and instruments/service per procedure | Organic procedure growth and post-deal incremental cash return | Collected reimbursement and staffed capacity after labor and maintenance capital |

The table is deliberately not a ranking. Intuitive's approximately $2.477B
screen is the cleanest first-pass conversion because its recurring instruments
and service revenue are linked to an installed platform, but inventory, stock
compensation, R&D, and hospital utilization remain claims. Stryker's negative
screen does not prove weak clinical economics; it shows that FY2025 growth was
funded through a major acquisition cycle and must be judged by future
incremental cash. HCA's $7.295B screen is large because it operates the care
sites, but the common-owner bridge still has to subtract $827M of
noncontrolling-interest distributions, $2.207B of interest, taxes, debt needs,
and the maintenance portion of its $4.944B capital program.

This separates three commonly confused statements:

1. A procedure can be clinically needed without being profitable for the
   hospital or device supplier.
2. A device can be placed without being used at a level that earns the hospital
   an adequate return.
3. Hospital revenue can rise without common-owner cash improving if payer
   concessions, labor, interest, and facility renewal absorb the increase.

The next filings should therefore be read as a linked set. For Intuitive,
follow installed systems to procedures, instruments, service, inventory, and
cash. For Stryker, separate organic procedure demand from acquired revenue and
test acquisition cohorts after amortization, integration, stock compensation,
and capex. For HCA, follow admissions and equivalent admissions to collected
revenue, staffing, service-line margin, maintenance versus growth capex, and
cash attributable to HCA shareholders.

### Abbott: broad clinical control with a newly leveraged acquisition layer

Abbott’s Q2 2026 sales grew `13.0%` on a reported basis but only `4.8%` on a
comparable basis. Medical Devices grew `7.9%` excluding currency, which is
strong evidence of existing-platform execution. Diagnostics grew sharply, but
Exact Sciences was acquired on March 23 and contributed `$1.0B` of first-half
sales while its earnings contribution was not material to Abbott’s consolidated
net earnings.

The cash bridge changes the interpretation. Six-month operating cash was
`$3.803B` and capex `$896M`, but Abbott spent `$19.962B` on acquisitions,
issued `$19.827B` of long-term debt, and paid `$3.425B` in dividends and
repurchases. Goodwill rose by `$11.4B`, and estimated 2026 acquired-intangible
amortization is `$2.6B`. Abbott therefore controls several valuable clinical
workflows, but its current growth must be separated into organic Medical Devices
execution and a highly leveraged Exact Sciences integration program.

The Q2 GAAP EPS of `$0.53` versus adjusted EPS of `$1.31` is a direct test for
the lane. The spread may contain legitimate acquisition and accounting effects,
but recurring amortization, integration, litigation, quality, and financing
costs cannot be treated as irrelevant to common-owner cash merely because they
are excluded from adjusted EPS.

### McKesson: scale is a control point, but cash is tied to the flow

McKesson adds the drug-distribution counterpoint to Baxter's hospital-supply
workflow. FY2025 revenue was $359.1B, of which approximately 99% came from
distribution and retail activity rather than services. Operating cash flow was
$6.085B, but the filing shows the increase was materially affected by a $3.7B
accounts-payable cash contribution, while receivables used $3.935B and
inventory used $2.270B. This is not evidence of manipulation; it is evidence
that the cash engine must be normalized for vendor scheduling, growth inventory,
specialty receivables, and customer credit.

The control point is channel continuity and specialty complexity. The burden
carrier is McKesson's balance sheet, compliance organization, legal reserves,
inventory, receivables, and thin gross spreads. The thesis strengthens if
specialty/technology mix improves cash returns without disproportionate working
capital; it weakens if revenue and adjusted EPS rise while receivables,
inventory, litigation, or vendor-financing dependence absorb the cash.

### Cencora: the specialty upgrade is funded by a negative growth residual

Cencora is the closest peer comparison. FY2025 operating cash flow was
$3.875B, but accounts payable supplied $3.7B while receivables used $1.9B and
inventory used $1.3B. Cencora then spent $668M on capex, $4.096B on acquired
companies, and $196M on equity investments. The post-growth-investment
residual was approximately negative $1.12B before dividends and buybacks. The
$3.9B Retina Consultants of America acquisition may deepen physician and
specialty control, but it must earn a cash return after integration, physician
retention, working capital, amortization, and debt.

McKesson's cleaner current screen is therefore not automatically a better
business: Cencora may have a stronger specialty growth runway, but its 2025
cash bridge carries much more acquisition and litigation burden. The next
filings should compare DSO/DIO/DPO, gross-profit margin, specialty mix, legal
cash, acquisition cohorts, and debt-funded capital returns on a like-period
basis.

### Cardinal Health: mixed-channel breadth, but acquisition cash is negative

Cardinal broadens the peer set beyond pure pharmaceutical distribution. FY2025
operating cash flow was $2.397B, capex was $547M, and acquisitions were about
$5.3B. Cardinal also reported a $2.732B accounts-payable contribution, a
$1.816B inventory use, and an $833M receivables use. The post-capex,
post-acquisition residual was approximately negative $3.45B before dividends
and buybacks. That does not invalidate the service-attachment strategy, but it
means the clean economics depend on acquisition returns, contract replacement,
working-capital normalization, and debt funding.

Across the three distributors, McKesson currently shows the cleanest listed
post-investment residual, Cencora the strongest specialty-acquisition tension,
and Cardinal the broadest mixed-channel exposure. None should be judged on
revenue or adjusted EPS without a like-period gross-profit, working-capital,
legal-cash, and acquisition-cohort bridge.

## Earnings-quality and shenanigan review

### Baxter: reset costs may be real, but “adjusted” recovery needs a bridge

The 2025 continuing-operations loss was $900M, or $(1.75) per diluted share,
with more than $2B of special-item drag. Q4 included a $485M goodwill
impairment and a $330M U.S. deferred-tax-asset valuation allowance. These are
not automatically shenanigans: impairments can be economically informative.
The forensic question is whether the recurring business earns an acceptable
return after recurring quality, remediation, product-exit, and working-capital
costs are restored to the model rather than repeatedly excluded as “special.”

Required next check: reconcile GAAP operating cash flow and free cash flow to
adjusted earnings across 2025-2026, separating inventory release/build,
recall/remediation, divestiture proceeds, restructuring cash, and ordinary
maintenance capital. The full FY2025 bridge is $951M of continuing OCF less
$513M of capex and $9M of developed-technology/investment spend, or roughly
$429M before financing. Baxter still carried $9.48B of debt and finance-lease
obligations, so a clean Q2 cash number is not enough while inventory cost drag,
quality remediation, and portfolio reset are still moving through results.

### Henry Schein: mix and add-backs must earn their premium

2025 GAAP diluted EPS was $3.27 versus non-GAAP diluted EPS of $4.97, while
adjusted EBITDA was $1.101B. The gap is not proof of manipulation, but it is a
material investigation target. The business is explicitly trying to move toward
specialty products, technology, corporate brands, and value-added services.

Required next check: classify every recurring adjustment, then test whether
reported gross margin, operating income, receivables, inventory, and operating
cash flow improve with the claimed mix shift. The thesis weakens if the higher
value layer is mostly acquisition accounting, repeated restructuring, or
non-cash compensation while distribution working capital absorbs the cash.

Henry Schein’s FY2025 bridge makes the issue concrete: $712M of OCF less $139M
of property capex, $52M of capitalized software, and $199M of acquisitions or
equity investments left roughly $322M before financing, while the company
repurchased about $850M of stock. The $105M restructuring charge is part of a
plan expected to continue through 2027. The provider-workflow moat is
credible, but the “higher value” layer has not yet demonstrated that it funds
its own reinvestment and capital returns.

### Intuitive Surgical: high-quality recurring revenue still has a placement trap

Intuitive's platform economics are the cleanest in this set because instruments
and accessories rise with procedures. But a system placement can be mistaken
for economic value creation if the hospital does not achieve adequate utilization
or if the vendor subsidizes adoption through financing, discounts, or bundled
service commitments.

Required next check: follow system placements into installed-base utilization,
procedure growth, instrument revenue per system, service revenue, gross margin,
capitalized development, and free cash flow. The key Damodaran question is not
whether revenue grows; it is whether incremental platform investment earns more
than its opportunity cost after training, support, and hospital capacity are
included.

### Stryker: recovery can conceal the cost of resilience

Stryker's Q1 disruption and Q2 rebound make the quality-of-earnings question
particularly concrete. A recovery in sales and adjusted EPS can reflect delayed
procedures and shipment normalization rather than a new structural growth rate.
The annual packet also shows a meaningful acquisition and capital-product
dependence.

Required next check: separate deferred demand from ongoing procedure growth;
reconcile reported and adjusted EPS; inspect acquisition-related amortization,
integration costs, stock compensation, inventory, receivables, and capex; and
test whether post-incident margins normalize without sacrificing service or
inventory availability.

Stryker is the healthcare lane's clearest acquisition-intensity contradiction.
FY2025 OCF was $5.044B, but acquisitions consumed $4.960B and PP&E capex
consumed $761M. The resulting negative post-acquisition residual contrasts with
the strong procedure-platform narrative. Its moat may be genuine, but the
economic return must be measured on the capital actually deployed to buy,
integrate, support, and defend the platform.

## Alden / Damodaran / forensic synthesis

The Alden-style resilience read favors Baxter and Henry Schein for routine,
distributed healthcare demand, but their resilience is operational rather than
magical: supply interruptions and provider budgets can transmit quickly. The
Damodaran read favors Intuitive's platform and recurring-use structure, while
warning that a great business can still be a poor investment if placement,
growth, or terminal assumptions are already priced in. Stryker sits between the
two: a broad, high-quality procedural platform with more visible exposure to
capital cycles and execution events.

The forensic read prevents the lane from becoming a quality ranking. The main
risks are:

1. recurring “one-time” charges that are actually part of the operating model;
2. adjusted EPS growth outrunning GAAP earnings or free cash flow;
3. working-capital release being mistaken for sustainable cash generation;
4. acquisitions and capitalized platform investment hiding weak marginal returns;
5. procedure recovery or hospital budget timing being mistaken for durable
   demand; and
6. installed-base claims being accepted without utilization and customer-return
   evidence.

## Current conclusion and falsifiers

The lane supports a nuanced conclusion: the cleanest healthcare economics may
sit in embedded workflow and recurring-use platforms, not necessarily in the
largest provider or distributor. However, the cleaner layer is only real when
the company can convert institutional dependence into cash after quality,
service, inventory, training, maintenance, and capital costs.

The conclusion would weaken if the next filings show:

- recurring-use or specialty growth without corresponding cash conversion;
- receivables or inventory growing faster than demand;
- persistent adjusted-to-GAAP gaps with recurring exclusions;
- system placements without rising utilization or consumables per system;
- procedure recovery reversing when the comparison period normalizes; or
- capital and remediation spending remaining structurally above the returns
  implied by management's growth narrative.

Henry Schein adds a distribution-specific falsifier: specialty/technology
growth can be real while shareholder economics deteriorate if internal growth
does not improve cash conversion after restructuring, acquisitions, software,
inventory, debt, and buybacks.

The Stryker case adds a specific falsifier: adjusted EPS and procedure growth
can remain strong while shareholder economics weaken if acquisition cash,
amortization, stock compensation, and cyber/quality investment compound faster
than incremental post-deal cash flow.
