# Healthcare middle layers: provider networks, product flow, and retained cash

Research date: `2026-09-15`

## The comparison question

Healthcare demand reaches patients through several middle layers. A company
may move medicine, place home equipment, process payment, manage a provider
network, or accept delegated medical risk. These businesses can all report
large revenue and recurring demand, but they do not carry the same obligations.

The question is: who controls the relationship, who carries the cost of care or
inventory, and what remains after the obligations are paid?

## Four different economic positions

| Company / position | What it controls | Main burden before shareholder cash | Critical filing test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Astrana Health | Provider networks, delegated-risk relationships, care delivery, and enablement workflow | Medical-cost variance, provider payments, clinical labor, reimbursement, integration, and required capital | Revenue and retained margin per patient; claims/reserve development; subsidiary capital; organic cash |
| Henry Schein | Practitioner procurement and distribution of healthcare products | Inventory, receivables, supplier terms, distribution labor, acquisitions, and thin product spreads | Gross-profit spread, inventory turns, working capital, acquisitions, and cash per share |
| AdaptHealth | Home medical equipment placement and recurring resupply | Claims collection, equipment deployment, resupply logistics, labor, debt, and payer policy | Patient volume, reimbursement, resupply retention, receivables, debt, and cash after equipment capital |
| UnitedHealth / Cigna | Payment, benefits, pharmacy services, and care coordination | Claims, reserves, medical-cost trend, required capital, regulation, debt, and pharmacy economics | Medical-cost trend, reserve development, risk adjustment, capital, and shareholder cash |

These are not interchangeable “healthcare growth” companies. Astrana's revenue
can be close to the risk-bearing and provider-management layer. Henry Schein's
revenue includes a large amount of product flow. AdaptHealth must place and
service equipment and collect payer claims. Cigna and UnitedHealth coordinate
premiums, claims, pharmacy services, and capital. The same aging or chronic-care
demand can reach each company through a different cash path.

## What Astrana adds

The Astrana packet reports FY2025 revenue of `$3.1818B`, adjusted EBITDA of
`$205.4M`, and free cash flow of `$104.5M`. Q2 2026 revenue reached `$972.5M`
and adjusted EBITDA `$68.9M`; six-month free cash flow reached `$92.9M`.
The company reported more than `20,000 providers` and over `1.6 million
patients` in value-based arrangements.

Those figures show scale, but also identify the missing bridge. Adjusted EBITDA
was roughly twice FY2025 free cash flow. The difference may include normal
investment, working capital, acquisition and integration costs, stock
compensation, or obligations attached to risk-bearing contracts. The comparison
cannot call Astrana a cleaner economic model until those differences are
reconciled.

## Control and burden are linked

Provider-network control can make the company valuable because it coordinates
physicians, payer contracts, care decisions, and workflow. But accepting more
control can also increase the company's exposure to medical-cost variance and
required capital.

Product distribution can look less strategically central, yet a distributor
may have a cleaner cash claim when it earns a spread without taking medical
risk. That spread is still vulnerable to working capital, supplier terms,
acquisitions, and litigation.

Home equipment can produce recurring resupply revenue, but the company must
place equipment, collect claims, maintain logistics, and comply with payer
rules. A recurring order is not a recurring shareholder return unless the
equipment and collection costs are included.

Payers control payment and benefit workflow, but they stand ahead of
shareholders in claims and reserve obligations. Scale does not remove the need
for capital or make medical-cost trend irrelevant.

## What should be compared in the next filing cycle

1. Revenue per patient, member, provider, prescription, or equipment unit,
   depending on the model.
2. Gross or retained margin after the obligation specific to the company:
   claims, product cost, equipment, or clinical labor.
3. Receivables, contract assets, inventory, claims payable, reserves, and
   supplier funding.
4. Acquisition spending, goodwill, integration cost, and stock issuance.
5. Required capital at subsidiaries or regulated entities.
6. Diluted share count and per-share cash after buybacks or equity-funded
   growth.

The correct conclusion is not that one healthcare middle layer always wins.
The conclusion is that healthcare demand must be followed through its actual
contract and obligation structure before revenue growth is treated as durable
shareholder cash.
