# Hotel relationship control versus property-owner cash

Date: 2026-09-13

## The question

Marriott and Host participate in the same lodging economy but own different
parts of it. Marriott owns the brand, reservation system, loyalty relationship,
and distribution to hotel owners. Host owns the buildings and receives the
remaining property cash after paying hotel operating costs and funding renewal.

The comparison tests four claims:

1. whether Marriott's lower property ownership produces cleaner cash;
2. whether Host's scarce locations compensate for its heavier capital burden;
3. whether either company is presenting an incomplete earnings measure; and
4. what the next filings must show before the valuation cases deserve more
   confidence.

## Definitions used in this comparison

- **RevPAR** means revenue per available room. It combines room price and the
  percentage of rooms occupied, so it is an operating indicator, not owner cash.
- **Adjusted EBITDA** is a company-defined profit measure before interest, tax,
  depreciation, and amortization, with additional adjustments. It is useful for
  comparing operations but does not pay capital or debt claims by itself.
- **Adjusted FFO** is a real-estate measure that adds back some non-cash property
  depreciation and removes selected gains. It is more useful than GAAP EPS for a
  REIT, but it still does not automatically subtract recurring renewal capital.
- **Renewal capex** is spending required to keep a property operating at its
  existing quality and earning capacity. It is different from expansion
  spending on a new property.
- **Owner cash** here means cash remaining after the recurring operating,
  property, technology, acquisition, financing, and other claims identified in
  the bridge. It is a valuation input, not a standardized accounting measure.

## Control point and burden map

| Company | What it controls | Who supplies the main physical capital | Main economic burden | Cleaner denominator |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Marriott | Brand standards, reservations, loyalty identity, revenue management, owner distribution, and co-brand relationship | Franchisees, managed-property owners, and other hotel owners | Loyalty redemption obligations, technology, brand support, guarantees, owner health, and corporate debt | Fee and co-brand cash after technology, loyalty, guarantees, acquisitions, tax, debt, and dilution |
| Host | Luxury and upper-upscale hotel locations, rooms, property condition, renovation timing, and asset recycling | Host and its operating partnership | Labor, insurance, utilities, hotel operating costs, renewal capex, interest, refinancing, and property-market risk | Property cash after recurring renewal capex, interest, debt claims, and REIT/OP attribution |

The difference is not simply “asset-light versus asset-heavy.” Marriott can
avoid owning most buildings because hotel owners need its demand and operating
system. Host can earn from scarce locations because the building and location
are part of what guests and hotel brands require. The relevant question is
whether each control point earns more than the capital and obligations needed to
protect it.

## What the burden-adjusted comparison actually says

The two residuals answer different questions. Marriott's approximately $2.245B
screen is cash generated after corporate capital and the CitizenM acquisition,
before loyalty obligations that will be served through future hotel stays,
guarantees, debt service, dividends, and repurchases. Host's approximately
$866M screen is cash left after the reported property-capital spend, before
interest, acquisitions, distributions, repurchases, and the long-run average
cost of major renewals. Neither number is a common-owner free-cash-flow figure.

| Test | Marriott | Host | Interpretation |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| OCF less stated capital / technology spend | $2.608B | $866M | Marriott reports the cleaner first-pass conversion because owners fund most hotel buildings; Host has already paid a large property-capital bill, but that bill is uneven across the renovation cycle |
| Stated capital as a share of OCF | 18.8% | 42.6% | The ratio is not a quality score: Marriott's loyalty and guarantee obligations are not comparable to Host's renewal capex, and Host's single-year capex may not equal through-cycle maintenance |
| Main claim still outside the first-pass residual | Loyalty liability, guarantee exposure, contract acquisition, debt, diluted shares | Interest, debt maturities, renewal-cycle capital, REIT/OP claims, distributions | Each model needs a different second bridge before valuation |
| Relevant operating denominator | Fee and co-brand cash after platform obligations | Property cash after normalized renewal capex and financing | Applying the same EBITDA or FFO multiple would mix unlike claims |

The useful conclusion is not that Marriott's residual is “better” because it is
larger. Marriott has transferred most building and hotel-labor capital to
owners, so the analyst must test whether that transfer remains stable: owners
must keep opening, renovating, and paying fees, while Marriott must honor
loyalty and guarantee commitments. Host retains the building economics, so the
analyst must charge the full long-run cost of keeping the rooms competitive and
the balance sheet refinanced. This is why the two companies belong in one
economic comparison but not in one undifferentiated cash-flow model.

### A simple reading order for a new analyst

Start with demand: RevPAR, occupancy, ADR, rooms, and pipeline. Then ask who
funds the rooms and the labor. For Marriott, move from fee revenue to loyalty,
technology, guarantees, debt, and diluted shares. For Host, move from hotel
revenue to wages, insurance, property capital, interest, debt maturities, and
REIT/OP attribution. Only after those claims are visible should the reader
compare cash conversion or valuation.

## Marriott: relationship economics and transferred burden

Marriott's chain is:

`brand and loyalty -> direct demand and reservation distribution -> owner room supply -> franchise, management, licensing, and co-brand fees`

The model is valuable because owners fund most hotel construction, labor, and
property renewal. Marriott still has obligations that the “asset-light” label
can hide:

- The guest-loyalty liability was $7.992B at year-end 2025. Points are earned
  before redemption, so Marriott must estimate breakage, future redemption
  behavior, funding received from properties and card partners, and the cost of
  providing benefits.
- A one-percentage-point change in estimated breakage can change the liability
  by approximately $50M. In 2025, updated estimates reduced revenue and
  increased the liability by approximately $102M. This is a disclosed estimate,
  not evidence of misconduct, but it is a material earnings-quality variable.
- Franchise guarantees can require Marriott to fund obligations when hotel
  cash flow fails specified tests. The fee stream therefore has contingent
  exposure to property-owner distress.
- The pipeline of about 610,000 rooms is future fee capacity, not current fee
  cash. The test is opening, owner financing, fee rate, retention, and owner
  return after property capital.
- Marriott repurchased $3.3B of stock and paid $718M of dividends in 2025.
  Those returns should be evaluated after loyalty, technology, guarantee, debt,
  and contract-acquisition requirements, not only against adjusted EBITDA.

The cleanest economic evidence would be fee growth that remains positive after
adjusting for room additions, stable or improving owner economics, controlled
loyalty cost, and guarantees that remain immaterial relative to fee cash. The
weakest evidence would be pipeline growth accompanied by owner distress,
falling fee rates, rising support obligations, or loyalty costs that consume
the apparent margin advantage.

## Host: property cash and renewal burden

Host's chain is:

`location and hotel asset -> rooms and food-and-beverage capacity -> guest and group demand -> hotel operating cash -> renovation, debt service, and residual property value`

Host's 2025 comparable Total RevPAR grew 4.2%, but food-and-beverage profit
margin fell to 32.4% from 33.5%. This matters because higher room revenue does
not automatically produce higher property cash after wages, benefits,
insurance, utilities, management fees, and renovation disruption.

Host reported $1.510B of OCF and $644M of capital spending. The $866M after-capex
screen is more informative than Adjusted FFO alone, but it still includes
$125M of asset-sale proceeds and $79M of loan-receivable proceeds in the
broader investing cash flow picture, and it precedes distributions and debt
claims. Host paid cash interest of $241M in 2025 and carried approximately
$5.077B of debt.

Host budgeted $525M–$625M of 2026 capital expenditures. A single year's spend
can understate the long-run burden because major hotel renovations occur in
cycles. The correct question is whether average renewal spending per room,
including periodic large renovations, is covered by normalized property cash.

GAAP net income also included $148M of property-sale gains and $17M of
condominium-sale income. These may represent good asset management, but they
should not be capitalized as recurring hotel operating earnings. They belong in
a separate asset-recycling and NAV analysis.

The REIT/operating-partnership structure creates another claim. Host Inc. owns
approximately 99% of Host L.P.; the remaining outside OP-unit interest shares
in the economics. A model using only public shares can overstate common-owner
cash per share unless it includes the partnership interest and conversion
waterfall.

## The same evidence through the three frameworks

### Alden: liquidity, credit, and durability

Marriott's system depends on hotel owners and franchisees obtaining financing
to open and renovate properties. In a credit contraction, the corporate fee
platform may remain profitable while future room growth slows and guarantees
become more important. Its lower property ownership reduces direct capital
needs but does not eliminate exposure to owner credit.

Host's liquidity depends on hotel cash flow, unencumbered asset value, debt
maturities, interest rates, and the ability to refinance or sell properties.
Luxury locations may have stronger demand, but they also require higher service
and renewal standards. The stress case is a RevPAR decline combined with wage,
insurance, and refinancing inflation.

### Damodaran: narrative to numbers and lifecycle

Marriott's “asset-light global lodging and loyalty platform” narrative requires
room additions, fee rates, loyalty economics, owner returns, guarantee losses,
technology spending, and diluted-share changes to be forecast separately. It is
a mature platform still reinvesting in international distribution, payments,
midscale brands, and loyalty.

Host's “high-quality lodging real estate” narrative requires normalized NOI,
renewal capex, hotel-level margins, debt cost, property cap rates, acquisitions,
dispositions, and OP-unit dilution. It is a mature property portfolio in a
renewal and recycling lifecycle, not a pure growth company.

### Forensic earnings quality

| Test | Marriott | Host |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Preferred measure | Adjusted EBITDA and fee cash | Adjusted FFO and EBITDAre |
| Main adjustment risk | Loyalty estimates, guarantee exposure, technology, acquisitions, buybacks, and debt | Depreciation add-backs, asset-sale gains, condo income, renewal capex, interest, and OP units |
| Cash question | Does fee cash remain after loyalty and support obligations? | Does property cash remain after long-run renewal capital and financing? |
| Falsifier | Fee-paying rooms and co-brand cash weaken while loyalty and guarantee claims rise | RevPAR rises while hotel margin, post-renewal cash, and refinancing capacity deteriorate |

## Valuation expectations

The synchronized valuation screen gives the following provisional cases:

| Company | Bear | Base | Bull | September 11, 2026 equity value |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Marriott | $1.6B × 30 = $48.0B | $2.3B × 38 = $87.4B | $3.0B × 45 = $135.0B | $87.3B |
| Host | $650M × 15 = $9.75B | $800M × 18 = $14.4B | $950M × 20 = $19.0B | $15.2B |

These are sensitivity screens, not price targets. Marriott's market value is
near the base case, so the price requires durable fee growth, loyalty economics,
owner openings, and capital returns that do not weaken the balance sheet. Host's
market value is above the base case, so the price requires normalized property
cash, location value, and refinancing access sufficient to offset renewal and
OP-unit claims.

The denominators must not be merged. Marriott deserves a fee-platform approach
with explicit contingent liabilities. Host requires property cash after
renewal capex plus a NAV cross-check. Applying Marriott's fee multiple to Host
FFO, or Host's property multiple to Marriott's adjusted EBITDA, would hide the
different capital burdens.

## Conclusion

Marriott captures the cleaner relationship economics when its brand,
distribution, loyalty, and owner network compound without a matching rise in
loyalty, guarantee, technology, or debt claims. Host can capture attractive
property economics when location scarcity and normalized NOI cover labor,
insurance, renewal capital, interest, and refinancing needs.

The stronger conclusion is conditional: Marriott owns the customer and owner
interface, while Host owns the scarce physical capacity. Neither control point
is sufficient by itself. The next filing must show whether each company's
reported growth is converting into cash after the burden specific to its model.
