# Industrial distribution and infrastructure: forensic comparison

Date baseline: 2026-08-10; latest evidence through Q2 2026.

This comparison uses the local company packets for Ferguson, Fastenal, W.W.
Grainger, Applied Industrial Technologies, WESCO, Sterling Infrastructure, and Quanta Services. It is a
research artifact, not a completed valuation or investment recommendation.
The purpose is to test whether the apparent infrastructure opportunity is
durable economic control or simply a period of favorable pricing, backlog,
acquisition, and capital-spending conditions.

## The lane question

Who captures the cleaner economics when physical infrastructure demand rises:
the distributor controlling procurement, the contractor doing field execution,
or the asset owner funding the build? What happens to cash conversion when
inventory, labor, project timing, and customer concentration are included?

| Company | Control point | Evidence of operating leverage | Main forensic risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ferguson | Local branch density, inventory, and contractor workflow | FY2025 OCF $1.908B; calendar-2025 OCF about $2.2B; roughly two-thirds of sales tied to repair, maintenance, and improvement | Margin and growth mix affected by supplier price timing, bolt-on M&A, working capital, and $4.2B debt |
| Core & Main | Water/wastewater/fire-protection specialty channel and local project support | Fiscal 2025 OCF $650M; gross margin 26.9%; replacement and smart-utility exposure | Fiscal 2024’s $741M acquisition outlay, fiscal 2025 acquisition cadence, TRA/NCI claims, municipal timing, inventory rebates/reserves, and cash available to Class A owners |
| Fastenal | Embedded replenishment, vending, onsite service, and digital procurement | Q2 2026 operating margin 21.0%; digital footprint was 61.4% of 2025 sales; 2025 OCF less net capex was about $1.07B | Tariff/pricing lift, larger-customer mix, and lower gross margin may obscure volume quality |

## What the evidence currently supports

The common pattern is that infrastructure value often accrues to the company
that removes coordination friction. Ferguson, Fastenal, and Grainger control
availability and procurement; WESCO controls project-channel access; Sterling
and Quanta convert large capital programs into field execution. None of these
control points is automatically a moat. The test is whether the relationship
produces durable cash returns after inventory, labor, trucks, branches,
equipment, acquisitions, warranty exposure, and project timing.

The distinction between demand and monetization is important. Quanta's Q2
revenue was $9.6B with $53.4B of total backlog, while Sterling's Q2 backlog was
$4.33B and its mission-critical work was 92% of E-Infrastructure backlog. Those
figures demonstrate visible demand, not necessarily high realized returns.
Fastenal's Q2 operating cash flow was $266M, equal to 69% of net income, which
is a useful cash check but still a single quarter. Ferguson's 2025 operating
cash flow of $2.2B and 1.1x leverage provide stronger resilience evidence than
backlog alone.

## Earnings-quality and financial-engineering review

### Distribution: price, mix, and customer size can flatter growth

Fastenal's Q2 sales rose 14.7% while gross margin fell from 45.3% to 44.6%.
Management attributed growth to larger customers, onsite and digital
solutions, pricing actions, and share gains, while unfavorable price/cost was
the main gross-margin headwind. This is a plausible transition toward deeper
embeddedness, but it creates a direct test: is lower margin buying a larger,
stickier wallet share, or is the company accepting structurally worse economics
to defend growth?

Fastenal is the cleanest industrial example of the combined framework's
“workflow control versus burden transfer” problem. Its 2025 operating cash
flow of $1.296B less roughly $231M of net capex left about $1.065B before
dividends, and debt was only $125M. That is materially cleaner than a
project-distribution model. But the balance sheet still carries $1.748B of
inventory, and the business needs branches, onsite labor, vending equipment,
trucks, and distribution centers. The digital footprint is an adoption metric,
not software-margin evidence. A durable thesis therefore requires site-level
returns and better price/cost realization, not just more digital sales.

Grainger's Q2 operating margin was 16.1%, with High-Touch Solutions sales up
11.9% and Endless Assortment up 13.5% reported. The faster digital channel is
not equivalent to the higher-profit enterprise channel. The forensic question
is whether mix and tariff/price effects are being mistaken for productivity,
especially while the company invests in online assortment and purchasing tools.

Grainger adds a specific accounting wrinkle to the distribution comparison.
About 80% of inventory is LIFO; the 2025 LIFO reserve meant reported inventory
would have been $939M higher and 2025 net earnings $102M higher under FIFO. This
does not imply misconduct, but it makes inflation-cycle comparisons of gross
margin, assets, and ROIC less reliable. At the same time, receivables used
$190M of cash, inventory used $147M, capex rose to $684M, and $196M of
divestiture losses were excluded in the adjusted operating bridge. The channel
control is credible, but the economic burden is larger than a digital-growth
headline suggests.

Ferguson reported Q2 sales growth of 4.6%, adjusted operating margin of 10.7%,
and higher 2026 capex guidance of $375M-$425M. Its 2025 result included eight
acquisitions with more than $300M of annualized revenue. The channel thesis is
credible, but the return calculation must separate organic share gains from
acquired revenue and test whether branch density and service investments earn
more than the acquisition and integration burden.

Ferguson is the broad-line counterweight to Fastenal. Its repair/maintenance/
improvement exposure and waterworks/mechanical mix support durability, but the
cash burden is heavier: FY2025 OCF of $1.908B less $305M of capex and $301M of
acquisition cash was roughly $1.302B before dividends, buybacks, and debt
service. Receivables were $3.964B, inventory $4.492B, and total debt $4.2B at
July 31, 2025. That makes Ferguson’s channel-control thesis more dependent on
working-capital discipline, acquisition returns, and balance-sheet resilience
than Fastenal’s.

The like-period cash bridge makes the control-versus-burden distinction more
precise. These are FY2025 GAAP filing denominators before interest, taxes,
working-capital normalization, and any claim not shown in the simple bridge:

| Company | OCF | Less productive-asset capex | Less acquisitions | Residual before returns | Capital-return overlay | Read-through |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Ferguson | $1,908M | $305M | $301M | $1,302M | $948M buybacks + $489M dividends | Strong operating cash, but broad-line scale carries inventory, debt, and capital-return claims |
| Fastenal | $1,296M | $245M | — | $1,051M | $1,004M dividends | Cleanest bridge in the cohort; the question is whether service intensity and inventory support durable returns |
| Grainger | $2,015M | $684M | — | $1,331M | $1,045M buybacks | Strong cash generation, but replacement-cost economics, LIFO, and channel mix matter |
| WESCO | $125M | $100M | $36M | $(11M) | $75M buybacks | Demand and backlog can expand while cash is absorbed by project inventory and receivables |

The table is not a valuation model. It is a falsifiable earnings-quality screen:
Ferguson, Fastenal, and Grainger generated substantial cash after the visible
reinvestment line, while WESCO's FY2025 bridge did not cover capex and
acquisitions. Fastenal's dividends nearly consumed its post-capex residual;
Ferguson's buybacks plus dividends exceeded it; and Grainger's buybacks did not
resolve the need to test working-capital and replacement-cost economics. The
same demand narrative therefore produces four different owner-cash profiles.

### Project businesses: backlog is an expectation, not cash

WESCO's Q2 sales were $6.7B, adjusted EBITDA margin was 7.3%, backlog rose
about 60%, and data-center sales reached $1.5B. The demand is compelling, but
the full filing bridge is more cautionary: FY2025 operating cash flow was only
$125M against $99.8M of capex and $36.1M of acquisition payments, while
receivables and inventory absorbed substantial cash. First-half 2026 OCF
improved to $275M, but receivables and inventory still used cash while
accounts payable and other liabilities supplied funding. Project distribution
can therefore consume cash before revenue recognition through inventory,
deposits, receivables, and customer-specific stock. The next review must
reconcile backlog growth with operating cash flow, inventory turns, receivable
days, gross margin, cancellations, and customer concentration.

Sterling's Q2 guidance and backlog expanded sharply, while Transportation
Solutions revenue declined 20% and adjusted operating income rose 8% as capital
was reallocated toward E-Infrastructure. This may be excellent portfolio
management; it can also make the company look cleaner by shrinking a weak lane
while the favored AI-capex lane is at a peak. Signed backlog, combined backlog,
and future-phase opportunities must be tracked separately from recognized
revenue, gross profit, retention, and cash collected.

Quanta's Q2 RPO of $33.6B and total backlog of $53.4B are powerful visibility
signals. The Damodaran question is what incremental capital and labor are needed
to convert that visibility into economic profit. The Alden-style resilience
question is whether grid modernization and load growth create a durable
multi-year demand floor or whether a data-center-heavy mix creates a new
concentration risk. Adjusted EBITDA should not substitute for cash after
working-capital investment, equipment, safety, and skilled-labor costs.

## Alden / Damodaran / forensic synthesis

The Alden lens favors the distributors and contractors tied to unavoidable
maintenance, grid reliability, water, and operating continuity rather than only
new discretionary construction. The Damodaran lens separates growth from value:
backlog, acquisitions, and pricing matter only if the return on incremental
capital exceeds the cost of capital after the full burden stack is included.
Our forensic lens asks whether adjusted metrics, price inflation, acquisition
growth, and backlog layers are making the underlying business appear smoother
or more profitable than it is.

The cleanest current economic-control candidates are Fastenal and Grainger at
the procurement-workflow layer, and Ferguson where branch density and repair,
maintenance, and improvement demand reduce new-build dependence. The strongest
structural-demand candidates are Quanta and Sterling, but they carry more field
labor and project-conversion risk. WESCO is the bridge: it benefits from
structural data-center demand while carrying distributor working-capital and
project-concentration exposure.

Generac extends the lane below the distributor and field contractor. It monetizes
the need for reliable electricity through qualified equipment and service rather
than through procurement or construction execution. That creates a useful
control-versus-burden contrast: C&I and hyperscale demand can be structurally
important, but FY2025 cash after capex was only about $268M while inventory,
warranty, legal provisions, tariff effects, and factory investment remained in
the system. The [Generac deep memo](../deep-company-pages/generac-holdings-inc.md)
therefore serves as the power-resilience counterpart to Vertiv and Quanta.

## Falsifiers and next checks

The lane conclusion weakens if:

- backlog rises while cash conversion, gross margin, or customer advances worsen;
- inventory and receivables grow faster than recognized demand;
- price/tariff effects explain most growth after volume and share gains are
  removed;
- acquisition-adjusted growth fails to produce higher returns on capital;
- large-account or data-center mix creates persistent margin dilution;
- future-phase opportunities are repeatedly presented as near-term backlog but
  do not convert to signed work; or
- skilled-labor, warranty, safety, and equipment costs rise faster than project
  pricing.

The next filing review should capture, on a comparable basis, revenue, organic
growth, price contribution, gross and operating margin, GAAP versus adjusted
earnings, operating cash flow, free cash flow, inventory, receivables, payables,
capex, acquisitions, stock compensation, backlog/RPO definitions, cancellations,
customer concentration, labor availability, and realized margin by project or
segment where disclosed.

The deeper cross-sector pattern is now testable: modern infrastructure demand
is rewarding workflow and execution intermediaries, but the apparent cleaner
economics may be purchased by shifting inventory, labor, financing, and timing
burdens onto those same intermediaries.

Fastenal sharpens the comparison: the distributor with the most embedded
workflow may deserve the strongest durability premium, yet its gross-margin
decline and inventory burden show exactly how a moat can be monetized through
more service intensity rather than higher unit economics.

Grainger is the enterprise-procurement version: its assortment and workflow
relationship are broad and sticky, but LIFO/inflation accounting, supply-chain
capex, and the lower-margin Endless Assortment channel make replacement-cost
economics and segment-level cash returns essential.

Ferguson adds the broad-line version: repair-oriented demand can be durable and
share gains can coexist with weak housing, but breadth is purchased through
inventory, acquisitions, debt, and integration. The cleaner economics question
is therefore not “which distributor has the largest market?” but “which control
point produces the highest cash return after the full burden stack?”

Core & Main is the specialized replacement-infrastructure contradiction. Its
water and fire-protection control point is durable, and fiscal 2025 OCF was
$650M against $46M of capex and $61M of acquisitions. The prior fiscal year,
however, consumed $741M on acquisitions against $621M of OCF, demonstrating
that a clean current year does not establish steady-state owner cash. The
parent also has a $725M Tax Receivable Agreement payable and non-controlling
interests. This is the clearest warning in the lane against treating adjusted
EBITDA, municipal need, or “national scale” as shareholder cash without
tracing the ownership and reinvestment claims.

Applied adds the technical-MRO version of channel control. Its fiscal 2025
filing shows $492.4M of operating cash flow, $27.2M of capex, and $293.4M of
acquisition cash, leaving only $171.8M before financing and capital returns.
That contrasts with the company’s approximately $465M management FCF measure:
the difference is not a fraud allegation, but it identifies acquisition spend
as a real cost of the growth algorithm. Engineered Solutions approaching 40%
of sales can improve technical value and customer retention, while also adding
engineering labor, project timing, and working-capital complexity. The next
proof is segment-level incremental ROIC and post-acquisition cash, not mix shift
alone.
