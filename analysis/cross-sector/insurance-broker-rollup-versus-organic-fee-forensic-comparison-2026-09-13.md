# Insurance broker roll-up versus organic fee growth: forensic comparison

This comparison adds Gallagher to Aon and Marsh. All three control insurance
distribution and advisory workflows, but their acquisition intensity differs.

| Company | FY2025 OCF | FY2025 acquisition cash | Organic growth evidence | Main forensic question |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| Aon | $3.481B | $394M | 6% organic revenue growth | Do restructuring, debt, pensions, and M&A normalize without reducing cash per share? |
| Marsh | $5.292B | $652M | ~4% underlying revenue growth | Is fee growth durable after talent, receivable, legal, and acquisition costs? |
| Gallagher | $1.930B | $15.766B | 6% organic fee growth | Does AssuredPartners earn a high return, or is growth being bought faster than cash compounds? |

The comparison changes the meaning of “best broker.” Aon and Marsh show a
cleaner near-term OCF-minus-acquisition bridge, while Gallagher may have the
larger post-deal runway. That runway is an investment claim, not current owner
cash. Gallagher's $9.904B of goodwill acquired in 2025 and $22.593B ending
goodwill make return-on-acquisition and impairment surveillance mandatory.

## Business economics: framework conclusion

The shared moat is the workflow: trusted advice, producer relationships, data,
placement, and renewal. The differentiated burden is reinvestment. For Aon and
Marsh, test organic fee growth against restructuring, talent, working capital,
debt, and modest M&A. For Gallagher, add a full acquisition cohort model:

`purchase price -> acquired revenue -> retention -> producer/talent cost ->
incremental margin -> integration and earnout cash -> debt/equity cost ->
per-share owner cash`

The Alden lens favors all three when risk complexity rises, but rates and
corporate liquidity can constrain both client budgets and acquisition funding.
The Damodaran lens says Gallagher's terminal value is especially sensitive to
the return on reinvestment. A high multiple is earned only if acquired
relationships become organic cash generators.

None of the filings establishes misconduct. The possible shenanigans are
denominator and presentation risks: adjusted growth that obscures acquisition
dependence, EBITDA that excludes recurring integration or earnout costs,
goodwill that is treated as value without a return test, and capital returns
that look attractive while debt and dilution fund growth.

## Definitions and the common economic chain

An **insurance broker** helps a client assess risk, arrange coverage, and
renew or change that coverage. The broker generally earns commissions or fees;
the insurer—not the broker—normally bears the claims and reserve risk.

**Organic growth** is management's measure of revenue growth excluding items
such as acquisitions, divestitures, and currency. It is useful for testing
existing client relationships, but it is not a GAAP measure and does not prove
that the resulting revenue produces cash after producer compensation.

The three companies should be read through this chain:

`client risk or workforce complexity -> broker advice and placement -> insurer or capital-market access -> renewal relationship -> fee cash -> talent, technology, acquisitions, debt, and shareholder cash`

The chain has two different growth engines. Existing-client growth comes from
pricing, exposure, retention, and new business. Acquired growth comes from
buying brokerages, producers, and client relationships. The second engine can
be valuable, but its purchase price is a reinvestment requirement, not a free
addition to revenue.

The residual is only a comparison screen. It does not subtract all producer
compensation, working-capital normalization, pension, legal, debt, SBC, or
future acquisition spending. Gallagher's negative screen is not a claim that
the operating business destroyed value; it means the company paid for a large
future platform during the period.

## What each business must prove

### Aon: fee growth after restructuring and portfolio changes

Aon's 6% organic revenue growth is meaningful because it indicates continued
client demand beyond acquisition and currency effects. The filing also shows
2025 revenue growth of about 9%, so the difference between organic and reported
growth must be tracked rather than treated as one number.

The 2025 cash flow included $2.349B of business-sale proceeds and a $1.201B
gain from business sales. Those items improved liquidity and accounting income,
but they do not represent recurring advisory fees. The owner-cash test is
whether the remaining platform produces cash after technology, talent,
restructuring, pensions, acquisitions, debt, and dilution.

### Marsh: cleaner intermediary economics, but not a free acquisition option

Marsh's 2025 underlying revenue growth was approximately 4% against 10% GAAP
revenue growth. This difference is the central acquisition-quality test. The
business controls trusted risk, reinsurance, consulting, and workforce advice,
but it also depends on professional talent and roughly $7B of fee and
commission receivables.

Marsh repurchased $2.012B of stock and paid $1.699B of dividends in 2025. These
returns were funded alongside strong OCF, but the analysis should still ask
whether buybacks and dividends leave enough cash to retain producers, fund
technology, repay debt, and pursue acquisitions at acceptable returns. SBC of
$394M is an economic compensation cost even when it is non-cash in the period.

### Gallagher: acquisition return is the investment case

Gallagher completed 33 acquisitions in 2025 with approximately $3.562B of
annualized acquired revenue and paid $15.766B of acquisition cash. The
annualized revenue number is a run rate, not collected profit. The acquired
relationships must retain clients and producers, generate margin after
compensation, and cover integration, earnout, debt, equity, and amortization
claims.

Gallagher ended 2025 with approximately $22.593B of goodwill and $12.873B of
long-term debt. Goodwill is not cash and does not prove that the acquired
relationships earn their cost. A future impairment would be informative, but
waiting for an impairment is not the right test; the stronger test is cash
return on acquired revenue before impairment is recorded.

## The three analytical lenses

### Alden: risk complexity is durable, acquisition funding is cyclical

More regulation, cyber exposure, catastrophe risk, employee complexity, and
specialized insurance needs can increase demand for broker advice. That is the
durability case. Interest rates, corporate liquidity, insurer capacity, and
credit spreads can reduce client budgets or make brokerage acquisitions more
expensive. That is the liquidity case.

Aon and Marsh have more visible current residual cash, but both still depend on
professional talent and corporate risk budgets. Gallagher has the larger
capital-allocation sensitivity because its acquisition spending is many times
annual operating cash flow. The stress case is not only lower insurance
pricing; it is lower client activity combined with expensive debt and weaker
acquisition financing.

### Damodaran: separate organic value from acquired value

For each company, the model should forecast:

- organic growth from retention, pricing, exposure, and new business;
- producer and specialist compensation;
- fee and commission receivables and collection timing;
- technology and compliance spending;
- acquisition price, acquired revenue, margin, retention, and integration;
- goodwill, amortization, earnouts, debt, SBC, and share count; and
- the terminal growth rate after the acquisition program moderates.

Gallagher's terminal value is especially sensitive to the return on future
acquisitions. A high organic-growth rate with low acquisition return can still
destroy per-share value if management repeatedly pays too much. A lower
organic-growth rate can still create value if acquired relationships are bought
at disciplined prices and become self-funded.

### Forensic earnings quality

| Test | Aon | Marsh | Gallagher |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Growth denominator | Organic growth versus divestiture and acquisition effects | Underlying growth versus acquired revenue | Organic fee growth versus very large acquired revenue run rate |
| Cash denominator | OCF after M&A, sale-gain comparison, pensions, and restructuring | OCF after M&A, receivables, talent, debt, and SBC | OCF after acquisition cash, earnouts, integration, debt, and dilution |
| Main presentation risk | Sale proceeds and adjustments make cash look cleaner | Adjusted growth and legal exclusions hide recurring cost | EBITDA and acquired revenue obscure purchase price and return on capital |
| Burden carrier | Employees, debt, pensions, clients, and the company | Producers, debt, clients, and the company | Acquired producers, debt/equity holders, and existing shareholders |

None of these facts establishes misconduct. The concern is financial
engineering when an adjusted measure makes a recurring acquisition or legal,
talent, or integration cost appear exceptional, or when per-share returns are
shown without charging equity issuance and debt.

## Valuation expectations

Marsh's separate valuation bridge uses $3.2B/$4.2B/$5.2B of normalized owner
cash at 18x/22x/26x. The base case is close to the derived market value, so the
price requires organic fee durability, controlled receivables, talent
retention, and acquisition returns.

Aon's register uses $2.2B/$2.8B/$3.3B at 18x/22x/26x against approximately
$64.7B of equity value. That price requires recurring fee cash after
restructuring, pensions, debt, and acquisition normalization.

Gallagher's register uses $1.1B/$1.7B/$2.3B at 24x/30x/36x against
approximately $61.6B of equity value. The base case is below the market value,
so the market is paying for future acquired cash rather than the negative 2025
residual. It requires acquired producers and clients to remain, acquired
revenue to convert to margin and cash, and debt/dilution to remain controlled.

These denominators should not be merged. The question is not which broker has
the highest current OCF. It is which company can turn client trust and
professional labor into per-share cash after the reinvestment required to keep
the relationship.

## Conclusion

The cleanest economics in this cohort generally sit with the broker because it
controls advice, placement, data, and renewal without carrying the insurer's
claims balance sheet. But “fee-heavy” does not remove the need to pay people,
collect receivables, comply with regulation, fund acquisitions, and preserve
trust.

Aon and Marsh currently provide stronger evidence of recurring fee cash.
Gallagher provides the sharper test of whether a relationship roll-up can
create value through acquisition. The cross-company conclusion remains
conditional until organic growth, acquired cash returns, producer retention,
debt, dilution, and legal or conduct costs are measured together.
