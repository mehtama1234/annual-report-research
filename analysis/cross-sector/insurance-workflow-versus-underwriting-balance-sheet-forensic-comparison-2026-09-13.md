# Insurance workflow versus underwriting balance sheet: forensic comparison

Date baseline: 2026-09-13. This comparison adds Marsh and Chubb to the existing
Aon, Apollo, BlackRock, and T. Rowe Price financial lane. It tests whether the
cleaner economics accrue to the intermediary that controls risk distribution or
the carrier that underwrites and invests the float.

## Business economics: the lane question

Who owns the valuable relationship in insurance: the broker diagnosing and
placing risk, or the carrier selecting, pricing, reserving, and funding it?
Both can have durable moats. They do not carry the same capital, liquidity, or
earnings-quality burden.

| Company | Control point | Main economic burden | FY2025 evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Marsh | Risk placement, reinsurance, consulting, data, and institutional trust | Talent, receivables, conduct, legal, cyber, and acquisition integration | $5.292B OCF; $4.349B after PP&E/acquisitions |
| Aon | Advisory/brokerage workflow and fiduciary client relationship | Debt, pensions, restructuring, M&A, receivables, and conduct | Existing Aon dossier shows large residual but financing/M&A claims |
| Chubb | Underwriting, claims, reserve management, and invested float | Catastrophe, reserve, reinsurance, asset/liability, regulatory capital, and life risk | $54.84B net premiums written; 85.7% P&C combined ratio; $6.5B pre-tax investment income |
| Prudential | Retirement distribution, life and annuity underwriting, asset-liability management, and PGIM | Long-duration policyholder claims, assumption changes, investment spread, hedging, subsidiary capital, and parent liquidity | $5.161B FY2025 after-tax adjusted operating income; $1.609T AUM; $3.8B parent highly liquid assets |
| Markel | Specialty underwriting, claims discipline, investable premiums, and operating-company capital allocation | Catastrophe, reserve, reinsurance, invested-asset, subsidiary-capital, and holding-company risk | $2.3B+ FY2025 adjusted operating income; 95% FY2025 combined ratio; approximately $2.8B operating cash flow |
| Apollo / BlackRock | Asset allocation, fee platform, and private-market/investment workflow | Marks, insurance balance sheet, liquidity, fee compression, and acquisition/dilution | Existing dossiers require separate fee, spread, and performance denominators |

The cleaner economics usually sit with the workflow owner, but the carrier can
earn superior returns when underwriting discipline and float investment are
strong. The difference is not quality versus no quality; it is which risks are
being paid for and where the claims sit.

## Like-period cash and denominator warning

| Company | OCF | Listed PP&E / acquisitions | Simple residual | Capital returns / claims | Correct interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Marsh | $5.292B | $(291M) / $(652M) | $4.349B | $(2.012B) buybacks + $(1.699B) dividends; $394M SBC | Fee cash is relatively clean, but recurring M&A, talent, receivables, and SBC reduce distributability |
| Chubb | $12.816B | $(291M) / $(289M) | $12.236B | $(3.694B) buybacks + $(1.505B) dividends; policyholder and capital claims | OCF is not owner cash because premium, claim, investment, restricted-cash, and surplus mechanics dominate |

The table is deliberately asymmetric. Applying Marsh's OCF-minus-capex-minus-
acquisitions denominator to Chubb would overstate carrier owner cash. Applying
Chubb's underwriting/book-value framework to Marsh would miss the value of fee
relationships and the absence of a claims balance sheet.

## Earnings quality and forensic risks

### Marsh: organic fee quality and acquisition returns

Marsh reported approximately 10% GAAP revenue growth but roughly 4% underlying
revenue growth in 2025. The gap is the central test: if acquisitions are needed
to produce headline growth, the acquisition program is part of maintenance
capital. Receivables of approximately $7B require collection and allowance
testing. Legal and conduct charges, conflicts involving insurers or vendors,
professional retention, and $394M of SBC must be included in the recurring
cost base.

### Chubb: combined-ratio and reserve quality

Chubb's 85.7% combined ratio is stronger evidence than premium growth, but it
must be decomposed into current accident-year losses, catastrophe losses, prior
period development, rate, exposure, mix, acquisition costs, and expenses.
Investment income of $6.5B is real income on a liability-funded portfolio, not
unencumbered corporate cash. Life earnings add another duration and guarantee
system. Buybacks are only value-creating when surplus, reserve adequacy, and
normalized book value support them.

### Prudential: adjusted operating income and liability quality

Prudential reported FY2025 after-tax adjusted operating income of approximately
`$5.161B`, but the recurring value must be separated across retirement, life,
international, and PGIM. Its approximately `$1.609T` of AUM is a scale measure,
not automatically fee cash or distributable capital. The forensic work is to
reconcile adjusted operating income with GAAP net income, investment marks,
assumption updates, reserve development, policyholder behavior, statutory
surplus, and parent liquidity. The Q2 2026 result of approximately `$1.438B` of
adjusted operating income is useful evidence of current earnings, not proof of
through-cycle liability quality.

Neither memo establishes misconduct. The possible “shenanigans” are denominator
and presentation risks: adjusted earnings excluding recurring legal or
restructuring costs, organic growth obscured by acquisitions, favorable reserve
development, investment marks treated as recurring, and capital returns treated
as proof of owner cash.

## Alden lens

Both firms benefit from rising complexity, regulation, catastrophe awareness,
cyber risk, and institutional demand for trusted risk transfer. Marsh is more
resilient to underwriting losses but more exposed to corporate budgets, talent,
and insurance-market commissions. Chubb benefits from higher rates and pricing
discipline but carries direct claims, catastrophe, reserve, and asset-market
exposure.

In a liquidity tightening, Marsh may gain demand for advice while clients cut
consulting budgets; Chubb may gain investment yield while claims inflation and
credit losses rise. The macro lens therefore has to follow the cash and capital
claims, not simply label either business “defensive.”

## Damodaran lens

Marsh should be valued as a recurring fee stream plus an explicit acquisition
return model:

`organic fee growth -> operating margin after talent/legal costs -> tax/capex/working capital -> acquisition cash and debt -> diluted owner cash`

Chubb should be valued as a component model:

`normalized P&C underwriting income + after-tax investment income + normalized life income - required capital growth - reserve/catastrophe stress`

Prudential requires a related but different component model:

`normalized insurance and retirement earnings + PGIM fee income + investment spread - policyholder claims - required capital growth - adverse assumption and asset stress`

The lifecycle distinction matters. Marsh's growth can be reinvested through
acquisitions and producer relationships. Chubb's growth requires surplus and
capital to support premium, reserve, and investment assets. A high multiple on
either company must be earned by durable incremental returns, not by a single
strong cycle.

Prudential's value must also separate insurance and retirement earnings from
PGIM fee income. Its FY2025 adjusted operating income was approximately `$5.161B`
against `$1.609T` of AUM, while parent highly liquid assets were approximately
`$3.8B`. Those figures show scale but do not prove that earnings are freely
distributable. The required tests are liability experience, investment spread,
assumption changes, subsidiary capital, parent liquidity, and organic PGIM flows.

### Markel: underwriting plus capital allocation

Markel adds a hybrid case. FY2025 adjusted operating income exceeded `$2.3B`,
the combined ratio was `95%`, and operating cash flow was approximately `$2.8B`.
Q1 and Q2 2026 combined ratios improved to `93%`, while insurance adjusted
operating income rose to approximately `$369M` and `$376M`. The numbers support
current underwriting improvement, but the investor must still separate current-
year losses from reserve releases, insurance cash from operating-company cash,
and investment gains from repeatable underwriting economics. Markel's advantage
is capital-allocation flexibility; its burden is a less simple denominator and
the possibility that catastrophe, reserve, or operating-company weakness arrives
at the same time.

## Filing-based thesis breakers

The broker thesis weakens if organic growth is persistently below acquisition
growth, receivables deteriorate, producer retention fails, legal/conduct charges
recur, or buybacks and M&A increase leverage without per-share cash growth.

The carrier thesis weakens if combined ratios deteriorate, reserve releases
reverse, catastrophe/social inflation exceeds the modeled load, investment
income depends on illiquid or impaired assets, life spreads weaken, or capital
returns outrun surplus and required capital.

For Prudential, the thesis also weakens if assumption updates recur, PGIM AUM
grows only through market appreciation, international earnings cannot be
remitted, or parent liquidity falls while capital returns continue.

The next comparable filing set should capture organic growth, acquisition
cohorts, producer retention, receivables, combined ratios, accident-year loss
trends, reserve development, invested-asset quality and duration, capital and
surplus, debt, SBC, book value, and capital returns.

## Valuation: fee income and underwriting capital need separate denominators

Value the broker from organic fee growth, retention, producer economics,
receivables, compensation, acquisitions, debt, and diluted shares. Value the
carrier from normalized underwriting profit and investment spread after reserve,
catastrophe, reinsurance, asset-liability, and required-capital costs. Life and
retirement earnings require a separate spread and liability model; PGIM fee
income should not be used to hide the burden of Prudential's insurance balance
sheet.

Use normal, stressed-loss, reserve-development, and lower-flow cases. A broker
multiple is too high if acquisition cash and debt are required to maintain
organic growth. A carrier multiple is too high if current investment income or
reserve releases are treated as permanent distributable earnings.
