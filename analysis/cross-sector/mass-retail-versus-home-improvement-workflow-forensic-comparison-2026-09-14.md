# Mass retail versus home-improvement workflow: relationship control and owner cash

## Purpose

Walmart and Lowe's are both large retailers, but they monetize different
customer relationships. Walmart is built around frequent household shopping
and is adding advertising, membership, marketplace, fulfillment, and financial
services. Lowe's is built around repair, improvement, and contractor projects,
where Pro accounts, product availability, delivery, installation, and service
matter more than daily frequency.

Target adds the stressed relationship-layer case. Its core merchandise
business weakened in fiscal 2025 while membership, Roundel advertising,
marketplace, and same-day delivery improved. The central question is whether
the added monetization is collected and incremental enough to repair the base
retail economics.

The comparison asks which relationship is more durable, which attached revenue
is cleaner, and which company carries the greater capital and execution burden.

## Operating comparison

| Dimension | Walmart | Lowe's |
|---|---|---|
| Primary relationship | Frequent household shopping across food, general merchandise, and digital channels | DIY repair, larger home projects, Pro supply, installation, and home services |
| FY2025 / annual-window sales evidence | Q2 FY2027 revenue grew `5.9%`; Q2 U.S. comp sales grew `2.6%` | Q2 2026 sales were `$26.0B`; comparable sales grew `0.2%` |
| Recent demand signal | Q2 FY2027 eCommerce grew `23%`; global advertising grew `38%`; membership fee revenue grew `17%`; U.S. comp sales grew `2.6%` | Q2 2026 online sales grew `15.7%`; comparable sales grew `0.2%`; Pro and home services offset weak DIY |
| Relationship frequency | High-frequency routine purchases and replenishment | Lower-frequency but potentially deeper project and Pro workflows |
| Attached monetization | Advertising, membership, marketplace, fulfillment, and financial services | Pro accounts, online ordering, home services, loyalty, delivery, and acquisitions |
| Main operating burden | Low prices, stores, labor, inventory, fulfillment, shrink, and financial services | Stores, broad inventory, distribution, project timing, delivery, installers, acquisitions, and housing cycle |
| Main cash-quality risk | Inventory/payable timing, deferred maintenance, fulfillment cost, and service-layer claims | Inventory and supplier terms, acquisition costs, project mix, maintenance capex, and buybacks |
| Vendor and service risk | Supplier-funded advertising and membership benefits must convert to cash | Pro accounts, delivery, installation, and acquisition integration must convert to cash |

## Q2 2026 update: both models are growing attached services, but both carry the cost

Walmart's Q2 FY2027 revenue rose 5.9%, U.S. comparable sales rose 2.6%, global
eCommerce rose 23%, advertising rose 38%, and membership fee revenue rose 17%.
Its first-half operating cash flow was `$19.710B`, but capital spending rose to
`$14.181B`, leaving company-defined free cash flow of only `$5.529B` for the
period. The 10-Q attributes part of the gross-margin improvement to tariff
refunds and says eCommerce growth was primarily store-fulfilled delivery. The
control point is strengthening, but the cost base needed to serve it is also
expanding.

Lowe's Q2 sales rose to `$26.0B`, but comparable sales rose only 0.2%. Online
sales rose 15.7%, with Pro and home services offsetting weak discretionary DIY.
Reported EPS was `$4.27` and adjusted EPS `$4.40`; the quarter included `$96M`
of acquisition expenses and an `$0.11` tariff-refund benefit. First-half
operating cash flow fell to `$7.009B` from `$7.610B`, while capital spending
rose to `$1.063B`. Lowe's lowered its full-year comparable-sales outlook to
flat and diluted EPS outlook to approximately `$11.75`.

The current evidence makes the comparison sharper. Walmart has stronger
frequency and attached advertising economics, but it is funding substantially
more omnichannel capacity. Lowe's has a more project-specific relationship,
but its mix improvement is occurring alongside weak DIY, acquisition charges,
tariff refunds, and a lower outlook. Neither attached layer should be valued
without the capital, labor, service, and working-capital costs that create the
customer relationship.

The companies should not be ranked by growth rate alone. Walmart's frequent
traffic can support more advertising impressions and membership occasions, but
its low-price model carries a large recurring operating burden. Lowe's has less
frequent demand, but a successful Pro or project relationship can generate
repeat purchases, delivery, installation, and service attachment.

Target is the stressed relationship-layer case, but Q2 2026 showed a genuine
operating improvement: sales rose 5.3%, comparable sales 3.8%, traffic 3.6%,
and non-merchandise sales 20.1%. The quarter also included a `$994M` tariff
refund that added `$1.65` to EPS and 3.7 percentage points to gross margin.
First-half operating cash flow rose to `$4.519B`, but the 10-Q attributes the
increase primarily to higher accounts-payable leverage; inventory rose to
`$13.2B` and property spending was `$2.404B`. The key forensic difference is
still vendor income: the year-end vendor-income receivable was `$542M` and its
valuation was a critical audit matter. Target needs proof of collection,
tariff-normalized margin, and incremental supplier value before the attached
layer can be valued like recurring platform income.

## Who controls the relationship?

Walmart controls the household shopping interface, store and fulfillment reach,
supplier access, marketplace entry, and purchase data. Lowe's controls the
home-improvement assortment, Pro account relationship, project availability,
distribution, and service connections.

Neither company controls the full outcome:

- Walmart depends on suppliers, marketplace sellers, carriers, store labor,
  and household budgets.
- Lowe's depends on manufacturers, contractors, installers, carriers,
  homeowners, and housing activity.

The cleaner economics belong to the company that can increase customer value
without paying all of the physical cost of the transaction. The burden remains
with the company when it must fund inventory, availability, delivery, service,
returns, labor, and capital to keep the relationship active.

## Attached revenue and incremental value

### Walmart advertising and membership

Walmart's Q2 FY2027 filing reports global advertising growth of `38%` and
global membership fee growth of `17%`. The 10-Q says eCommerce growth was
primarily store-fulfilled delivery and that gross-margin improvement benefited
from tariff refunds and advertising mix. The attached revenue is growing, but
the retail network and its capital spending remain part of the same economics.

The test is whether those dollars are incremental:

`advertising revenue + membership fee + incremental basket margin - ad-service
cost - member benefits - delivery - support - acquisition cost`

Supplier advertising is cleaner when it creates additional conversion rather
than charging suppliers to preserve visibility. Membership is cleaner when
renewal and frequency improve after delivery and discount costs.

### Lowe's Pro, online, and services

Lowe's Q2 2026 online sales grew `15.7%`, while Pro and home services offset
weak discretionary DIY. Reported EPS included an acquisition adjustment and a
tariff-refund benefit. The incremental calculation is different:

`Pro or project gross profit + service attachment - account support - delivery
- installer and warranty cost - receivables and inventory - branch and
distribution investment`

The Pro relationship can be more valuable per customer than ordinary DIY
traffic, but it also requires reliable stock, credit, job-site delivery, and
service quality. A sale is not an economic success if the project creates
returns, rework, claims, or uncollected receivables.

## Cash conversion and financial engineering

Walmart's Q4 2025 operating cash flow was `$41.6B` and free cash flow was
`$14.9B`; Q2 FY2027 first-half operating cash flow was `$19.710B` and free
cash flow was `$5.529B`. Lowe's fiscal 2025 net
earnings were `$6.7B`, while the packet emphasizes the sales, EPS, acquisition,
and project-mix bridge rather than providing a comparable normalized owner-cash
figure in the summary. The companies therefore need different next steps:

| Cash question | Walmart | Lowe's |
|---|---|---|
| Working capital | Inventory, supplier payables, customer balances, seasonal retail timing | Inventory availability, seasonal projects, supplier terms, Pro receivables |
| Maintenance | Stores, fulfillment, technology, delivery, and price/service capacity | Stores, distribution, inventory systems, delivery, and service capability |
| Growth investment | eCommerce, advertising technology, marketplace, membership, financial services | Pro branches, online, home services, FBM/ADG integration, distribution |
| Non-GAAP risk | Adjusted operating income, ad and membership contribution, free-cash-flow definition | Adjusted EPS, acquisition costs, buyback effects, and normalized housing demand |
| Per-share test | Cash after capital spending, leases, SBC, and attached-service obligations | Cash after capital spending, acquisitions, debt, leases, SBC, and buybacks |

An apparent retail cash improvement can come from inventory liquidation or
longer supplier terms. An apparent Lowe's EPS improvement can come from price,
mix, cost cuts, or buybacks while project traffic and maintenance investment
remain weak. Both require multi-year bridges.

## Numeric valuation screen

The primary filings provide a more disciplined comparison than revenue growth.
Walmart's company-defined free cash flow was `$15.120B`, `$12.660B`, and
`$14.923B` for fiscal 2024, 2025, and 2026. Lowe's simple cash after capex was
`$6.176B`, `$7.698B`, and `$7.651B` for fiscal 2023, 2024, and 2025. Lowe's
also spent `$10.088B` on ADG and FBM acquisitions in fiscal 2025, so its
reported cash-after-capex number is not the same as post-acquisition owner
cash.

| Company | Latest simple cash denominator | September 14, 2026 equity value | Simple multiple | What the price requires |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Walmart | `$14.923B` FY2026 company-defined FCF | approximately `$854.8B` | over `57x` | Large growth in normalized cash from retail scale, advertising, membership, marketplace, and omnichannel investment |
| Lowe's | `$7.651B` FY2025 OCF less capex; Q2 first-half OCF less capex was `$5.946B` before other claims | approximately `$110.2B` | approximately `14.4x` on FY2025 screen | Successful ADG/FBM integration, Pro and services growth, and durable cash after acquisition debt and housing-cycle normalization |
| Target | `$2.835B` FY2025 OCF less property spending; Q2 first-half OCF less property spending was approximately `$2.115B` before other claims | approximately `$71.2B` | approximately `25x` on FY2025 screen | Tariff-normalized merchandise recovery, vendor-income collection, Roundel and membership contribution, and cash after the planned capital program |

The current screen is not a recommendation. It identifies different
expectation burdens. Walmart's market value is difficult to support with a
mature-retail cash denominator alone; the price requires substantial attached-
service growth and a high valuation for the resulting platform economics.
Lowe's price is less demanding on the simple denominator, but the denominator
must be adjusted for acquisition financing, inventory optimization, and the
return on the newly acquired Pro and building-products relationships.

## Macro and durability

Walmart serves more routine demand and can gain share when households trade
down. It remains exposed to wage, logistics, supplier, shrink, and inventory
costs. Its scale improves purchasing and distribution, but low prices limit
the room to absorb cost inflation.

Lowe's is more exposed to interest rates, home turnover, housing affordability,
weather, contractor liquidity, and deferred remodeling. Repair demand can be
necessary, but large projects can be postponed. A Pro network may be more
durable than casual DIY demand, but it can also transmit construction stress
through receivables, inventory, and project volume.

The Lyn Alden question is which company can maintain liquidity and service
quality through a demand shock. The Damodaran question is whether incremental
capital invested in advertising, membership, Pro, online, services, and
acquisitions earns more than the cost of capital after the full burden stack.

## Valuation expectations

For Walmart, the market must be underwriting continued retail scale plus a
higher-margin attached-services mix. The valuation should separate mature
retail earnings from advertising, membership, marketplace, and financial
services. The key expectation is higher cash per customer without weaker price,
availability, or store investment.

For Lowe's, the market must be underwriting normalized housing demand plus
better Pro, online, services, and loyalty economics. The key expectation is
that the existing store and distribution network produces more owner cash per
project without excessive acquisition, inventory, delivery, or installation
capital.

Applying the same multiple to both companies without adjusting for frequency,
capital burden, working capital, and attached-service risk would hide the
economic difference between them.

## Cross-company thesis breakers

| Question | Walmart confirmation | Lowe's confirmation | Common falsifier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Is the relationship strengthening? | Traffic, retention, availability, membership renewal, and basket improve | Pro retention, project frequency, online conversion, and service attachment improve | Activity grows while customer retention or contribution declines |
| Is attached revenue incremental? | Supplier conversion and member contribution improve after costs | Pro and service gross profit improves after delivery and claims | New revenue mainly shifts economics from suppliers/customers or requires permanent subsidy |
| Is cash durable? | OCF and FCF remain strong after normalized inventory, payables, capex, leases, and SBC | Cash remains strong after inventory, acquisitions, maintenance capex, debt, leases, and buybacks | Working-capital release or deferred maintenance drives the apparent improvement |
| Does capital earn a return? | Advertising, membership, marketplace, and fulfillment returns exceed network reinvestment | Pro, service, online, and acquisitions exceed the cost of capital | Sales or adjusted EPS grows while return on incremental capital falls |

## Conclusion

Walmart owns a higher-frequency household relationship with more opportunities
to attach advertising and membership. Lowe's owns a lower-frequency but more
project-specific relationship where Pro accounts and services can deepen the
customer connection. Walmart's main risk is the cost of maintaining low-price
scale; Lowe's main risk is housing-cycle, project-execution, and acquisition
burden.

The clean conclusion is not that one is automatically superior. It is that
the two companies require different denominators: Walmart needs cash per
household relationship after retail and fulfillment investment; Lowe's needs
cash per project and Pro relationship after inventory, delivery, service,
acquisition, and maintenance costs.
