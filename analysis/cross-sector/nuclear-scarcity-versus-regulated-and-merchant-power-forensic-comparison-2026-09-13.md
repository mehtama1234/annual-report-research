# Nuclear scarcity versus regulated and merchant power: who gets paid for reliability?

Date: 2026-09-13. This is a cohort comparison, not an investment recommendation.

## Business economics: the first-principles question

The large-load and AI-power narrative often treats “reliable clean power” as a
single scarce product. It is not. The economic claim can sit in a regulated
rate base, a merchant fleet, a nuclear plant with scarcity value, a contracted
PPA, or the customer interface that decides whether new generation gets built.
Those claims have different cash timing, financing, regulatory, outage, and
stranded-capital risks.

| Model | Control point | Who funds construction? | What the owner must prove |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regulated utility | Approved rate base and customer access | Ratepayers through tariffs, subject to regulatory approval | Earned return, affordability, timely recovery, prudent spending, and manageable leverage |
| Merchant nuclear / clean generation | Scarce dispatchable output and reliability attributes | Equity/debt plus contracts and market cash | Availability, hedge settlement, power-price durability, fuel, relicensing, decommissioning, and post-capex cash |
| Gas/merchant platform | Flexible capacity and dispatch economics | Corporate balance sheet, project finance, and market cash | Spark spreads, utilization, fuel/logistics, emissions, contract duration, and replacement capital |
| Customer-backed generation | Long-term PPA or large-load contract | Shared between generator and creditworthy customer if terms are real | Take-or-pay strength, customer contribution, interconnection milestones, curtailment, and stranded-asset allocation |

## Constellation as the forensic anchor

Constellation’s filed FY2025 bridge gives the cohort a useful denominator. It
reported $4.237B of operating cash flow, $2.949B of PP&E capex, and $14M of
acquisition cash, leaving a mechanical $1.274B residual before dividends,
buybacks, nuclear-fuel economics, decommissioning, and debt. The residual was
negative in both 2023 and 2024, and the filing period excludes the Calpine
acquisition that now supports much of the enlarged-platform thesis.

That does not make the business poor. It identifies the exact burden of proof.
At approximately $100.9B of equity value, the market is not merely paying for
FY2025 cash; it is paying for a materially larger post-Calpine denominator,
durable nuclear scarcity pricing, successful PPAs, and disciplined restart and
life-extension capital.

## The cash-quality distinctions

1. **Hedge marks are not settled cash.** Fair-value gains can improve earnings
   before the generation is delivered and cash is collected. A cohort model must
   reconcile marks, collateral, settlements, and physical output.
2. **Rate base is not free growth.** Regulated investment can earn an allowed
   return, but the customer funds it through bills and the equity holder still
   bears regulatory, affordability, construction, and financing risk.
3. **A PPA is not a risk-free asset.** The contract reduces price uncertainty;
   it does not eliminate restart capex, outage risk, customer credit, permitting,
   interconnection, or the possibility that the asset is built before demand is
   energized.
4. **Reliability attributes need a physical denominator.** Capacity, clean
   attributes, and scarcity pricing only become owner cash after maintenance,
   fuel, labor, safety, insurance, waste, decommissioning, and debt.
5. **Large-load growth can shift rather than remove risk.** The decisive term is
   who funds the network upgrade and who pays if the customer delays, curtails,
   or changes technology.

## Shared diligence template

For every utility or power-platform company, the next filing pass should extract:

- settled hedge cash versus unrealized marks;
- generation availability, outage duration, and maintenance capex;
- fuel, spent-fuel, decommissioning, and environmental obligations;
- customer concentration, contract tenor, take-or-pay terms, and credit support;
- interconnection and large-load milestones before construction spending;
- operating cash after maintenance and growth capex, not EBITDA alone;
- debt, preferred/NCI claims, tax-credit timing, and equity dilution;
- the return on the next dollar of rate base, restart, or merchant expansion.

## Falsification matrix

| Claim | Evidence that would support it | Evidence that would break it |
|---|---|---|
| Nuclear scarcity creates durable value | High availability, contracted cash, customer funding, and post-capex returns | Outages, relicensing/fuel burden, weak settlements, or customer contracts that do not cover renewal capital |
| Data-center demand funds new power | Take-or-pay contracts, deposits/contributions, energized load, and milestone-linked capex | Delays, curtailment rights, speculative builds, or generator-funded upgrades |
| Regulated growth compounds owner value | Timely recovery, earned returns above funding cost, and manageable customer bills | Construction disallowance, regulatory lag, debt-funded dilution, or affordability pressure |
| Merchant platform is durable | Mid-cycle cash after capex, diversified hedges, and strong fleet availability | One-cycle spread capture, rising maintenance/replacement cost, or leverage that requires high prices |

The cross-sector conclusion is narrow but important: “power scarcity” is not an
asset class. It is a set of contractual and physical arrangements. The best
research separates the party that owns the bottleneck from the party that funds
it, then values only the cash that survives the full arrangement.

## Definitions and the cash boundary

- **Rate base** is the utility investment that regulators allow to earn an
  approved return through customer rates. A larger rate base is not cash profit
  until the investment is placed in service, recovered, and financed at an
  acceptable cost.
- **Merchant generation** sells power into markets or under contracts whose
  economics depend on prices, fuel, hedges, and operating availability.
- **A PPA**, or power-purchase agreement, is a contract under which a customer
  agrees to buy power or related attributes. A PPA improves visibility but does
  not remove construction, outage, customer-credit, or renewal risk.
- **A hedge mark** is the accounting value of a derivative that protects a
  future price or volume. It can affect reported earnings before the related
  cash settles.
- **Capacity factor** measures how much electricity a plant produced compared
  with the maximum it could have produced. It is an operating indicator, not a
  profit measure.

The minimum cash chain is:

`load or reliability need -> contract / rate approval / market position -> generation and grid investment -> delivery and settlement -> maintenance, fuel, labor, debt, environmental, and regulatory claims -> common-owner cash`

The company that owns the power plant does not necessarily own the customer
relationship. The customer that signs a contract does not necessarily fund all
network upgrades. The model must identify both control and funding.

the annual reporting period.

## Constellation: scarce nuclear output and a new post-Calpine denominator

Constellation controls nuclear and clean-generation assets whose output can be
valuable when new reliable supply is slow to build. The Microsoft PPA supporting
the Crane restart shows the logic: a creditworthy customer can support a plant
investment and provide demand visibility.

The FY2025 cash bridge remains the anchor. OCF of $4.237B less $2.949B capex and
$14M acquisition cash left $1.274B. The improvement from negative 2023 and 2024
residuals must be decomposed into power prices, hedge settlements, receivables,
collateral, nuclear production, and working capital.

The current thesis adds Calpine, acquired in January 2026, after the FY2025
period. The relevant return test is post-acquisition cash after integration,
debt, fleet maintenance, hedge settlement, gas exposure, PPA obligations, and
the existing nuclear burden. A pro forma EBITDA number without pro forma cash
and capex is not enough.

Unbilled customer revenue of $1.305B is normal utility accounting, but it makes
the cash test important. Delivered or estimated energy is not the same as cash
collected. Track billing, settlement, receivables, collateral, and customer
credit as the large-load contracts develop.

## Vistra: merchant optionality with a thin current residual

Vistra combines retail load, dispatchable generation, nuclear assets, gas
plants, hedges, PPAs, and acquisitions. Retail load can offset generation
exposure, but it adds churn, bad debt, customer-service, collateral, and
affordability risk.

The $178M FY2025 screen is the important contradiction to the adjusted-FCF
narrative. Capex included nuclear-fuel purchases and long-term service-agreement
prepayments, and Lotus consumed $1.140B. Dividends and repurchases exceeded the
screen by more than $1.1B. The gap may have been funded by cash balances, hedge
collateral, asset transactions, debt, or expectations of future cash; the next
filings must show which.

Vistra's 2025 materials cite a large hedged position. Hedging can protect future
cash, but an unrealized loss or gain is not the same as settled operating cash.
The model should show generation availability, realized power and fuel
spreads, hedge settlement schedule, nuclear fuel, fleet maintenance, debt, and
asset-retirement obligations.

## NRG: customer access plus acquired generation

NRG controls a retail and home-services relationship alongside generation and
demand-response capabilities. Bring Your Own Power can make the customer part
of the capacity solution, but the economics depend on contract terms,
interconnection, equipment ownership, incentives, service costs, and customer
retention.

FY2025 OCF of $1.913B less $1.147B capex left $766M before LS Power, debt,
dividends, buybacks, and further investment. The LS Power acquisition added
approximately 13 GW of generation assets and CPower after the reporting period.
The acquired MW count is not a cash return. Track availability, realized
margin, maintenance capex, acquisition financing, integration cost, retail
receivables, and customer contributions.

## Alliant and NextEra: regulated recovery is a contract, not free cash

Alliant's rate-base model can create durable value when construction is prudent,
approved, placed in service, and recovered at an allowed return. Its FY2025
negative $1.314B post-investment screen is not by itself a failure. It shows
that the company needs external financing and future customer-rate recovery.

The questions are whether regulators permit the spending, whether customers
can afford the resulting bills, whether the project earns the allowed return,
and whether debt and equity issuance dilute or overburden common shareholders.
Large-load contracts can help, but the model must disclose energized load,
customer contributions, take-or-pay terms, interconnection milestones, and
stranded-capital allocation.

NextEra combines FPL regulated investment with NEER development and market
exposure. Its $24.606B capital investment exceeded OCF by a wide margin in
2025. Tax credits, project sales, partner capital, and future rate recovery can
make that rational, but the owner-cash test must identify who supplies each
dollar and when the asset begins earning.

## The three analytical lenses

### Alden: liquidity and physical scarcity

Reliable clean power can command value when grid connections, permitting,
transmission, fuel, and generation capacity are constrained. But the burden is
capital-intensive and policy-sensitive. Higher rates increase financing costs;
outages, fuel, safety, and environmental obligations can reduce availability;
and a large load can be delayed or become less power-intensive.

Constellation's scarce nuclear output has a durable demand case but a high
post-Calpine integration and maintenance burden. Vistra and NRG have more
merchant and retail exposure. Alliant and NextEra have greater regulatory
recovery visibility but depend on customer affordability, approval, and
financing access.

### Damodaran: separate the power engines

The model should value mature nuclear cash, contracted PPA cash, merchant
generation, retail margin, regulated rate base, tax credits, development
options, and acquisition cohorts separately. It must also forecast fuel,
maintenance, life extension, decommissioning, debt, collateral, required
capital, and dilution.

The narrative “AI needs reliable power” becomes value only when a customer pays
for the delivered output, the asset earns an adequate return after all capital,
and the contract allocates delay and stranded-asset risk fairly.

## Valuation expectations

The current scenario register and dossiers show distinct hurdles. Constellation's
approximately $100.9B equity value is about 79x the $1.274B FY2025 screen; the
price requires post-Calpine cash to rise materially, not merely nuclear demand
to remain strong. Vistra's approximately $50.3B equity value is about 283x its
$178M screen; the market requires a large normalization after acquisition,
hedge, and fleet investment. NRG's approximately $23.9B value is about 31x its
$766M standalone screen and requires LS Power/CPower integration. Alliant's
approximately $17.5B equity value cannot be judged on its negative screen; it
requires approved rate-base growth, financing discipline, and affordability.

The valuation denominators are therefore deliberately separate. A nuclear
scarcity multiple cannot be applied to a regulated rate-base cash flow, and a
merchant adjusted FCF measure cannot be treated as recurring fee income.

## Conclusion

Constellation owns scarce nuclear output, Vistra owns a merchant and retail
portfolio, NRG owns customer access plus acquired generation, and Alliant and
NextEra own regulated or development capital platforms. Their shared exposure
to electricity demand does not create a shared owner-cash denominator.

The strongest conclusion is conditional: power scarcity creates value only when
the physical asset is available, the contract or rate structure pays for its
full burden, and the resulting cash survives fuel, maintenance, regulation,
financing, acquisition, and dilution claims.
