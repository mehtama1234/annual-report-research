# Physical Content, Optical Control, and Validation Economics

Date baseline: `2026-09-14`

This comparison links three different ways of monetizing rising system
complexity:

- [Amphenol](../deep-company-pages/amphenol-corporation.md) sells connectors,
  cables, fiber interconnects, power systems, antennas, and sensors.
- [Corning](../deep-company-pages/corning-inc.md) sells optical fiber,
  connectivity, glass, and engineered materials while carrying a larger
  manufacturing and capacity burden.
- [Keysight](../deep-company-pages/keysight-technologies-inc.md) sells the
  measurement and validation systems needed to qualify increasingly fast,
  dense, and complex networks and devices.

The common question is not whether all three benefit from data growth. It is
whether the benefit becomes collected owner cash after the specific burden each
company must fund.

## Business economics: the economic chain

| Position | Company | What the customer is paying for | Main burden carried by the company |
|---|---|---|---|
| Optical medium and material | Corning | Fiber, glass, optical performance, and qualified manufacturing capacity | Plants, yield, inventory, capex, customer concentration, pensions, environmental obligations |
| Interconnect and system content | Amphenol | Reliable signal, power, cable, connector, antenna, and sensor content inside a qualified system | Manufacturing, inventory, tariffs, customer concentration, and repeated acquisition spending |
| Validation and assurance | Keysight | Proof that systems meet performance, compliance, and reliability requirements before deployment | R&D, product transitions, acquisition integration, deferred revenue obligations, SBC, and customer capex cycles |

This is a functional sequence, not a claim that one company is upstream of all
the others in every product. A single data-center or network program may use
all three, but their cash conversion depends on different events: Corning must
run capacity profitably, Amphenol must win and retain qualified content, and
Keysight must remain necessary when standards and system complexity change.

## 2. Cash quality and reinvestment

| Test | Amphenol | Corning | Keysight |
|---|---|---|---|
| Latest annual sales anchor | FY2025 sales `$23.1B` | FY2025 GAAP sales `$15.63B`; core sales `$16.41B` | FY2025 sales `$5.375B` |
| Annual operating cash flow | `$5.375B` | `$2.695B` | `$1.409B` |
| Annual capex | `$996.6M` | `$1.282B` | Approximately `$128M` of PP&E additions used in the current screen |
| Mechanical cash after capex | Approximately `$4.378B` | Approximately `$1.413B` | Approximately `$1.281B` |
| Item that changes owner-cash interpretation | Acquisition cash of `$3.819B` | Planned 2026 capex near `$1.7B`, plus working-capital and claim burden | Spirent acquisition cash, integration, amortization, and SBC |

The mechanical residual is only a starting point. Amphenol's owner-cash test
must charge acquisition cash because M&A is a recurring part of its operating
model. Corning's residual must be tested against sustaining versus expansion
capex, utilization, and future delivery obligations. Keysight's lighter
physical-capital burden does not remove the need to charge acquisition price,
integration cost, amortization, and stock compensation.

## 3. Revenue quality

### Amphenol

FY2025 organic growth was `38%`, while reported growth was `52%`. Q2 2026
reported sales rose `55%` and organic growth was `30%`, with orders of `$10.7B`
and book-to-bill of `1.23:1`. The evidence supports strong underlying demand,
but the CommScope CCS acquisition and the Q2 `$80M` net tariff-recovery benefit
must be separated from recurring product economics. The central test is whether
organic growth and margin remain strong after the acquired revenue and policy
benefit roll off.

### Corning

Q2 2026 core sales reached `$4.74B`; Optical Communications sales were
`$2.07B`, up `32%`, and Enterprise Networks growth was `65%`. Multiyear
agreements and customer-supported capacity improve visibility. They do not by
themselves prove that Corning will earn an adequate return on new plants. The
central test is whether utilization, price, mix, and cash conversion keep pace
with capacity spending after the initial optical surge.

### Keysight

FY2025 revenue was `$5.375B`; Q3 FY2026 revenue reached `$1.85B` and orders
exceeded `$2B`. Keysight's risk is less about factory utilization and more about
whether acquired capabilities and new measurement products remain necessary as
standards evolve. The Spirent acquisition produced only `$9M` of post-close
revenue in the reported period and included a `$40M` net loss including
discontinued operations, so headline strategic fit must be tested against
post-acquisition revenue, margin, and cash.

## 4. Who controls the relationship and who carries the burden?

| Question | Amphenol | Corning | Keysight |
|---|---|---|---|
| Valuable relationship | Design-in and qualification with equipment and system customers | Long-term optical, materials, and capacity relationship with large customers | Engineering workflow and standards-compliance relationship with test users |
| Customer switching friction | High for high-speed, defense, aerospace, automotive, and power applications; lower for generic connectors | High when performance, quality, and supply assurance matter; still exposed to customer pricing power | High when tools, workflows, data, and expertise are embedded; product cycles can reset the relationship |
| Main source of pricing power | Specification, reliability, signal integrity, and content per system | Materials science, process know-how, yield, and qualified capacity | Measurement accuracy, installed workflows, software, and standards expertise |
| Main capital burden | Manufacturing plus repeated acquisitions | Plants, capacity expansion, inventory, pensions, and environmental claims | R&D, product transitions, acquisitions, and SBC rather than heavy plant capex |
| Main demand risk | Hyperscale/datacom cycle and acquisition-assisted growth | Optical build timing, utilization, and customer concentration | Customer test budgets, standards cycles, and acquisition execution |

The cleaner economics are not determined by gross margin alone. Corning can
have a valuable physical position while still producing a lower return if it
adds capacity before demand is collected. Amphenol can generate high cash flow
while reducing per-share value if acquisition prices rise faster than acquired
returns. Keysight can look asset-light while losing owner economics through
acquisition integration, SBC, or a product cycle that weakens pricing.

## 5. Forensic tests that distinguish the businesses

### Test A: growth versus purchased growth

- Amphenol: reconcile organic growth, CommScope revenue, price, mix, and
  tariff recovery by segment.
- Corning: reconcile optical growth with customer agreements, capacity coming
  online, utilization, and customer deposits.
- Keysight: reconcile acquired revenue and cross-selling with organic orders,
  deferred revenue, and integration cost.

### Test B: cash versus accounting profit

- Amphenol: compare operating cash and cash after capex with acquisition cash,
  inventory, receivables, SBC, and diluted shares.
- Corning: compare GAAP operating cash with adjusted free cash flow after
  translated-contract gains, deposits, incentives, capex, pensions, and
  environmental cash.
- Keysight: compare operating cash with capex, acquisition cash, deferred
  revenue movements, acquired intangibles, amortization, SBC, and dilution.

### Test C: customer visibility versus customer bargaining power

Orders, agreements, and backlog are useful only when they can be reconciled to
price, margin, shipment, collection, and return on invested capital. A large
customer commitment can improve planning while still giving the customer power
over price, capacity, or delivery terms.

### Test D: durability after the current cycle

- Amphenol must show organic growth and acquisition returns after exceptional
  datacom conditions normalize.
- Corning must show that new optical capacity earns acceptable returns through
  a slower utilization environment.
- Keysight must show that validation remains necessary through a standards or
  customer-capex pause and that acquired products remain integrated and useful.

## 6. Alden and Damodaran synthesis

From a liquidity and durability perspective, Amphenol has the broadest end
market spread but the most explicit acquisition cash requirement. Corning has
the strongest physical manufacturing exposure and therefore the greatest
operating leverage to utilization and capacity timing. Keysight has the lowest
plant burden but remains exposed to engineering-budget cycles, acquisitions,
and the need to keep its tools relevant.

From a narrative-to-numbers perspective, each story requires a different
denominator:

- Amphenol: normalized owner cash after acquisition spending per diluted share.
- Corning: normalized owner cash after productive capex, working capital, and
  claims per diluted share.
- Keysight: normalized owner cash after acquisition integration, SBC, and
  product-cycle R&D per diluted share.

The same AI or bandwidth growth rate cannot be applied to all three companies.
The value of the growth depends on who must fund the next unit of capacity,
whether the customer captures the benefit through pricing, and whether the
growth creates cash after the company-specific reinvestment burden.

## Valuation: technical content after capacity and acquisition cost

Value Amphenol, Corning, and Keysight from the cash each business retains after
the cost of preserving its control point. That means separating organic growth
from acquisitions, customer deposits, inventory and receivables, manufacturing
capacity, R&D, warranty, integration cost, stock compensation, and diluted
shares. Corning's optical capacity and Keysight's test systems require a more
explicit replacement and utilization case than a pure recurring software
multiple.

Use normal, weak-demand, capacity-overbuild, and successful-acquisition cases.
The current valuation is demanding if it assumes technical complexity will
raise pricing while acquisition cash, capital spending, and dilution remain
unchanged. The useful denominator is owner cash per continuing share after the
company funds the equipment, people, and product renewal that keep customers
dependent.

| Keysight | Spirent revenue and margins, deferred-revenue conversion, orders, SBC and diluted shares, customer test-budget resilience | Acquired capabilities fail to produce cash or measurement tools become less central during a product or standards transition |

## Conclusion

Amphenol, Corning, and Keysight all benefit from rising technical complexity,
but they do not represent the same investment. Amphenol is a diversified
content-and-acquisition compounder. Corning is an optical materials and
manufacturing capacity case. Keysight is a validation and engineering-workflow
case.

The useful cross-company conclusion is that physical control does not remove
financial risk. The analyst must identify the exact point of customer
dependence, then subtract the capital, inventory, acquisition, policy, and
dilution burden required to maintain it. The next filings should be read for
that conversion, not for headline growth alone.
