# Process control versus application control

Date: 2026-09-13

## The comparison

KLA and F5 both sell control layers that customers may find difficult to
replace. KLA helps semiconductor manufacturers detect defects and protect
manufacturing yield. F5 helps enterprises keep applications available and
secure. Both sit between a customer's critical workflow and a costly failure,
but their cash economics differ substantially.

The comparison asks:

1. what each company actually controls;
2. whether the control point creates recurring revenue or only intermittent
   product demand;
3. how much capital and technical labor must be reinvested;
4. whether reported cash is supported by collections and delivered service; and
5. what the current valuation requires.

## Definitions

- **Yield** is the proportion of manufactured semiconductor units that meet
  required specifications. KLA's tools help customers find process problems
  that reduce yield.
- **Installed base** is the equipment or software already deployed at customer
  sites. An installed base can create service or renewal revenue, but only if
  customers continue using and maintaining it.
- **Deferred revenue** is cash or an invoiced amount recorded before the company
  has delivered the promised product or service. It provides visibility but
  also represents work and cost still owed to the customer.
- **SBC**, or stock-based compensation, is employee compensation paid through
  shares or options. It is a recorded expense and can also dilute existing
  shareholders when new shares are issued.

## Control point and burden map

| Company | Control point | Customer failure avoided | Main recurring burden |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| KLA | Inspection, metrology, process control, and yield analysis embedded in semiconductor production | Defects, lower yield, delayed production, and inability to qualify complex processes | Semiconductor-capex cycles, R&D, technical labor, service response, inventory, warranty, export rules, and customer concentration |
| F5 | Application delivery, API management, security, and hybrid-cloud operation | Application outage, security breach, poor traffic handling, and fragmented tooling | Software development, support labor, cloud hosting, cyber remediation, service delivery, customer trust, SBC, and cloud-native competition |

KLA's control point is validated by technical qualification and measurement
accuracy. F5's control point is validated by reliable deployment, security, and
renewal inside the customer's application environment. Neither company
controls the customer's entire end market.

## KLA: technical qualification with semiconductor-cycle exposure

KLA's operating chain is:

`semiconductor complexity -> defect and yield risk -> measurement and correction -> qualified tool installed -> service and renewal -> customer cash savings`

FY2026 product revenue was $10.454B and service revenue was $3.126B. Service
revenue grew 16%, faster than the installed-control question would require if
the installed base were merely a maintenance obligation. Gross margin was
61.3%, up from 60.9% in FY2025.

The positive interpretation is that advanced logic, memory, and packaging make
measurement more valuable and difficult to replace. The cautious interpretation
is that product demand is still linked to customers' fab and equipment budgets.
KLA can be essential to a fab program and still experience a material revenue
decline when customers delay the program.

The FY2026 cash flow also requires interpretation. Receivables used $642M,
inventory used $466M, accounts payable supplied $172M, and deferred system
revenue supplied $116M while deferred service revenue used $55M. These are
ordinary operating movements, not evidence of manipulation. They show that
growth requires customer collections, inventory, supplier timing, and contract
delivery to remain aligned.

KLA recorded $310M of SBC and repurchased $2.290B of stock. A buyback may
reduce the share count, but it does not remove the economic cost of stock
awards. The proper per-share test charges R&D, technical labor, service
capacity, SBC dilution, and peak-cycle repurchase prices against the cash
surplus.

KLA's risk is therefore not that the process-control relationship is imaginary.
The risk is that a high-quality control point is valued as if semiconductor
capital spending, customer concentration, export access, gross margin, and
service growth will remain favorable at the same time.

## F5: application control with trust and labor exposure

F5's operating chain is:

`application complexity -> outage and security risk -> F5 software/system/service layer -> customer uptime and protection -> renewal and expansion -> cash`

F5's FY2025 revenue included approximately $705.6M of systems revenue,
$803.1M of software revenue, and $1.579B of services revenue. The approximately
81.4% gross margin is attractive, but the services share matters. Recurring
revenue is higher quality when it expands platform retention without requiring
proportionate support labor, hosting, and implementation cost.

Deferred revenue ended at approximately $1.999B, with $1.344B of additions not
yet recognized and $1.144B recognized from opening balances. This is contracted
visibility, not completed economic profit. F5 must deliver support, software,
cloud, and managed services before the amount becomes earned revenue, and the
associated personnel and hosting cost must be paid.

The October 2025 cyber incident is unusually important because F5 sells trust,
availability, and security. The disclosed access and exfiltration may produce
remediation, customer, insurance, regulatory, and retention costs. Calling the
initial investigation a one-time item would not resolve the possibility that
secure development, testing, support, and insurance costs remain structurally
higher.

F5 paid $145.2M for CalypsoAI. The strategic rationale is application and AI-
inference security, but the return is unproven until acquired revenue,
customer retention, attach rates, gross profit, integration spending, and
impairment risk become visible.

F5 recorded $231.5M of SBC and repurchased $502.1M of stock. The per-share
question is whether the buyback creates value after employee share issuance,
cyber investment, services labor, and acquisition spending.

## Direct framework comparison

### Alden: liquidity and durability

KLA has high margins, cash, and service revenue, but its customers fund large
semiconductor projects. A capex pause, export restriction, or loss of a major
Asian customer can reduce demand quickly. KLA's liquidity is strong only if
technical labor, R&D, service response, debt, and purchase commitments remain
funded through the cycle.

F5 has lower physical-capital needs and approximately $1.36B of cash and
investments, making its direct balance-sheet liquidity more resilient. Its
durability depends on customer trust, renewal, support capacity, and the
ability to compete with cloud-native and hyperscaler tools. A cyber event can
weaken the control point without first appearing as a large balance-sheet loss.

### Damodaran: narrative to numbers

KLA's narrative requires assumptions for product growth, service revenue per
installed system, gross margin, R&D, technical labor, capex, customer
concentration, export controls, working capital, SBC, and the normalized
semiconductor cycle.

F5's narrative requires assumptions for systems/software/services mix, renewal,
deferred-revenue conversion, RPO, hosting and support cost, cyber remediation,
CalypsoAI attach, R&D, SBC, and cloud-native competition.

KLA's lifecycle is a mature technical franchise with continuing R&D and service
reinvestment. F5 is a platform transition in which the market expects software
and AI-security growth to replace or improve the systems-and-services mix.

### Forensic earnings quality

| Test | KLA | F5 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Revenue timing | Customer-control and allocation judgments; system and service deferrals | Delivery across systems, software, cloud, managed services, and support obligations |
| Cash timing | Receivables, inventory, payables, and deferred revenue | Deferred revenue, RPO, unbilled receivables, contract costs, and collections |
| Main adjustment risk | Warranty, tariffs, inventory, impairments, SBC, and peak-cycle buybacks | Cyber, acquisition, service labor, SBC, and recurring non-GAAP adjustments |
| Customer risk | Semiconductor capex, TSMC/concentration, geography, export restrictions | Retention, trust after cyber incident, vendor consolidation, cloud-native substitution |
| Falsifier | Service and margin weaken with capex cycle; R&D and SBC absorb cash | Renewals weaken, services become labor-heavy, or deferred revenue fails to convert profitably |

## Valuation expectations

The current scenario register assigns KLA bear/base/bull normalized cash of
$2.5B/$3.8B/$5.0B at 35x/45x/55x, against an approximate $238.4B equity value.
The base case is below the market value, so the price requires continued
service growth, high margins, AI/HPC and advanced-packaging demand, and a
semiconductor cycle that does not impose a large cash reset.

F5's scenario register assigns $550M/$750M/$950M at 30x/32x/35x, against an
approximate $23.7B equity value. The base case is near the market value, so the
price requires software and security renewal, profitable services, cyber-trust
repair, CalypsoAI integration, and no material dilution or margin deterioration.

The valuation denominators are not interchangeable. KLA needs a cycle-normalized
technical franchise model. F5 needs a recurring-control model that proves
services and deferred revenue produce profit after labor and trust costs.

## Conclusion

KLA appears to control a harder-to-replace technical qualification point, but
its demand is tied to customer semiconductor investment. F5 controls a useful
application and security workflow, but its economic quality depends more
directly on trust, renewal, services labor, and software delivery.

KLA currently offers the stronger control-point evidence but carries the higher
cycle valuation burden. F5 offers lower physical-capital intensity and a lower
market screen, but the cyber event and mixed systems/services model require
more proof that recurring revenue is profitable and durable.

Neither conclusion is established by revenue growth alone. The next filings
must show whether each control point produces cash after the specific costs
required to keep customers dependent on it.
