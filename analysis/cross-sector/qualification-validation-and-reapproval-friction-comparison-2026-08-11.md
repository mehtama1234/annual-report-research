# Qualification, Validation, And Reapproval Friction Comparison

Date: 2026-08-11
Repo: `annual-report-research`

## Plain Claim

Some businesses become sticky not because the customer loves switching costs in the abstract, but because replacing the product or platform means re-validating a critical workflow.

That re-validation can involve:

- technical qualification
- regulatory comfort
- clinical acceptance
- workflow retraining
- systems interoperability
- trust recovery

When reapproval is painful enough, the incumbent can hold a stronger position than its size alone suggests.

## Why This Comparison Matters

The archive already has strong pages on:

- point-of-use control
- narrow chokepoints
- trust and proof
- standardization

What it did not yet have as a direct comparison is a memo centered on reapproval friction itself.

This matters because some moats are not mainly:

- brand love
- scale alone
- physical asset ownership
- simple network effects

They come from the fact that the customer does not want to reopen a validated system unless the benefit is overwhelming.

## What The Comparison Clarifies

### 1. Switching cost is often too vague a phrase.

These companies are better understood through reapproval friction.

The customer may not stay because moving is annoying.

The customer may stay because moving means reopening:

- validation work
- training work
- safety work
- workflow risk
- governance risk

That is a more concrete explanation than generic customer stickiness.

### 2. Reapproval friction shows up in both physical and digital systems.

West and Aptar show it in regulated physical interfaces.

KLA shows it in advanced manufacturing precision.

Intuitive and BD show it in hospital workflow and procedure systems.

CrowdStrike shows it in enterprise trust and security control.

The bigger pattern is that the moat is often strongest where the customer has already accepted the system into a sensitive operating environment.

### 3. The pain is rarely only technical.

Changing vendors may also mean:

- retraining clinicians
- reconfiguring software
- re-documenting workflow
- retesting patient-safety systems
- risking process drift
- exposing the organization to new failure modes

That means the incumbent can keep an edge even if a nominal alternative exists.

### 4. Installed bases become more powerful when they are also approved systems.

Intuitive is not only an installed base story.

It is an installed base plus procedural standardization story.

BD is not only a medical-products story.

It is a workflow-approval and interoperability story.

CrowdStrike is not only a subscription story.

It is a trusted-control-fabric story.

The shared lesson is that approved installed systems can become harder to displace than ordinary recurring vendors.

### 5. This kind of moat is easy to miss in taxonomy.

West can look like supplies.

Aptar can look like packaging.

KLA can look like equipment.

BD can look like a diversified device maker.

CrowdStrike can look like another software platform.

The real question is:

how much work does the customer have to redo if it wants to replace the layer?

## Stronger Conclusion

The stronger conclusion is that some of the archive's best businesses are protected by the cost of reopening a trusted system.

That cost can be technical, clinical, operational, or governance-related.

Once the layer is:

- validated
- integrated
- trained into workflow
- trusted by operators

the replacement decision becomes much heavier than a normal purchase comparison.

That is why qualification, validation, and reapproval friction deserve to be treated as a distinct moat type in the archive.

## Aha Moments

### 1. West and CrowdStrike belong in the same conversation.

One lives in sterile therapy delivery.

The other lives in digital security control.

Both benefit because customers do not want to reopen a sensitive trust decision without strong reason.

### 2. Intuitive and BD show that hospital moats are often workflow-approval systems, not only product systems.

The installed device matters, but the larger moat is often the surrounding clinical routine and interoperability.

### 3. Aptar looks much more valuable once packaging is reframed as approved product functionality.

The packet is not really about packaging volume alone.

It is about keeping a trusted delivery and dosing behavior intact.

### 4. KLA proves that advanced manufacturing moats can look like technical routine from the outside.

Inside the customer workflow, that routine is exactly what makes the supplier hard to displace.

## Thesis Breakers

This qualification-and-reapproval-friction thesis weakens if:

- customers begin changing providers without major workflow pain
- regulation or standardization makes these layers more interchangeable
- interoperability improves enough to reduce incumbent advantage materially
- trust shocks or product failures make reapproval costs worth paying
- the economic value of the validated layer falls faster than the cost of replacing it
