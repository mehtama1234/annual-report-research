# Regulated Wires Versus Large-Load Growth: Exelon and AEP

Date baseline: `2026-09-14`

Evidence base: Exelon and American Electric Power fiscal `2025` annual filings,
fiscal `2025` Q4, fiscal `2026` Q1 and Q2 materials, and the corresponding
company dossiers. This comparison uses reported figures and management
disclosures from the archive. It does not use a current market-price feed.

## Executive conclusion

Exelon and AEP both own regulated electric networks, but their current growth
stories emphasize different evidence:

- Exelon emphasizes regulated transmission and distribution, recovery coverage,
  reliability, and a `$41.7B` four-year capital plan.
- AEP emphasizes large-load demand, `69 GW` of signed load by 2030, a `$78B`
  five-year capital plan, new generation capacity, and rate-base expansion.

Exelon is the cleaner test of recovery architecture. AEP is the stronger test of
demand upside and the risk that demand encourages capital spending before the
customer, regulator, and financing chain is fully secured.

Neither company's headline growth measure is shareholder cash. In both cases,
the required chain is:

`customer need -> approved investment -> construction -> in-service asset -> rate recovery -> earned return -> common cash per share`

The main conclusion is conditional. Large-load demand can improve the utility
system's growth opportunity, but it also increases construction, generation,
transmission, financing, and affordability risk. Recovery coverage and earned
returns matter more than the size of the load announcement.

## 1. Control point comparison

| Company | Primary control point | What the customer depends on | Main burden carrier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exelon | Regulated urban and regional transmission/distribution networks | Reliable local service, restoration, grid modernization, and approved connection | Shareholders finance construction; ratepayers fund recovery; regulators determine prudence and timing |
| AEP | Regulated transmission/distribution plus generation and large-load interconnection | Power availability, new connections, transmission, generation adequacy, and reliability | Shareholders, lenders, ratepayers, and large customers share construction and financing burden |

Exelon's control point is more clearly the regulated wires relationship. AEP has
that relationship but adds a more aggressive supply-and-load conversion task.

The residual is operating cash flow less the reported construction or capital
spending measure. It is a diagnostic, not a company-defined free-cash-flow
measure. It answers a simple question: did current operating cash fund the asset
investment required in the period? For both companies, the answer was no in all
three years.

That does not invalidate regulated-utility economics. It changes the valuation
question from “what is free cash flow today?” to “does future regulated recovery
create enough common return to justify the financing and dilution required
today?”

## 3. Demand visibility versus recovery visibility

| Evidence | Exelon | AEP | Correct interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capital program | `$41.7B` four-year plan | `$78B` five-year plan | Intended investment, not yet earned return |
| Rate-base growth | Expected `7.9%` | Nearly `11%` from new additions in management framing | Asset growth that requires approval and financing |
| Recovery coverage | Close to `90%` of rate base covered by established mechanisms through 2026–2027 | State-by-state recovery and customer-load structures | Recovery architecture is more important than project count |
| Large-load evidence | Less central to the thesis | `69 GW` signed load by 2030 | Signed demand is not energized, collected, or necessarily permanent |
| Transmission opportunity | Additional `$12B`–`$17B` beyond base plan | More than `$10B` beyond the capital plan | Optionality requires project approval, execution, and funding |

Exelon has stronger stated recovery visibility. AEP has greater demand and
capital upside, but more steps must be completed before the demand becomes
common-shareholder economics.

## 4. Earnings quality and financial-engineering tests

### Exelon

Exelon's main accounting question is whether regulatory assets, recovery
mechanisms, and adjusted operating earnings represent future collections that
will earn more than their financing and operating cost. A regulatory asset is a
cost recorded for expected future recovery; it is not current cash.

The tests are:

- regulatory assets and liabilities by major category;
- approved versus pending recovery;
- regulatory lag and disallowance history;
- interest and debt growth relative to rate base;
- storm, pension, environmental, and reliability cost recurrence; and
- common cash per diluted share after dividends and capital spending.

### AEP

AEP has the same regulated-accounting questions plus a more demanding load-
conversion question. Its cost-offsets, federal loans, grants, customer
contributions, and large-load agreements must be tracked from announcement to
cash funding.

The tests are:

- executed and credit-supported load versus signed load;
- customer deposits and contributions;
- turbine procurement versus energized demand;
- construction cost and schedule variance;
- rate recovery by state and project;
- debt and equity issuance needed to fund the plan; and
- affordability and political resistance to rate increases.

## 5. Alden lens: durability and liquidity

Both companies benefit from essential electricity demand and regulated customer
relationships. Exelon has lower narrative dependence on a single AI or data-
center cycle. AEP has greater exposure to a potential multi-year power-demand
cycle, but it must fund more growth before the economic return is proven.

The liquidity stress test is:

1. interest rates rise while construction spending accelerates;
2. load projects are delayed or customers reduce commitments;
3. regulators allow only part of the planned investment;
4. reliability, storm, or environmental costs rise; and
5. equity issuance becomes necessary at a price that weakens per-share value.

Exelon is more resilient if recovery coverage remains high and capital growth is
steady. AEP can outperform if demand converts cleanly, but it has more ways for
the capital program to outrun collected revenue.

## 6. Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers

| Narrative | Exelon evidence required | AEP evidence required |
|---|---|---|
| Rate-base growth | Assets in service, allowed ROE, earned ROE, recovery timing | Same, plus new-load-related assets and customer funding |
| Transmission opportunity | Approved projects, cost, in-service dates, recovery | Same, with more interconnection and generation dependence |
| Reliability | Outage performance, restoration cost, capital productivity | Same, plus adequacy and large-load reliability requirements |
| Customer growth | Bill growth, affordability, collections, political support | Large-load credit, deposits, executed contracts, and residential bill impact |
| Earnings growth | GAAP-to-adjusted bridge, regulatory items, financing | Same, plus tax credits, grants, cost offsets, and generation economics |
| Per-share value | Debt, equity issuance, dividends, and common cash | Same, with higher risk of capital-plan funding and dilution |

The lifecycle question differs. Exelon is testing whether steady regulated wires
investment can compound through a large installed base. AEP is testing whether
new demand justifies a larger system before the full customer and regulatory
economics are visible.

## 7. Who captures the value and who funds it?

| Layer | Cleaner economics | Burden |
|---|---|---|
| Existing regulated wires | Exelon, if recovery remains established and reliability stays strong | Construction, debt, regulatory lag, storms, and customer affordability |
| Large-load interconnection | AEP, if customers provide credit support and fund an appropriate share | Generation, transmission, turbines, delays, stranded-capital risk, and rate politics |
| Transmission expansion | Both companies if projects are approved and earn allowed returns | Siting, permitting, construction, financing, and customer contributions |
| Customer relationship | Both have essential local access | Regulators and customers constrain pricing and affordability |
| Capital plan | Future earnings visibility | Debt, equity dilution, interest, labor, materials, and execution |

The cleaner economics sit with approved, in-service, recoverable assets. The
heavier burden sits with proposed projects, unenergized load, and capital that
must be financed before recovery is certain.

## 8. Valuation and market expectations

The archive now preserves utility-specific valuation screens for both companies.
They are not ordinary post-capital-spending owner-cash screens because the
current construction deficit is part of the regulated growth mechanism. The
correct comparison therefore uses risk-adjusted common earnings and rate-base
conversion, not a software cash multiple:

`equity value -> rate base -> allowed return -> earned return -> financing and dilution -> common earnings and cash`

For Exelon, the key valuation inputs are recovery coverage, capital-plan
conversion, allowed and earned ROE, and financing cost. For AEP, the inputs add
executed load, customer contributions, generation cost, and the probability that
`69 GW` of signed demand becomes paying demand.

| Company | Current equity-value screen | Bear / base / bull common-earnings cases | Implied value at the registered multiples | What the base case requires |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Exelon | `$44.3B` | `$1.8B / $2.6B / $3.4B` | `$21.6B / $39.0B / $61.2B` at `12x / 15x / 18x` | Approved wires investment enters rate base, recovery remains high, earned returns cover financing, and dilution stays contained |
| AEP | `$67.9B` | `$1.8B / $3.0B / $4.5B` | `$21.6B / $45.0B / $81.0B` at `12x / 15x / 18x` | Signed large load becomes credit-supported and energized, customer contributions reduce funding burden, and the capital plan earns recoverable returns |

These are expectation screens, not price targets. The implied-value ranges are
deliberately not presented as a ranking: Exelon has more recovery visibility,
while AEP needs more demand conversion and funding proof. The screen also does
not turn regulatory assets, signed load, or rate base into current cash.

The market is paying for future regulated earnings in both cases. AEP's market
expectation should carry a larger discount for load conversion and capital
funding until the filings show energized, credit-supported, and recoverable
projects.

## 9. Next-filing watchlist

### Exelon

1. Does the `90%` recovery-coverage statement remain valid?
2. Does the `$41.7B` plan enter service on schedule and within cost?
3. Does transmission optionality become approved projects?
4. Does debt remain proportionate to rate base and earnings?
5. Does common cash per diluted share support dividends after capital spending?

### AEP

1. What portion of `69 GW` is executed, credit-supported, and energized?
2. Do customer contributions and federal support reduce equity funding?
3. Does the `$78B` plan remain within schedule and budget?
4. Do turbine purchases become productive assets or idle capacity?
5. Does rate recovery keep pace with debt and construction cost?
6. Does large-load growth improve affordability for existing customers?

The comparison thesis breaks if Exelon's recovery coverage weakens or if AEP's
load pipeline fails to become collected revenue while capital spending,
financing, and customer bills continue to rise.

## Conclusion

Exelon and AEP show two forms of regulated utility growth. Exelon offers more
recovery visibility through an established wires platform. AEP offers more
large-load upside but requires more proof that customer demand, generation,
transmission, regulation, and financing will align.

The durable control point is not the largest capital plan or load announcement.
It is the set of assets that are in service, approved for recovery, financed at
acceptable cost, reliable for customers, and capable of producing common cash
per diluted share after the burden is paid.
