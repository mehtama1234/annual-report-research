# Rental access versus ownership burden: forensic comparison

Date baseline: 2026-09-13. This comparison adds United Rentals to the
industrial distribution, contractor, and energy-infrastructure work. It tests
whether outsourcing physical ownership creates cleaner economics or merely
concentrates utilization and capital risk in the access provider.

## The lane question

Who owns the valuable relationship when customers need physical capability:
the distributor supplying parts, the contractor executing the project, or the
rental platform supplying the equipment? The access model can be strategically
valuable without being asset-light.

| Company type | Relationship control | Burden carried | FY2025 cash signal |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Fastenal / Grainger | Embedded procurement and replenishment workflow | Inventory, branches, service labor, working capital | Strong post-capex residuals, with different margin and inventory risks |
| WESCO | Project distribution and supplier access | Receivables, project inventory, debt, acquisitions | Negative residual after capex and acquisitions |
| Quanta / EMCOR / Comfort | Field execution and skilled labor | Project estimates, labor, equipment, retainage, claims | Wide spread between backlog visibility and owner cash |
| United Rentals | Equipment access, fleet availability, logistics, specialty capability | Fleet capex, maintenance, utilization, resale values, financing | $2.181B management FCF; about $1.824B after FY2025 acquisitions |

United Rentals is the clearest case where “outsourcing ownership” strengthens
the customer relationship but leaves the provider carrying the physical asset
and cycle burden. Its FY2025 revenue was $16.099B and OCF was $5.190B, yet
management-defined FCF was $2.181B after fleet and non-fleet investment.

## Control point versus burden transfer

The cross-sector chain is:

`project demand -> equipment / parts / labor need -> access or procurement interface -> physical execution -> collected cash`

Fastenal and Grainger monetize availability and workflow. WESCO finances more
project-specific inventory and receivables. Contractors monetize labor,
coordination, and estimates. United Rentals monetizes the option to access
equipment without customer ownership, but it must keep the fleet productive and
replace it before availability or safety deteriorates.

This creates an important correction to “asset-light” thinking: a company can
be asset-light for its customer while being asset-heavy for shareholders.

## Cash and earnings-quality comparison

United Rentals' filing-defined bridge is unusually useful:

| Item | FY2025 |
|---|---:|
| OCF | $5.190B |
| Rental-equipment purchases | $(4.149B) |
| Non-rental equipment and intangible purchases | $(379M) |
| Sale proceeds and insurance proceeds | $1.519B |
| Management FCF | $2.181B |
| Acquisitions | $(357M) |
| Residual after acquisitions | ~$1.824B |

The risk is not that the FCF definition is hidden; the 10-K discloses it. The
risk is interpretation. Rental-equipment purchases include growth and
replacement. Sale proceeds can be favorable in a tight used-equipment market.
Strong utilization can reflect a high-demand cycle rather than a permanent
increase in economic returns. A durable thesis therefore needs fleet-age,
utilization, rate, productivity, sale-gain, and maintenance-capex evidence.

WESCO offers the opposite contradiction: project demand and backlog can be
strong while receivables and inventory absorb cash. United Rentals can produce
better cash conversion, but only if fleet returns and resale economics persist.
Contractors add a third burden: customer-funded contract liabilities can support
cash, but cost-to-cost estimates, retainage, claims, and labor remain real.

## Alden lens

The access model benefits from durable infrastructure and industrial demand,
but is highly sensitive to liquidity. In a tightening cycle, customers may rent
more rather than buy, which supports the access thesis; they may also delay
projects, reduce utilization, default, and flood the used-equipment market,
which stresses the provider. The same macro shock can therefore create a
relative advantage and an absolute cash-flow problem.

The key Alden test is balance-sheet resilience: can United Rentals fund fleet
renewal and debt service through lower utilization without relying on peak
resale values or buybacks? Can distributors reduce inventory and receivable
exposure? Can contractors preserve labor and project execution while customers
slow payments?

## Damodaran lens

The narrative-to-numbers translation for United Rentals is:

`fleet density and specialty relevance -> utilization / rental rate -> gross margin -> maintenance and growth fleet capex -> through-cycle owner cash`

The lifecycle question is whether scale has moved the company into a mature,
high-return fleet-replacement phase or whether large projects are still pulling
forward growth capex. The valuation register uses $1.5B/$2.2B/$3.0B bear/base/bull
normalized cash and 18x/24x/30x sensitivities. That base case does not clear the
September 2026 equity value, so the market must prove more than another record
revenue year.

## Filing-based thesis breakers

The access thesis weakens if utilization and rental rates fall while fleet
spending continues; specialty growth is increasingly lower-margin ancillary or
re-rent revenue; used-equipment proceeds decline; acquisition cohorts fail to
improve utilization or cash; restructuring recurs; or buybacks and dividends
exceed post-fleet cash while leverage rises.

The broader infrastructure thesis weakens if distributors, contractors, and
rental providers all show the same pattern: backlog or project demand rises,
but inventory, fleet, labor, receivables, debt, and capital returns consume the
cash. That would indicate demand is being monetized through burden transfer,
not durable economic profit.
