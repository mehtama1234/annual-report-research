# Restaurant franchise, occasion, and operating-burden forensic comparison

Date baseline: 2026-09-14  
Scope: CAVA Group, Restaurant Brands International, Wingstop, Yum! Brands, Chipotle Mexican Grill, Starbucks Corporation, and Darden Restaurants  
Evidence boundary: 2025 annual-report packets and their source ledgers, with Q1/Q2 2026 updates where the local deep dossiers preserve them

## Why these companies belong together

These companies all monetize food occasions, but they sit at different points
on the relationship-versus-burden spectrum. CAVA is still building a
company-led restaurant system. Darden and Chipotle are mature or maturing
company-operated comparisons. RBI and YUM are global franchise platforms with
large system sales and relatively limited company-restaurant exposure.
Wingstop is a concentrated, highly franchised concept whose digital channel
and brand proposition must withstand a 2025 domestic same-store-sales decline.

The useful question is therefore not simply which chain has the strongest
consumer brand. It is:

> Which company owns the repeat demand, standards, data, and franchise
> economics—and which party carries labor, food, real-estate, maintenance,
> financing, and execution risk?

This is a packet-backed research comparison, not an investment conclusion.
The packets contain strong annual-report context and selected reported facts,
but not a complete comparable restaurant-unit, traffic/mix, or franchisee-cash
bridge.

## Franchisee health: the missing denominator behind “asset-light”

The franchisors do not report a complete, comparable cash return for the
typical franchisee. That absence is important. RBI, YUM, and Wingstop can
collect royalties and report system-wide sales before the operator has paid
labor, food, rent, remodels, debt service, taxes, and replacement equipment.
The parent therefore needs a second scorecard that uses observable network
signals as imperfect evidence of franchisee health.

| Franchise system | Parent-level cash screen | What the parent carries | What franchisees carry outside the parent income statement | Most informative health signal |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| RBI | $1.297B after 2025 PP&E and acquired restaurants; $1.108B of common and Partnership-unit distributions; $13.25B long-term debt | Brand, technology, selected company stores, acquired stores, franchise support, debt, and distributions | Store labor, food, occupancy, remodels, local financing, and most execution | Comparable sales and traffic by brand, closures/delinquencies, remodel completion, development pace, incentives, and acquired-store four-wall margin |
| YUM | $857M after 2025 capital spending and franchise-restaurant acquisitions; $1.341B of dividends and buybacks; $11.872B long-term debt | Brand systems, digital/Byte investment, acquired stores, refranchising, debt, and capital returns | Local labor, commodities, rent, remodels, financing, and market execution across 155 countries | Brand-level traffic and price/mix, franchisee openings and closures, remodel deferrals, acquisition-to-refranchising returns, and royalty collection |
| Wingstop | $87.1M after 2025 PP&E and restaurant acquisitions; $254.3M of dividends and buybacks; approximately $1.22B securitized notes | Brand, digital/POS system, support, selective restaurant acquisitions, technology, and securitized debt | Food, wage, rent, financing, local operations, and nearly all store-level capital | Domestic same-store sales, AUV, transactions, openings/closures, franchisee financing, royalty receipts, and debt-service coverage |

These residuals are not directly comparable owner cash. RBI and YUM have
advertising-fund and property flows, noncontrolling or partnership claims, and
different acquisition and refranchising activity. Wingstop has a smaller
parent cash base, a large debt-supported buyback history, and advertising-fund
timing. The table is useful because it shows the burden transfer in both
directions: a parent can appear capital-light while still buying troubled
stores, supporting franchisees, funding technology, or increasing debt when
the network is under stress.

## Store-burden comparison: the cash left after the system is funded

The direct-operator comparison needs a different denominator. A company-owned
chain records the guest sale and the food, labor, occupancy, maintenance, and
opening cost. Operating cash flow therefore looks more burdened than a pure
franchisor's, but it also gives the investor more direct evidence about what
the system costs to run. McDonald's is included as a hybrid franchise-heavy
control case: its parent residual is cleaner because franchisees fund much of
the store burden, but that burden must be tested outside the parent income
statement.

| Company | Latest filed period | Operating cash flow | PP&E / restaurant capex | Acquisition cash | OCF less listed capital and acquisitions | What the residual still does not prove |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| CAVA | FY2025 | `$184.8M` | `$158.7M` | — | `$26.1M` | Maintenance versus growth capex, leases, SBC, and the return on 73 openings |
| Chipotle | FY2025 | `$2.114B` | `$666.3M` | — | `$1.448B` | Lease commitments, SBC, new-store growth, and buybacks above the residual |
| Darden | FY2026 | `$1.853B` | `$734.0M` | `$0` comparable acquisition cash | `$1.119B` | Fiscal-period mismatch, maintenance versus growth capital, leases, and the next acquisition cycle |
| McDonald's | FY2025 | `$10.551B` | `$3.365B` | `$354M` | `$6.832B` | Franchisee health, support obligations, dividends, debt, and system-level reinvestment |

The figures are screening bridges, not a ranking. Darden's latest period is
FY2026, while the others are FY2025. Chipotle's residual is large because the
company has strong cash generation and no conventional debt burden, but its
2025 buybacks exceeded cash after restaurant capex. CAVA's small residual is
consistent with a rapidly expanding company-operated base, not necessarily a
weak concept. McDonald's residual is structurally higher because franchisees
fund much of the restaurant-level labor and working-capital burden; that
advantage must be tested through franchisee margins, remodels, closures, and
support spending.

Starbucks is kept outside this table because its latest evidence is a repair
period rather than a clean comparable cash bridge: FY2025 revenue grew 3%, but
operating income fell to `$2.9B` from `$5.4B` as the company funded labor,
service, and store-reset work. Its current test is whether transaction growth
and service investment restore store-level margin and cash after the repair
costs, not whether it matches the direct-operator residuals above.

### What would count as evidence rather than a slogan

The franchise platform is earning its keep when four observations move
together: same-store traffic or healthy price/mix, stable or improving
franchisee development, completed remodels, and parent royalty cash after
support and capital spending. System sales or digital transactions alone are
not enough. New units can increase system sales while the mature store base
loses traffic; price can increase sales while operator margins fall; and
parent buybacks can increase EPS while debt and franchisee support obligations
rise.

For RBI and YUM, the next annual and quarterly filings should be read by brand
and geography. For Wingstop, the current quarterly evidence already shows the
required contradiction: second-quarter 2026 system sales rose 5.3% while
domestic same-store sales fell 7.5% and domestic AUV fell to $1.893M from
$2.112M. The question is whether new unit growth is creating future royalty
capacity or masking deterioration in mature operator returns.

The same test applies to the parent's capital allocation. Distributions are
economically sound only after the system has funded technology, brand support,
company-store or acquired-store obligations, debt service, and any assistance
needed to keep franchisees solvent. A franchisor that must repeatedly acquire
or subsidize stores is carrying more of the operating burden than its
consolidated revenue suggests.

## CAVA: company-led wellness positioning still carries the store burden

### What the report says

CAVA's bounded packet reports 367 locations after 58 net new openings and describes
Mediterranean food as a growing cultural and wellness category. It presents
eat-in, take-out, catering, loyalty, and relationship-building as distinct
guest occasions. It also says it raised prices approximately 3% in 2024,
below inflation, while emphasizing value, operational excellence, hospitality,
team development, and disciplined growth (2025 annual report, pp. 3-6). The
current FY2025 SEC 10-K reports 439 ending restaurants after 73 openings and
one closure, 4.0% same-restaurant sales growth, $2.934M AUV, and 24.4%
restaurant-level margin. Operating cash flow was $184.8M against $158.7M of
PP&E purchases, leaving about $26.1M before other owner claims. The
packet/10-K date mismatch is a data-lineage warning, not a reason to blend the
two periods.

### Alden lens: the demand may be durable, but the cost stack is local

The durable part of the thesis is not the word “wellness.” It is the possibility
that a recognizable food identity can become a repeated, affordable occasion
across dine-in, takeout, catering, and loyalty. The burden side is more
immediate: company-led openings, restaurant labor, training, food waste,
occupancy, and reinvestment remain close to the operating model. CAVA's annual
report narrative does not quantify those burdens sufficiently to conclude that
the cultural positioning has produced durable economic surplus.

### Damodaran lens: translate openings into mature-store economics

The key narrative-to-numbers bridge is:

`58 net openings -> mature-store sales and contribution -> reinvestment -> free cash flow -> incremental return`

The packet does not provide the full bridge. A serious valuation model should
separate new-store ramp from same-store demand, price from traffic, and
company-level revenue growth from store-level cash returns. A growth rate that
looks attractive while requiring ever-higher opening and training spend would
be a different business from a repeatable, high-return unit model.

### Earnings-quality and shenanigan tests

- Reconcile reported revenue growth to comparable-store sales, new-store
  contribution, catering, and price/mix; do not treat total openings as demand
  proof.
- Reconcile operating cash flow to net income after inventory, payables,
  pre-opening costs, lease payments, and capex.
- Track whether “disciplined growth” is supported by store-level contribution
  after maintenance capex rather than only adjusted restaurant metrics.
- Test whether below-inflation pricing is a durable value choice or a temporary
  traffic subsidy that later appears as price, mix, or margin pressure.
- Separate wellness positioning from measured traffic or customer-frequency
  evidence; the packet establishes narrative, not causality.

### Next-filing falsifier

The clean growth thesis weakens if comparable sales remain dependent on price,
new-store openings fail to mature toward targeted returns, restaurant-level
margin falls despite scale, or operating cash flow repeatedly trails reported
profit after capex and working capital.

## RBI: the franchisor owns the system while franchisees absorb the local vise

### What the report says

RBI reports nearly $47B of system-wide sales, more than 33,000 restaurants in
more than 120 countries and territories, and more than 95% of system-wide
restaurants franchised at year-end. Its four brands produced 2025 global
system-wide sales of $46.762B. Consolidated revenue was $9.434B and operating
income was $2.202B; company restaurant sales were $2.348B and company
restaurant expenses were $1.968B (2025 annual report, pp. 4-5, 67). The cash
bridge is less frictionless than the system-sales headline: 2025 operating
cash flow was $1.714B, PP&E purchases were $265M, acquired franchised
restaurants cost $152M, and common/Partnership-unit distributions were
$1.108B. Long-term debt was $13.25B and net interest expense was $516M.

The report also describes mobile/web ordering, kiosks, third-party delivery,
digital loyalty, and AI-supported drive-through and ordering/payment choices.
It explicitly names discretionary spending, inflation, labor costs,
franchisee profitability, commodities, supply chain, geopolitical events, and
regulatory fragmentation as risks (pp. 9, 12-15).

### Alden lens: franchise architecture transfers operating volatility

RBI's scale and franchising can make the corporate earnings stream more
durable than the economics of any individual restaurant. But that durability
is conditional: the franchisee must remain economically healthy enough to
open, remodel, staff, and pay royalties. System-wide sales are therefore a
leading indicator of network activity, not a substitute for franchisee cash
generation.

### Damodaran lens: avoid the system-sales denominator trap

RBI's reported system sales are much larger than consolidated revenue because
the company does not record franchisee sales as its own restaurant revenue.
The correct bridge is:

`system sales -> franchisee unit economics -> royalty/rent/advertising flows -> RBI cash flow -> capital allocation`

Company restaurant sales and expenses give a bounded owned-store surface, but
they do not disclose a clean system-wide franchisee margin. Growth in system
sales can therefore coexist with weaker franchisee returns if labor, food,
rent, or remodel costs rise faster than menu pricing.

### Earnings-quality and shenanigan tests

- Reconcile system sales, comparable sales, restaurant count, royalty growth,
  advertising funds, and company-restaurant revenue by brand.
- Examine refranchising, acquisitions, franchisee incentives, and development
  commitments for growth that improves the franchisor's optics while moving
  risk to undercapitalized operators.
- Bridge operating income and adjusted measures to cash after interest, debt
  amortization, acquisition costs, and required brand investment.
- Test whether digital and AI claims show measurable labor-hour, throughput,
  conversion, or store-level margin improvement; adoption counts alone are
  insufficient.
- Monitor franchisee closures, delinquency, remodel obligations, and
  development pace as hidden indicators of network health.

### Next-filing falsifier

The asset-light franchise thesis weakens if royalty growth requires materially
higher franchisee incentives, franchisee failures increase, unit development
slows, company-restaurant economics deteriorate, or corporate cash returns
depend on leverage and adjusted add-backs rather than recurring royalty cash.

## Wingstop: digital relationship and franchise density meet a demand test

### What the report says

Wingstop describes a predominantly franchised, asset-light system: about 98%
of restaurants were franchisee-owned at year-end, with 3,056 restaurants. 2025
system-wide sales were $5.343B, domestic AUV was approximately $2.0M, domestic
same-store sales declined 3.3%, and total revenue was $696.853M. Digital sales
exceeded 60%. Company-owned restaurant labor was 23.2% of company-owned
restaurant sales, while company-owned food, beverage, and packaging were
36.8% (2025 annual report, pp. 7-11, 35, 39).

### Alden lens: the network is durable only if franchisee economics remain
fundable

The brand, digital ordering, POS data, and Smart Kitchen may create a strong
operating interface. Yet the same report identifies minimum-wage and benefit
standards, hiring difficulty, supplier and distributor dependence, commodity
prices, and franchisee financing as risks. The company may own the demand
relationship while franchisees carry the volatility required to fulfill it.

### Damodaran lens: AUV is not owner return

The critical distinction is:

`$2.0M domestic AUV -> store-level four-wall profit -> franchisee capex/debt/labor -> openings and royalties`

The packet provides AUV and selected company-owned cost percentages, but not a
complete franchisee cash return. High AUV can support an attractive narrative
while rising wings, labor, rent, delivery, and financing costs compress the
operator's actual return.

### Earnings-quality and shenanigan tests

- Reconcile domestic same-store sales to price, traffic, mix, digital order
  frequency, and promotions; the -3.3% result should not be hidden by system
  openings or AUV.
- Separate company-owned labor data from franchisee labor economics; the former
  cannot prove system-wide labor productivity.
- Test whether Smart Kitchen and digital sales reduce labor intensity or merely
  shift ordering and fulfillment costs across the system.
- Reconcile franchise revenue, advertising funds, development incentives, and
  cash flow after technology and support spending.
- Watch distributor concentration and commodity exposure for margin leakage
  that the franchisor may not bear directly.

### Next-filing falsifier

The clean interface-owner thesis weakens if same-store traffic remains weak,
franchisee financing stress slows openings, digital penetration rises without
better store-level returns, or reported cash conversion depends on payables,
franchisee fees, or nonrecurring benefits.

## YUM: global scale and Byte visibility require unit-level proof

### What the report says

YUM describes approximately 1,500 franchisees, more than 63,000 restaurants,
and operations across 155 countries and territories. 2025 total revenue was
$8.214B and operating profit was $2.574B; franchise and property revenue was
$3.473B. Taco Bell reported 7% same-store sales growth. Byte by Yum! processed
more than 370M digital transactions, up more than 60% year over year; KFC
digital sales rose more than 20%, and U.S. Taco Bell loyalty membership grew
more than 20% (2025 annual report, pp. 2-5, 102-120, 200).

The cash statement sharpens the comparison: YUM generated $2.010B of 2025
operating cash flow, spent $371M on capital spending and $782M acquiring
franchise restaurants, and then paid $552M of buybacks plus $789M of
dividends. Long-term debt was $11.872B. This is a larger and more leveraged
capital-return machine than the digital/system-sales narrative alone implies.

### Alden lens: geographic diversification is not the same as low burden

YUM's global footprint spreads brand and demand exposure, but it also adds
currency, regulatory, supply, privacy, geopolitical, commodity, and local
labor complexity. Byte and loyalty can make the customer relationship more
measurable, but the franchise network still has to fund restaurants and
deliver the experience.

### Damodaran lens: digital transactions are an activity metric, not a value
metric

The required bridge is:

`370M+ Byte transactions -> repeat frequency/conversion -> franchisee sales and margin -> royalties -> YUM cash and reinvestment returns`

The annual report establishes activity growth and brand-level same-store
performance, but not the full causal chain. The central valuation question is
whether the digital layer increases durable system economics or mainly lowers
friction while adding technology and data-compliance cost.

### Earnings-quality and shenanigan tests

- Reconcile company sales, franchise/property revenue, system sales, same-store
  sales, restaurant count, and franchise/property cash receipts.
- Break out Taco Bell's 7% same-store growth into price, traffic, mix, and
  promotions; do not let the strongest brand stand in for the portfolio.
- Test Byte transaction growth against average order value, frequency,
  retention, delivery commissions, and franchisee-level margin.
- Review refranchising, franchisee financing, development incentives, and
  impairment charges for evidence that capital burden is being transferred
  without improving system health.
- Bridge operating profit to free cash flow after interest, taxes, technology,
  remodels, acquisitions, and buybacks.

### Next-filing falsifier

The global franchise-platform thesis weakens if digital activity grows without
royalty or franchisee-return improvement, weaker brands offset Taco Bell,
development slows, or cash generation depends on leverage and accounting
benefits rather than recurring operating receipts.

## Starbucks: routine frequency and loyalty do not remove store burden

Starbucks adds a mature daily-consumption model rather than another franchise
platform. FY2025 revenue increased `3%` to `$37.2B`, but operating income fell
to `$2.9B` from `$5.4B`, operating margin fell to `7.9%` from `15.0%`, and
diluted EPS fell to `$1.63` from `$3.31`. Management responded with Back to
Starbucks, including coffeehouse labor investment, service changes, support
restructuring, and closures.

The current evidence shows a recovery in customer activity: Q2 FY2026 global
comparable sales rose `6.2%`, transactions rose `3.8%`, and average ticket rose
`2.3%`; Q3 global comparable sales rose `7.9%`, led by transactions. But the
recovery remains a repair case until store margin, labor productivity, service
quality, and cash improve together.

Starbucks also exposes a stored-value and loyalty liability that franchise
comparisons do not show in the same form. Card balances and unredeemed Stars
can bring cash before the associated product is delivered, but they remain
future redemption or breakage claims. The correct bridge is:

`transactions × ticket -> store sales -> labor, coffee, food, occupancy, and
service -> loyalty, support, technology, closures, and capex -> deferred-value
claims, debt, leases, and dilution -> owner cash`

This makes Starbucks a useful control case for frequency and loyalty. A loyalty
account can improve identification and repeat behavior, but it cannot prove
that each visit earns adequate cash after wages, coffee, food, tariffs, rent,
rewards, and maintenance. The [Starbucks deep memo](../deep-company-pages/starbucks-corporation.md)
sets the filing-based tests.

### Starbucks next-filing falsifier

The thesis weakens if comparable sales rise mainly through average ticket while
transactions stall, service investment fails to improve retention or store
margin, stored-value liabilities grow faster than profitable redemption, or
cash recovery depends on deferred maintenance, recurring restructuring, debt,
or buybacks rather than improved store economics.

## Cross-company synthesis

### The clean layer

RBI, Wingstop, and YUM have a structural opportunity to earn from brand,
franchise royalties, standards, digital interfaces, and system coordination
without owning every restaurant. That is the restaurant version of the broader
archive pattern: the relationship and control layer can be cleaner than the
physical fulfillment layer.

### The burden carrier

The burden has not disappeared. It is concentrated in franchisees for RBI,
Wingstop, and YUM, and more directly in CAVA, Chipotle, Starbucks, and Darden:
store labor, food and packaging, rent, maintenance, local compliance, staffing,
delivery execution, debt, and remodel requirements. A franchisor's asset-light
economics are only durable when the burden carrier has enough return to keep
investing. A direct operator's clearer data comes with a larger capital bill.

### The strongest contradiction

CAVA is the growth-stage contradiction to a simplistic “franchising always
wins” claim, while Darden is the mature direct-operator comparison. A
company-operated concept can preserve more control over hospitality, menu,
data, and execution, and may capture more of a successful unit's economics. It
also keeps the labor, opening, and capital burden. The right comparison is not
franchised versus company-owned in the abstract; it is whether the incremental
control produces better mature-store cash returns than the capital and
operating burden it retains.

### The common forensic trap

Across these companies, the most seductive metrics—system-wide sales, AUV,
restaurant count, digital transactions, loyalty members, and adjusted profit—
are not equivalent to shareholder cash economics. Each requires a bridge to
traffic, franchisee health, labor, capital, cash conversion, and incremental
returns.

## Bottom line

The restaurant lane supports the archive's broader control-point hypothesis but
adds an important qualification: franchise architecture can improve the
franchisor's reported economics by transferring physical burden, yet that
transfer is not free. The next analytical step is to prove whether franchisee
cash returns, not just franchisor revenue and digital activity, remain healthy
enough to sustain the network.

Chipotle supplies the clean company-operated control case. It keeps the store
economics rather than collecting mainly royalties, so labor, food, occupancy,
digital complexity, and new-unit capex appear directly in the parent company.
That makes its FY2025 comparable-sales decline and Q2 2026 restaurant-margin
pressure more informative than a franchisor's consolidated royalty growth. The
[Chipotle deep memo](../deep-company-pages/chipotle-mexican-grill.md) shows the
required comparison: company-owned economics are easier to observe, but the
parent cannot transfer the physical burden to franchisees.

Darden supplies the mature direct-operator comparison. CAVA shows the early
growth-stage cash burden; Darden shows that a mature, scaled system can produce
substantial operating cash while still requiring a large recurring property and
equipment program and acquisition decisions. Direct ownership makes the burden
more visible rather than eliminating it.

The important distinction is not simply “asset-light versus asset-heavy.” It is
whether the parent is collecting cash because it owns the customer relationship,
because it owns the store economics, or because it has shifted the hardest
operating costs to an independent operator. Each can be attractive, but each
requires a different evidence denominator.

## Valuation expectations across the restaurant models

The scenario register makes the valuation difference visible, but these values
are not directly interchangeable. Direct operators subtract substantial store
capital from operating cash. Franchise platforms show cleaner parent cash, but
their residuals still require franchisee health, debt, advertising-fund, and
support tests.

The table is a comparison of expectations, not a ranking. CAVA's current
screen requires the largest growth from its present owner-cash base. Chipotle
and Darden are judged on direct store economics. RBI, YUM, and Wingstop appear
more cash efficient at the parent level, but their valuation depends on a
separate question: can franchisees continue funding labor, rent, debt, and
remodels well enough for the royalty system to persist? The next filings must
test that burden rather than allowing parent-level cash conversion to end the
analysis.

McDonald's and Chipotle are the cleanest paired test. McDonald's parent cash is
cleaner because franchisees fund much of the store labor and operating system;
the hidden question is whether franchisees still earn adequate returns after
rent, royalties, discounts, wages, food, remodels, and debt. Chipotle's cash is
closer to the underlying restaurant economics because the company runs most
stores; its visible margin pressure is therefore more informative, but the
parent funds more of the physical burden.

For the next filing window, capture comparable transactions and average check,
restaurant-level margin after labor, food, occupancy, technology, and delivery,
openings, closures, remodels, mature-store sales, franchised versus company-
operated margin dollars, franchisee closures and development, parent cash flow,
capex, leases, dividends, buybacks, SBC, and whether loyalty or digital creates
profitable incremental visits rather than merely shifting ordering channel and
discount burden.
