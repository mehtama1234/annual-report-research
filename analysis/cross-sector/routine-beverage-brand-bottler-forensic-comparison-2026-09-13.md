# Routine beverages: brand, bottler, and owner-cash comparison

The key question in beverages is not whether people recognize the brand. It is whether repeat consumption becomes durable owner cash after the company pays for marketing, packaging, distribution, bottler support, product launches, acquisitions, working capital, and capital expenditure.

| Company | Filed operating evidence | Main quality question |
|---|---|---|
| [Coca-Cola](../deep-company-pages/the-coca-cola-company.md) | FY2025 revenue was $47.9B; organic revenue growth was 5%; unit case volume was even; reported free cash flow was $5.296B, or $11.365B excluding a $6.069B fairlife contingent-consideration payment. | Is growth coming from repeat demand and mix, or is price carrying revenue while bottlers and consumers absorb the strain? |
| [PepsiCo](../deep-company-pages/pepsico-inc.md) | FY2025 revenue was $93.9B; core operating profit was $14.9B; free cash flow was $8.2B; the business was 58% food and 42% beverage, with Walmart at 14% of revenue. | Does diversification improve resilience, or does food input cost, retailer power, labor, and debt dilute the apparent stability? |
| [Monster Beverage](../deep-company-pages/monster-beverage-corp.md) | FY2025 sales were $8.29B, operating income $2.42B, and net income $1.91B. | Can energy-drink identity and Coca-Cola distribution sustain growth after ingredient, can, marketing, regulatory, and category-expansion costs? |
| [Brown-Forman](../deep-company-pages/brown-forman-corp.md) | Fiscal 2025 net sales were about $4.0B, operating income about $1.1B, and free cash flow about $431M; reported sales fell 5% while organic sales grew 1%. | Can premium spirits brands preserve volume and cash when the category is discretionary, developed-market consumption softens, and inventory must be funded before sale? |

## Business economics: first-principles reading

A beverage sale begins with a consumer need or occasion, moves through a brand and package, then through a retailer, food-service channel, or bottler, and ends as revenue. The investor owns only the cash left after every participant and required reinvestment has been paid.

Coca-Cola is the cleanest test of the brand-and-bottler model. The parent owns trademarks, formulas, concentrate systems, and demand creation. Bottlers carry much of the local manufacturing, route delivery, labor, inventory, and equipment burden. That division can improve the parent's margins, but it also creates a proof requirement: parent revenue and volume can diverge from bottler health. Flat unit case volume with positive organic growth therefore requires a direct check on price/mix, channel affordability, concentrate sales, bottler economics, and consumer frequency.

PepsiCo adds a different burden. Food broadens consumption occasions, but it also exposes the company to agricultural inputs, packaging, labor, retailer negotiations, and a larger operating system. Its free cash flow must be read alongside dividends, capital spending, acquisitions, restructuring, and customer concentration. Monster has fewer categories and a sharper brand position, but its earnings are more exposed to whether energy consumption remains strong and whether adjacent products earn acceptable returns.

Brown-Forman adds the discretionary alcohol case. Its brand control can support premium pricing, but aging inventory and route-to-market changes tie up cash before demand is realized. A later cash-flow improvement must be split between operating earnings, working capital, and asset sales. The company therefore cannot be grouped with routine beverages solely because both products are consumed repeatedly.

## Forensic tests

1. Separate volume from price and mix. Revenue growth without stable or rising volume is not proof of stronger demand.
2. Track the bottler or distributor. A parent can report good margins while channel inventory, working capital, or partner returns deteriorate.
3. Reconcile operating cash flow to free cash flow. Remove acquisition payments, contingent consideration, unusual working-capital releases, and one-time tax or legal effects before assigning a normal cash number.
4. Treat impairments as evidence. Coca-Cola's BODYARMOR impairment and fairlife payment are not automatically fraud, but they show that brand and acquisition claims can require later cash or accounting correction.
5. Test the customer burden. Price increases may protect reported margin while lowering affordability, package size, frequency, or retailer acceptance.
6. Compare marketing and distribution spend with repeat demand. A campaign can create attention without creating durable cash.

## Lyn Alden and Damodaran lenses

Under a Lyn Alden lens, beverages have defensive demand, global reach, and relatively low ticket sizes, but the real question is whether purchasing power, currency, commodity inputs, and channel structure allow the parent to preserve cash through the cycle. Under a Damodaran lens, the narrative must become explicit assumptions about volume, price/mix, margins, reinvestment, working capital, and terminal growth. A high multiple is justified only if those assumptions remain credible after the forensic tests.

The next filings should show whether volume follows price, whether bottler health remains sound, whether reported free cash flow normalizes after fairlife, whether impairments recur, and whether shareholder returns are funded by recurring cash rather than balance-sheet movement.

## Definitions and the common cash chain

**Unit volume** measures physical cases, servings, or equivalent units sold.
**Price/mix** measures the effect of price changes and changes in package,
channel, geography, or product mix. **Organic revenue** removes specified
acquisition, divestiture, and currency effects. These measures answer different
questions and should not be substituted for one another.

The common chain is:

`consumer occasion -> brand and package choice -> retailer / bottler / distributor -> sale and collection -> marketing, ingredients, packaging, labor, and capital -> owner cash`

The parent brand owner may report high margins because bottlers or distributors
fund local plants, trucks, inventory, and route labor. A company with more
vertically integrated food and beverage operations may capture more gross
profit but also carries more raw-material, labor, plant, and working-capital
risk.

The cash screens are not directly comparable. Coca-Cola's reported FCF includes
a large contingent payment; PepsiCo's company-defined FCF includes property

screen excludes treasury investments but includes promotional and working-
capital effects; Brown-Forman's screen must account for whiskey aging and
working-capital timing.

## Coca-Cola: brand control with bottler dependence

Coca-Cola controls trademarks, formulas, marketing, package architecture, and
consumer demand creation. Bottlers and distributors perform much of the local
manufacturing, route delivery, labor, and equipment work. This can produce a
clean parent fee and concentrate model, but the burden has not disappeared.

FY2025 unit case volume was flat while organic revenue grew 5%. That gap may
reflect acceptable price/mix, but it can also reduce affordability and future
frequency. The $960M BODYARMOR trademark impairment and $6.069B fairlife
contingent-consideration payment show two different risks: acquired brand
expectations can be revised down, and an acquisition can create a large later
cash claim.

The key test is bottler health. If bottlers cannot fund packaging, routes,
coolers, labor, and local marketing, Coca-Cola's parent margin can remain high
temporarily while system demand and availability deteriorate.

## PepsiCo: scale with a larger direct cost stack

PepsiCo combines convenient foods and beverages. The portfolio creates frequent
consumption and shelf access, but it also carries ingredients, packaging,
factories, distribution, labor, retailer negotiations, and capital spending.
Walmart and affiliates represented approximately 14% of revenue, so retailer
terms, promotional allowances, inventory, and payment timing matter to cash.

The 2025 company-defined FCF of $8.2B should not be treated as a stable
denominator without charging $4.415B of capex, $3.15B of poppi and Siete
acquisition cash, $7.6B of dividends, and the debt used alongside capital
returns. Lower capital spending and $528M of property-sale proceeds helped the
reported cash result. The quality question is whether investment was deferred
or genuinely became more efficient.

## Monster: case growth but rising route-to-market cost

Monster's 13.3% energy-drink case growth is stronger demand evidence than
revenue alone. Average net sales per case fell 1.6% because of currency and
geographic mix, while promotional allowances, commissions, and other expenses
rose 22.6% to $1.57B.

The company therefore grew physical volume while paying more to place and sell
it. Accounts receivable increased $300.6M, inventories increased $34.9M, and
accrued promotional allowances increased $98.3M. These are ordinary operating
movements, not evidence of false revenue. They show that brand growth does not
automatically produce proportionate cash.

Monster also depends on Coca-Cola-related bottlers and distributors. The brand
relationship is an advantage, but commission, route, shelf, and inventory
economics must be monitored separately from the parent gross margin.

## Brown-Forman: brand pricing with production lead time

Brown-Forman's premium spirits brands can support pricing and identity, but
spirits demand is more discretionary than routine soft drinks or food. Whiskey
must be produced and aged before sale, so capital is committed well before the
consumer purchase.

Fiscal 2025 reported sales fell 5% while organic sales rose 1%, and management
implemented workforce and route-to-market changes. Those actions can improve
the system, but they create transition costs and execution risk. The owner-cash
test must separate operating improvement from lower working-capital needs,
asset sales, and any temporary reduction in production or marketing.

## Alden, Damodaran, and forensic synthesis

### Alden: affordability, input costs, and channel liquidity

Routine beverages have relatively resilient consumption, but resilience is not
the same as unlimited pricing power. Inflation, household income, currency,
commodity inputs, packaging, retailer concentration, and bottler liquidity can
change the cash outcome. Premium spirits add greater discretionary and travel or
hospitality sensitivity.

The stress test should combine price increases with lower unit volume, retailer
concessions, bottler distress, packaging inflation, higher marketing needs, and
working-capital funding. A brand owner may appear resilient while the bottler,
retailer, distributor, or consumer absorbs the adjustment.

### Damodaran: narrative to numbers

The valuation model should separately forecast volume, price/mix, gross margin,
marketing, bottler/distributor economics, retailer terms, working capital,
capex, acquisitions, impairment, taxes, debt, and terminal growth.

For Coca-Cola, the central question is whether flat volume can become durable
cash without permanent affordability damage. For PepsiCo, the question is
whether broader categories justify the larger capital and retailer burden. For
Monster, it is whether case growth produces cash after promotion and route
cost. For Brown-Forman, it is whether brand relevance and price/mix cover aging
inventory and discretionary-category risk.

### Forensic tests

| Test | Coca-Cola | PepsiCo | Monster | Brown-Forman |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Demand | Unit cases versus price/mix | Food and beverage volume versus affordability | Cases versus revenue per case | Volume versus price/mix and category consumption |
| Channel | Bottler cash, support, and receivables | Walmart terms, promotion, and channel inventory | Coca-Cola bottler commissions and placement | Distributor terms and route transition |
| Cash | Fairlife payment, capex, working capital, acquisitions | FCF after capex, acquisitions, property sales, dividends, and debt | Receivables, inventory, promotions, SBC, and impairments | Aging inventory, cooperage, working capital, restructuring, and asset sales |
| Capital allocation | Contingent payments, impairments, buybacks | Debt-funded returns and portfolio acquisitions | Treasury investments, buybacks, and SBC | Dividends, buybacks, and production investment |

The evidence may identify a weak investment or a recurring burden without
establishing misconduct. The concern rises when a company repeatedly calls
marketing, restructuring, acquisition, legal, or channel-support costs
exceptional while those costs are necessary to preserve the business.

## Valuation expectations

The current scenario register places Coca-Cola at approximately 32x its $10.0B
base normalized-cash case, PepsiCo at approximately 20x its $8.5B base case,
Monster at approximately 22x its $2.0B base case, and Brown-Forman at
approximately 24x its $500M base case.

These multiples do not establish which brand is better. Coca-Cola's price
requires volume stabilization and recurring cash after fairlife, bottler, and
marketing claims. PepsiCo's price requires affordability actions to restore
volume without permanently lowering margins, while maintaining capex and
retailer support. Monster's price requires case growth and promotional
intensity to remain favorable. Brown-Forman's price requires a return to
recurring cash without relying on asset sales or underinvesting in aging stock.

## Conclusion

Coca-Cola has the cleanest brand-and-bottler control point, but flat volume and
large acquisition claims require discipline. PepsiCo has greater operating
scale and category breadth, but carries more direct cost and retailer burden.
Monster has the strongest current case-growth evidence, but promotion and
bottler economics determine how much reaches owners. Brown-Forman has valuable
brand identity, but its aging inventory and discretionary demand make cash more
cyclical.

The common conclusion is conditional: repeat consumption supports durability,
but owner value exists only after the system pays for volume, affordability,
marketing, packaging, channel partners, inventory, production, acquisitions,
and shareholder claims.
