# Standards, ratings, and clearing infrastructure: forensic comparison

S&P Global and CME both monetize financial-market coordination without being
ordinary lenders. Their control points and cycle exposures are different.

| Company | Control point | FY2025 OCF | Listed acquisition/capex burden | Core quality test |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| S&P Global | Ratings, indices, commodity benchmarks, and decision data | $5.651B | $2.023B acquisitions / $195M capex | Do recurring subscriptions, surveillance, and benchmark centrality offset issuance and asset-linked cycles? |
| CME Group | Clearing, execution, market data, and collateral workflow | $4.183B | $0.0B acquisitions / $0.0B ordinary PP&E screen in the comparable register | Does volume, collateral interest, and clearing trust remain durable after technology, regulation, and incentive claims? |

S&P's 2025 residual after acquisitions and capex was approximately $3.433B,
while CME's owner-cash denominator is not directly interchangeable because
collateral interest, clearing guarantees, and market-structure obligations
shape CME's cash. S&P has more visible acquisition and disposition accounting;
CME has more collateral and market-volume mechanics.

## Who captures the cleaner economics?

Both companies own coordination layers rather than physical production or
ordinary credit books. S&P captures payment for being a trusted reference
point; CME captures payment for being the venue, clearinghouse, and liquidity
hub. S&P's cleaner recurring components are subscriptions, surveillance, and
index licensing. CME's cleaner components are clearing, data, and execution
fees, but collateral balances and interest income require separate treatment.

The main burden differs:

- S&P carries data integrity, analyst talent, cyber, regulation, legal,
  acquisition integration, and credibility risk.
- CME carries clearing resilience, default-fund and collateral mechanics,
  technology, market oversight, liquidity shocks, and interest-rate exposure.

## Alden and Damodaran synthesis

The Alden lens favors both when institutional complexity and liquidity needs
rise, but higher rates are asymmetric: they may increase refinancing demand for
Ratings while reducing issuance and may increase CME collateral income while
changing trading volumes and balance-sheet liquidity.

The Damodaran lens asks whether the next dollar of data, index, clearing, or
technology investment earns a durable return. S&P's $2.023B acquisition program
and $1.549B of dispositions make portfolio return the central question. CME's
question is less M&A-driven and more about the normalized relationship between
volume, collateral interest, required resilience spending, and owner cash.

Both can be excellent businesses at the wrong price. The falsifier for S&P is
loss of reference-point authority or recurring acquisition-adjusted cash
weakness. The falsifier for CME is a deterioration in clearing trust, volume,
collateral economics, or capital requirements.

## Definitions and the two control points

S&P Global and CME both sit inside financial decision-making, but they control
different steps.

- A **reference point** is a rating, index, benchmark price, or data series
  that many participants agree to use when making a financing, investment, or
  hedging decision.
- **Clearing** is the process of standing between buyers and sellers after a
  trade, collecting collateral, calculating obligations, and managing a
  member default if necessary.
- **Collateral** is cash or securities posted to protect the clearing system
  from a participant's loss. It may appear on a clearinghouse balance sheet but
  is not ordinary cash available to shareholders.
- **Asset-linked fees** vary with the amount of money invested in products that
  use an index. They are recurring while assets remain invested, but they can
  fall with markets and investor flows.

S&P's chain is:

`issuer / investor / commodity participant -> rating, benchmark, price, or data -> financing, allocation, hedging, or monitoring decision -> subscription / licensing / ratings fee`

CME's chain is:

`risk-management need -> liquid contract and trading venue -> clearing and collateral -> market data and recurring participant workflow -> transaction, clearing, and data fee`

S&P is paid for trusted information and standards. CME is paid for execution,
liquidity, clearing, and the infrastructure that makes contracts usable.

## S&P Global: authority over the accepted reference

S&P's economic control point is the accepted reference. A rating can affect an
issuer's financing terms and eligibility. An index can direct portfolios and
license payments. A commodity price can enter a contract or risk model. Market
Intelligence products can become part of an analyst or corporate workflow.

FY2025 segment evidence shows several different businesses:

- Ratings revenue of $4.549B and segment operating profit of $3.013B;
- Market Intelligence revenue of $4.902B and segment operating profit of
  $991M;
- Energy revenue of $2.299B and segment operating profit of $943M;
- Mobility revenue of $1.747B and segment operating profit of $378M; and
- Indices revenue of $1.839B and segment operating profit of $1.271B.

The high Ratings margin is attractive but issuance-sensitive. A financing
drought can reduce transaction fees even if surveillance remains durable.
Indices fees can rise with market levels and assets, then fall without a loss
of index relevance. Data subscriptions are more recurring but require ongoing
technology, data quality, and specialist labor.

S&P spent $2.023B on acquisitions and received $1.549B of disposition proceeds
in 2025. The net cash movement should not be presented as if the company had
not made a large acquisition decision. With Intelligence, Visible Alpha, and
other portfolio actions must be tested on acquired revenue, retention, margin,
integration cost, and cash return.

S&P also repurchased $5.001B of stock, paid $1.170B of dividends, and paid
$321M to noncontrolling interests. The capital-return program exceeded the
$3.433B mechanical residual after capex and acquisitions. This can be rational
if the recurring reference-point cash stream is durable, but it increases the
need to verify debt, acquisition funding, and per-share cash rather than
assuming buybacks alone create value.

## CME: liquidity and clearing obligations

CME's economic control point is the combination of liquid contracts, trading
venue, central counterparty clearing, collateral management, and market data.
The network is valuable because participants want to trade and hedge where
other participants already trade. CME must continuously protect that network
through technology, margin, settlement, surveillance, cybersecurity, and
regulatory compliance.

FY2025 contract volume was 7.060B round turns, average daily volume was
28.129M contracts, and average rate per contract was $0.696. Volume contributed
approximately $261.2M of the year-over-year clearing-fee increase, while rate
per contract contributed $28.5M. This is a useful bridge because it shows
whether revenue growth comes from more activity, product mix, or pricing.

The balance sheet requires a hard boundary. CME reported approximately
$159.656B of performance bonds and guaranty-fund contributions as restricted
cash and equivalents, with the same amount in liabilities. This collateral
supports the clearing system and is not corporate liquidity. CME may earn
interest on it, but the related cash and obligation should not be valued like
unrestricted exchange-owner cash.

Investment income was $5.737B in 2025, while other non-operating expense was
$4.834B. A material part of the economics reflects interest and distributions
associated with collateral. That is a legitimate revenue stream when earned,
but it is more sensitive to short-term rates and collateral balances than core
transaction and data fees. A normalized valuation must show low-rate, normal-
rate, and high-rate cases.

CME's corporate liquidity is much smaller than its clearing collateral: cash
and cash equivalents were approximately $4.417B, marketable securities were
$125M, long-term debt was $3.422B, and the company had a $2.3B revolver with no
borrowings at year-end. This distinction is central to earnings quality and
balance-sheet resilience.

## Direct framework comparison

### Alden: rates, liquidity, and institutional dependence

S&P benefits when debt issuance, regulation, passive investing, commodity
complexity, and institutional decision-making require trusted external
references. Higher rates may increase the need for credit analysis but can
reduce issuance, asset values, M&A, and index-linked fees. A severe credit
contraction can expose Ratings transaction revenue.

CME benefits from hedging and volatility, but interest income on collateral is
rate-sensitive. Higher volatility can increase volume and collateral while a
rate decline can reduce investment income. A market-structure or clearing
failure can create a much larger trust and regulatory cost than ordinary capex
would suggest.

Both are more resilient than lenders because they do not primarily fund a loan
book. S&P's burden is credibility, data integrity, acquisition execution,
regulation, and talent. CME's burden is operational continuity, clearing
default management, collateral protection, cybersecurity, and market oversight.

### Damodaran: break the narrative into engines

S&P should be modeled as recurring subscriptions and benchmark licensing, normal
Ratings surveillance, normalized issuance, asset-linked index fees, Energy and
Mobility cash, technology/talent spending, acquisitions, restructuring, debt,
NCI, and dilution.

CME should be modeled as clearing and transaction fees by contract volume and
rate, market data, normalized collateral interest, technology and regulatory
spending, default and resilience requirements, SBC, and shareholder returns.

The lifecycle question for S&P is whether acquisitions still earn high returns
or whether portfolio changes and buybacks compensate for slower organic growth.
For CME, it is whether new products deepen liquidity and expand the network
without weakening pricing, risk controls, or capital efficiency.

## Valuation expectations

The current scenario register assigns S&P normalized owner cash of $3.0B/$4.0B/$5.0B
at 28x/32x/36x, against approximately $121.4B of equity value. The base case
is close to the market value. The price requires reference-point authority,
subscription renewal, normalized Ratings activity, successful acquisitions,
and capital returns that do not rely on asset-sale proceeds or excessive debt.

CME's register assigns $3.5B/$4.2B/$4.8B at 20x/23x/26x, against approximately
$99.5B of equity value. The base case is also close to the market value, but
the cash must be adjusted for collateral interest, rates, volume, clearing
obligations, technology, regulation, and SBC. A low capex number does not
justify a higher multiple if the market is capitalizing a temporarily high
interest environment.

The two prices therefore require different evidence. S&P needs durable
reference-point and acquisition economics. CME needs durable liquidity and
clearing economics after rate normalization and restricted-cash boundaries.

## Conclusion

S&P owns the accepted reference and decision standard. CME owns the venue,
liquidity, and clearing workflow. Both can earn high margins without owning
factories or loan books, but their risks are not interchangeable.

S&P's cleaner economics are threatened by acquisition return, issuance cycle,
and credibility risk. CME's cleaner economics are threatened by volume and
rate cycles, restricted-cash confusion, clearing failure, and regulation.
The correct investment conclusion is not that one is universally better. It is
that each must be valued on the cash engine it actually controls and the
obligations required to keep that control credible.
