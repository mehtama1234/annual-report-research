# Validation, assurance, and test control: three ways customers pay to avoid failure

## Purpose

Validation is not one business. KLA, Teradyne, and Keysight sit at different
points in the engineering and manufacturing sequence:

`manufacturing process -> device production test -> system/design/network assurance`

Each company helps customers avoid a different failure. The comparison matters
because “mission-critical validation” can sound recurring and software-like
while the actual cash flow may still depend on fab budgets, product launches,
electronics capex, acquisitions, or technical reinvestment.

## Control-point map

| Company | Validation point | Customer failure being avoided | Revenue and cash evidence | Main quality question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| KLA | Wafer inspection, metrology, process control, and yield protection | Defects, process drift, and lost wafer yield | FY2026 revenue `$13.579B`, OCF `$4.143B`, service revenue `$3.126B`, cash after capex about `$3.767B` | Does qualified process-control demand and service remain strong through a fab-capex downturn? |
| Teradyne | Semiconductor, memory, system, defense, and industrial production test | Shipping a device or system that fails performance or reliability requirements | FY2025 revenue `$3.190B`, OCF `$674M`, PP&E `$224M`, FCF about `$450M`; Q2 FY2026 Semiconductor Test `$1.122B` | Does AI and memory test intensity offset the cycle, and do Product Test and Robotics diversify cash? |
| Keysight | Design simulation, communications, network, signal, and deployment assurance | Releasing a system that fails speed, interoperability, security, or technical standards | FY2025 revenue `$5.375B`, OCF `$1.409B`, PP&E `$128M`, cash after PP&E about `$1.281B`; Q3 FY2026 revenue `$1.85B` | Does the measurement workflow become recurring software/service cash, or remain a high-value electronics-capex tool cycle? |

The companies are complementary, not interchangeable. KLA is closest to the
fabrication process. Teradyne tests the production output. Keysight reaches
back into design and forward into network and deployment assurance.

## Who owns the relationship?

### KLA: the fab-process relationship

KLA's relationship is with semiconductor manufacturers and process engineers.
The customer qualifies measurement accuracy, tool integration, process history,
service response, and yield impact. Replacement can create production risk and
requalification cost.

The relationship is technically deep but economically tied to fab investment.
KLA does not control when a customer builds capacity or which geography gets
the fab.

### Teradyne: the production-test relationship

Teradyne's relationship is with semiconductor, memory, system, and industrial
production teams. The customer needs throughput, coverage, reliability data,
and test economics. The equipment becomes part of the production line, but a
customer can defer purchases when product launches or capacity plans move.

Teradyne's relationship is less pure than KLA's because Robotics and Product
Test have different adoption, competition, and margin structures. The
Semiconductor Test segment currently carries most of the AI narrative.

### Keysight: the engineering and assurance relationship

Keysight's relationship is with engineers, design teams, network operators,
defense programs, and standards-driven organizations. The customer needs
trusted measurement and analysis across a product lifecycle, not merely a
single production checkpoint.

That can create a wider workflow and software opportunity than a single test
system. It also creates a broad exposure to customer design cycles, network
upgrades, defense timing, automotive programs, and the need to keep measurement
standards current.

## Cleaner economics versus heavier burden

| Dimension | KLA | Teradyne | Keysight |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Technical qualification | Very high in process control and yield | High in production-test programs; varies by product | High where standards, signal integrity, and workflow data matter |
| Recurring layer | Services, spares, and installed-base support | Service, upgrades, and repeat production programs | Software, support, calibration, services, and repeat design workflows |
| Customer-capex exposure | High: fab and WFE budgets | High: semiconductor, memory, and system production capacity | Medium to high: engineering, network, defense, automotive, and industrial programs |
| Physical-capital burden | Lower than fab owners, but needs service and R&D infrastructure | Equipment inventory, R&D, PP&E, and customer support | Relatively light PP&E, but substantial R&D, software, and acquisition burden |
| Concentration risk | Meaningful customer and geography concentration | Semiconductor concentration plus Robotics diversification risk | Less than 10% of revenue from any one customer in FY2025, but end-market and acquisition concentration remain |
| Main valuation risk | Peak WFE economics | Peak AI/memory test economics | Growth and integration premium applied to normalized measurement cash |

The cleanest position cannot be selected from gross margin alone. It depends on
whether customer dependence survives a budget reduction and whether required
reinvestment rises slower than the value protected.

## Earnings-quality comparison

### KLA: service and margin quality must survive a down-cycle

KLA's process-control position is narrow and technically important. The main
tests are product versus service revenue, service revenue per installed system,
receivables, inventory, deferred revenue, warranty, tariffs, R&D, and customer
concentration. A high gross margin can reflect durable pricing, favorable mix,
or temporary volume absorption. The next down-cycle must separate those.

### Teradyne: cash is positive but the segment mix is less clean

Teradyne's 2025 OCF was $674M and PP&E spending was $224M. Its cash flow
included a $306M receivable use and a $53M source from deferred revenue and
customer advances. It also spent $144M on acquisitions and $26M on a strategic
investment, while recording $64M of SBC.

Q2 FY2026 Semiconductor Test revenue was $1.122B of $1.329B total revenue.
That is strong evidence of current AI and memory test demand. It does not show
that Product Test and Robotics can diversify owner cash or that the $450M 2025
FCF base is a normal floor.

### Keysight: stronger workflow breadth, more acquisition accounting

Keysight's 2025 OCF was $1.409B and PP&E spending was $128M. The company also
had $884M of deferred revenue and $176M of SBC. Cash flow was affected by taxes,
derivatives, variable compensation, pension funding, deferred revenue, and
acquisition-related payments.

The Spirent acquisition cost $1.564B, added $698M of goodwill and $515M of
developed technology and customer-relationship intangibles, and contributed
only $9M of revenue after closing in fiscal 2025. Keysight also sold selected
Spirent lines for $399M to meet regulatory requirements. The strategic story
may be right, but the economic return cannot be judged from reported revenue
growth alone.

## Macro and liquidity lens

The validation layer is exposed to liquidity through different channels:

- KLA follows fab construction, wafer-fabrication equipment, and technology
  transitions.
- Teradyne follows semiconductor, memory, compute, product-launch, and system
  production spending.
- Keysight follows engineering budgets, communications upgrades, network
  buildouts, defense programs, automotive electronics, and industrial design.

The common stress case is a customer spending pause combined with higher rates,
inventory digestion, export controls, and delayed product transitions. The
companies can remain technically necessary while their customers postpone
orders. That is why recurring service or software must be tested against
installed-base utilization and customer workflow continuity.

has the clearest current AI acceleration but the weakest normalized cash
denominator relative to its valuation.

## Filing tests

1. Does service, support, or software revenue hold when customers reduce new
   tool and system purchases?
2. Do receivables, inventory, customer advances, and deferred revenue convert
   into cash and gross profit without repeated timing support?
3. Does validation intensity rise faster than semiconductor and electronics
   unit volumes fall?
4. Does customer qualification remain valuable when customers seek internal,
   virtualized, or lower-cost test tools?
5. Does R&D produce measurable productivity, standards leadership, and share?
6. Do acquisitions create organic growth and cash returns after amortization,
   integration costs, divestitures, and goodwill?
7. Do buybacks reduce diluted shares after SBC, or only offset issuance?
8. Can each company preserve margins and service capability through a full
   liquidity and capex cycle?

## Current conclusion

Validation is a real economic control layer, but it has three different forms:

- **KLA:** protects manufacturing yield.
- **Teradyne:** proves production output and deployment readiness.
- **Keysight:** proves design, signal, network, and system performance.

The cleaner economics appear where validation is qualified into a workflow and
failure is expensive. The burden increases when the company depends on a
short-lived customer capex cycle, acquisition accounting, broad product mix,
or high R&D and software reinvestment.

The correct investment conclusion is not “validation is recurring.” It is:

`measure the failure being avoided, identify who pays for the proof, subtract the full cost of maintaining technical trust, then test the market price against normalized owner cash.`
