# AdaptHealth Corp. — home equipment, recurring resupply, and owner cash

Date baseline: `2026-09-14`. Primary evidence: [AdaptHealth source ledger](../../extracted/healthcare/medical-appliances-equipment/adapthealth-corp/source-ledger.md), [AdaptHealth company packet](../../extracted/healthcare/medical-appliances-equipment/adapthealth-corp/company-packet.md), the FY2025 Form 10-K, and the Q1–Q2 FY2026 filings and results materials listed in that ledger.

## Investment question

AdaptHealth supplies medical equipment, recurring supplies, respiratory
support, sleep-health products, diabetes products, and post-acute equipment in
the home. It links hospitals, sleep labs, pulmonologists, skilled nursing
facilities, clinics, patients, and public and private payers.

The investment question is whether referral access, patient retention,
equipment placement, and resupply can produce durable owner cash after product
cost, delivery, field service, documentation, payer authorization, capitated
contract risk, inventory, receivables, goodwill, debt, technology, and required
capital.

The company is not simply a device retailer. Its economic value lies in keeping
patients supplied and compliant after the clinical encounter, while maintaining
the payer and referral workflow needed to receive payment.

## What the patient, referral source, and payer are buying

The patient is buying equipment, supplies, delivery, setup, education, service,
and continuity. The referral source is buying reliable discharge or chronic-
care follow-through. The payer is buying equipment and supplies that meet
clinical, coding, documentation, utilization, and cost requirements.

AdaptHealth's operating sequence is:

`clinical need or discharge -> referral and authorization -> equipment
placement -> delivery, setup, and education -> recurring supply use -> resupply
and field service -> documentation and claim -> collection -> patient retention
-> owner cash`

AdaptHealth controls referral relationships, branch and field execution,
equipment access, resupply reminders, payer routing, documentation, and the
patient relationship after discharge. It does not control manufacturer pricing,
payer coding, patient adherence, referral volume, or every reimbursement rule.

## Scale and service mix

The FY2025 filing reported approximately `4.3M` annual patients, approximately
`640` locations in `48` states, and operations across all `50` states. The
reportable segments were Sleep Health, Respiratory Health, Diabetes Health, and
Wellness at Home.

The economic mix matters. Sleep and respiratory products can combine an initial
equipment placement with recurring consumable resupply. Wellness at Home adds
post-acute equipment and supplies. Diabetes Health was later agreed to be sold
for `$235.0M` in cash, showing management narrowing the portfolio around the
categories where it believes the referral, payer, and resupply system is
strongest.

Track:

- new patients, active patients, and patient retention;
- equipment placements and utilization;
- recurring resupply orders and revenue per patient;
- reimbursement per item and payer mix;
- delivery, setup, repair, and field-service cost;
- authorization, documentation, denial, and collection rates;
- capitated-contract revenue, cost, and medical or equipment responsibility;
- inventory, equipment ownership, rental, and replacement capital;
- acquisitions, goodwill, impairments, debt, and interest; and
- operating cash and diluted cash per share after capex and stock compensation.

Patient count and delivery volume are activity measures. They become owner
economics only after collection, product cost, service cost, capital, and
contractual claims are included.

## Equipment placement versus recurring resupply

An equipment placement can create a patient relationship, but the durable cash
may come from recurring supplies, replacement cycles, service, and continued
eligibility. A placement can also consume inventory, delivery labor, training,
and capital before the company earns the recurring contribution.

The bridge is:

`new patients × equipment placement -> setup and initial reimbursement -> supply
use and resupply -> product, delivery, field service, documentation, and payer
cost -> repeat collection -> equipment replacement and maintenance capital ->
owner cash`

Track resupply frequency, patient retention, authorization, item utilization,
product margin, delivery density, and replacement cost. A growing patient count
with declining resupply or rising service cost is weaker evidence than a stable
patient base with improving retained contribution.

## Referral and payer control

AdaptHealth depends on hospitals, sleep labs, physicians, skilled nursing
facilities, clinics, and payers. Referral access can create a valuable channel,
but the channel is not fully controlled by AdaptHealth. Coding rules,
authorization, competitive bidding, payer networks, and clinical documentation
can change the economics.

The cash bridge must separate:

- referred patients from accepted and delivered patients;
- equipment delivered from paid claims;
- recurring supplies from one-time placements;
- billed revenue from collected cash;
- commercial, Medicare, Medicaid, and capitated revenue;
- denial, appeal, and authorization timing; and
- patient retention from payer eligibility and resupply approval.

The most important question is whether the company is paid for a service it can
deliver profitably, not whether a referral or order was received.

## Capitated contracts and portfolio change

Capitated contracts can create a large patient and revenue opportunity because
the provider receives a fixed or structured payment for a population or service
set. They can also create responsibility for utilization, equipment cost,
patient support, and outcomes that would otherwise be billed item by item.

Q2 2026 showed the full-scale West Coast capitated partnership, an unexpected
manufacturer price increase, margin pressure, and a guidance reset. That is a
useful forensic checkpoint: growth can be real while the contract's cost and
pricing assumptions are still being learned.

The contract bridge is:

`covered patients × capitated payment -> equipment, supplies, delivery,
clinical support, and administration -> utilization, replacement, quality, and
vendor cost -> contract settlement and owner cash`

Do not compare capitated patient count directly with fee-for-service revenue.
Track contract reserves, utilization, vendor price changes, service cost,
quality adjustments, and cash settlement.

The Diabetes Health sale for `$235.0M` in cash also requires a separate bridge:
sale proceeds, lost future revenue, retained overhead, stranded cost, taxes,
debt effects, and whether the continuing business has better cash conversion.

## Earnings-quality and financial-engineering tests

### 1. Patient count is not resupply cash

The company reported approximately `4.3M` annual patients. Test how many remain
active, how often they reorder, whether payer authorization continues, and what
contribution remains after delivery, service, and product cost.

### 2. Adjusted EBITDA can coexist with impairment and weak free cash flow

FY2025 adjusted EBITDA was `$616.7M`, while net loss included a `$128.0M`
goodwill impairment. Q2 2026 adjusted EBITDA was `$132.0M`, while the company
reported a `$144.2M` pre-tax goodwill write-down and negative year-to-date free
cash flow of `$48.4M`. Impairment is noncash in the current period, but it is
evidence that earlier acquisition prices or expected returns need reassessment.

### 3. Organic growth must be separated from contract and portfolio effects

Q2 organic revenue growth was `15.9%`, but the quarter also included a full-
scale capitated partnership, a manufacturer price increase, a Diabetes sale
agreement, and guidance reset. Separate continuing organic patient and resupply
growth from new contract volume, price, mix, and divestiture effects.

### 4. Free cash flow can lag growth during rollout

Q1 and Q2 2026 showed stronger revenue but negative quarterly or year-to-date
free cash flow. Reconcile receivables, inventory, equipment purchases,
capitated onboarding, supplier terms, and capital expenditure. Growth that
requires cash before reimbursement is less valuable than growth with stable
collection and resupply economics.

### 5. Manufacturer pricing can reverse the margin story

Recurring healthcare access does not guarantee input-price stability. A
manufacturer price increase can reduce margin inside a capitated or payer-
constrained contract. Track vendor concentration, price changes, rebates,
chargebacks, and the timing of reimbursement resets.

### 6. Goodwill is a record of acquisition expectations

Goodwill impairments of `$128.0M` in FY2025 and `$144.2M` pre-tax in Q2 2026
require a review of acquisition returns, patient retention, payer mix,
resupply, and continuing cash flows. The impairment is not itself a misconduct
finding; it is evidence that the acquired economics deserve a lower confidence
grade until rebuilt.

## Lyn Alden lens: home-based demand and capital liquidity

Demand for home medical equipment is supported by aging, chronic disease,
respiratory illness, sleep apnea, post-acute discharge, and pressure to avoid
more expensive settings. That supports recurring need.

The cash flow remains exposed to payer policy, coding, authorization, supplier
pricing, patient adherence, referral volume, labor, fuel, delivery, and
equipment capital. Capitated arrangements can make the cash flow more sensitive
to contract design and utilization than an itemized reimbursement model.

The durable control point is a compliant national or regional service network
that can place equipment, resupply patients, document use, and collect from
payers. The network is durable only if patient retention, referral access,
vendor economics, field execution, and balance-sheet liquidity remain healthy.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers and lifecycle

The narrative should become:

`referral and eligible patient base × placement and resupply frequency ×
reimbursement -> retained contribution -> product, delivery, field service,
documentation, payer, capitated, corporate, and quality costs -> working
capital, replacement capital, acquisitions, debt, and dilution -> owner cash`

The model should separate:

1. new equipment placements from recurring resupply;
2. patient count from active and paying patient count;
3. fee-for-service from capitated contracts;
4. organic growth from acquisitions and portfolio sales;
5. product price from item volume and reimbursement;
6. maintenance equipment and replacement capital from expansion; and
7. impairment and restructuring from recurring cost of the platform.

AdaptHealth is a scaled home-continuity platform undergoing portfolio and
contract transition. Reinvestment creates value if branch density, digital
self-service, resupply, equipment utilization, and payer access increase
collected contribution above the full cost of equipment, delivery, labor,
quality, and working capital.

Valuation should use normalized owner cash after working-capital needs,
equipment replacement, capitated contract cost, debt, acquisition
normalization, impairment risk, and stock compensation—not adjusted EBITDA or
patient count alone.

## Management incentives and thesis breakers

Management has reason to emphasize patients served, organic revenue growth,
adjusted EBITDA, free cash flow, myAPP users, capitated expansion, debt
reduction, and portfolio focus. Pair each with resupply, paid claims, product
cost, delivery and field service, contract utilization, inventory, cash,
capex, goodwill, debt, and diluted shares.

The thesis weakens if:

- patient and referral growth do not produce active resupply and collected cash;
- organic revenue growth depends on rate, price, capitated volume, or
  acquisitions while retained contribution falls;
- capitated contracts create equipment, service, or utilization cost above the
  payment structure;
- supplier price increases, rebates, or product shortages compress margin;
- receivables, denials, inventory, or delivery cost prevent free-cash-flow
  conversion;
- goodwill impairments recur because acquired patient or resupply economics do
  not meet expectations;
- the Diabetes sale leaves stranded costs or reduces profitable network density;
- digital self-service grows without lower support cost or better retention; or
- debt, leases, acquisitions, or buybacks reduce resilience while free cash
  flow remains weak.
