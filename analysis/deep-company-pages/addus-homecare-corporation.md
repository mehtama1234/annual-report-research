# Addus HomeCare Corporation — aging in place, caregiver labor, and owner cash

Date baseline: `2026-09-14`. Primary evidence: [Addus source ledger](../../extracted/healthcare/specialized-health-services/addus-homecare-corporation/source-ledger.md), [Addus company packet](../../extracted/healthcare/specialized-health-services/addus-homecare-corporation/company-packet.md), the FY2025 Form 10-K, and the Q1–Q2 FY2026 filings and results materials listed in that ledger.

## Investment question

Addus provides personal care, hospice, and home health services. Its central
economic role is helping older, disabled, or seriously ill people receive care
at home rather than in a more expensive institutional setting.

The investment question is whether recurring care hours and local branch
density produce durable owner cash after caregiver wages, recruitment,
supervision, scheduling, transportation, documentation, state reimbursement,
managed-care contracts, patient quality, acquisitions, debt, and the capital
needed to maintain the service network.

The company is not primarily a medical-device or drug business. Its scarce
input is reliable labor coordinated through a compliant local operating system.
That makes labor economics and payer policy central to the valuation.

## What the patient, family, and payer are buying

The patient and family are buying caregiver hours, help with daily living,
supervised support, hospice visits, clinical home-health services, scheduling,
and continuity. The payer is buying a care setting that may cost less than an
institutional alternative while meeting eligibility, documentation, and
quality requirements.

Addus's operating sequence is:

`eligible patient -> referral and authorization -> caregiver or clinician
assignment -> scheduled home visit -> documented service -> payer claim and
collection -> repeated care -> local density and referral relationship ->
owner cash`

Addus controls branch operations, scheduling, compliance, referral
relationships, caregiver deployment, and the patient-service relationship. It
does not control the supply of available caregivers, state budgets, managed-
care rates, patient adherence, or all family and clinical outcomes.

## Service mix and operating denominator

Q2 2026 revenue was `$377.4M`, with personal care representing `78.4%` of
service mix, hospice `17.0%`, and home health `4.6%`. Addus should therefore be
read first as a personal-care labor network with hospice and home-health
adjacencies, not as a balanced three-segment clinical platform.

Track:

- authorized and delivered caregiver hours;
- revenue per hour and reimbursement by state or payer;
- caregiver fill rate, wage, overtime, turnover, and recruiting cost;
- branch density, referral source, scheduling productivity, and travel time;
- hospice census, visits, length of stay, and clinical labor;
- home-health episodes, visits, authorization, and denial rates;
- gross margin and operating margin by service line;
- acquisitions, integration cost, debt, interest, and goodwill;
- operating cash after working capital and capital expenditure; and
- diluted cash per share after stock compensation and buybacks.

Revenue growth can occur without owner value if rate increases exceed hours
delivered, if caregiver wages absorb reimbursement, if unfilled authorized
hours reduce service quality, or if acquisitions add revenue without adequate
cash return.

that acquisition-led density creates returns above the cost of capital.

## Labor is the core economic constraint

Personal care revenue is largely the product of authorized hours and the rate
paid for each hour. A caregiver must be recruited, scheduled, trained,
supervised, documented, and retained. The company may also incur travel time,
payroll taxes, benefits, workers' compensation, compliance cost, and branch
overhead.

The bridge is:

`authorized hours × filled hours × reimbursement per hour -> service revenue ->
caregiver wages, payroll burden, recruiting, supervision, scheduling, travel,
branch cost, compliance, and quality -> corporate cost -> working capital,
acquisition, debt, and maintenance capital -> owner cash`

The difference between authorized and delivered hours matters. A payer may
approve care, but the company cannot recognize a durable economic benefit if it
cannot find and retain a worker. A rate increase is also not a margin increase
if wage pressure, overtime, recruiting, and supervision rise at the same time.

Local density can improve scheduling and travel efficiency, but density does not
remove labor scarcity. It can make the network more valuable to payers and
referral sources only if the service remains reliable.

## Hospice and home health are different businesses

Hospice adds a clinical service with a different patient pathway, staffing
model, length of stay, referral base, reimbursement, and quality requirement.
Home health adds skilled visits and authorization complexity. They can broaden
the care relationship and referral network, but their economics should not be
blended with personal-care hours.

The separate tests are:

| Service | Primary denominator | Main burden | Required proof |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Personal care | Filled caregiver hours and reimbursement per hour | Wages, recruitment, supervision, travel, state rates, authorization, and compliance | Delivered hours and cash margin after labor |
| Hospice | Census, visits, length of stay, and reimbursement | Nurses, aides, clinical oversight, quality, referral, and regulatory compliance | Stable census and contribution after clinical labor and quality cost |
| Home health | Episodes, visits, authorization, and reimbursement | Skilled labor, documentation, denial, scheduling, and clinical outcomes | Collected cash after visit cost and payer requirements |

An increase in hospice or home-health mix may change margins and working capital.
It should not be described as simple diversification without service-line cash
and quality evidence.

## Reimbursement and state-policy exposure

Addus operates in a policy-defined market. Medicaid long-term services, state
budgets, managed-care contracts, electronic visit verification, authorization,
rate updates, and federal rules determine how caregiver hours become revenue.

Q1 2026 management commentary identified Texas and Illinois rate increases as
support for personal care. The correct question is how much of the rate reaches
the company after caregiver wages and whether it continues when the state
budget or managed-care contract changes.

The cash bridge must track:

- billed hours versus paid hours;
- authorization and denial rates;
- accounts receivable and collection days;
- supplemental or retroactive rate payments;
- managed-care withholds or quality adjustments;
- state-by-state rate and wage changes; and
- patient and payer concentration.

Temporary rate catch-up or favorable collection timing can lift a quarter's
cash without changing normalized service economics.

## Earnings-quality and financial-engineering tests

### 1. Revenue growth can be rate growth rather than service growth

Separate caregiver hours, filled hours, reimbursement per hour, acquisitions,
and price or rate effects. A higher rate with flat or falling service hours is
not the same as expanding care demand.

### 2. Adjusted EBITDA can exclude the cost of maintaining labor capacity

Recruitment, retention, integration, restructuring, and technology may be
described as one-time. If the company must spend them repeatedly to keep
caregivers and branches functioning, they are part of the recurring owner-cash
requirement.

### 3. Operating cash is sensitive to payer timing

Q4 2025 operating cash flow was `$18.8M`, Q1 2026 `$52.4M`, and Q2 2026
`$40.0M`. The variation requires a working-capital bridge through receivables,
payables, rate settlements, authorization, and acquisition timing. One strong
quarter does not establish normalized cash.

### 4. Acquisitions can add density or only add revenue

HomeCourt in Indiana was intended to add local personal-care scale. Test
acquired hours, caregiver retention, branch overlap, referral growth, margin,
integration spending, and cash return. Goodwill and amortization should be
tracked against incremental cash, not used as an automatic sign of growth.

### 5. Electronic verification improves compliance but adds execution cost

Electronic visit verification can reduce billing error and fraud risk, but it
also requires worker adoption, technology, training, and exception handling.
The system is valuable only if it improves paid-hour collection without
reducing caregiver availability or increasing administrative cost excessively.

### 6. Quality failure can destroy the apparent labor advantage

Missed visits, poor documentation, abuse or neglect, preventable hospital
use, and compliance findings can lead to penalties, lost referrals, payer
restrictions, and higher supervision cost. Quality is a cash variable, not only
an ethical or regulatory variable.

## Lyn Alden lens: demographic demand versus labor liquidity

The demand for home-based support is supported by aging, disability, chronic
illness, and family preference for remaining at home. That creates more durable
need than discretionary consumer spending.

The supply side is less durable. The company needs caregivers willing and able
to work at the reimbursement-supported wage in the relevant local market. A
shortage can limit delivered hours even when authorized demand is high.

Liquidity conditions affect state budgets, managed-care contracting, family
support, wage competition, acquisition financing, and provider balance sheets.
In a weak labor market, hiring may improve but patient demand and payer budgets
can weaken. In a tight labor market, demand may remain strong while wage and
turnover pressure reduces owner cash.

The durable control point is a compliant local network that can reliably fill
care hours and satisfy payers. Demographic demand alone does not establish that
control.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers and lifecycle

The narrative should become:

`eligible patients × authorized hours × fill rate × reimbursement -> collected
revenue -> caregiver and clinical cost, scheduling, travel, branch, quality,
and corporate cost -> working capital, maintenance, acquisitions, debt, and
dilution -> owner cash`

The model should separate:

1. organic hour growth from reimbursement rate changes;
2. filled hours from authorized hours;
3. personal care from hospice and home health;
4. wage and productivity changes from acquisition effects;
5. state-rate support from permanent contract economics;
6. branch density from corporate overhead and integration cost; and
7. maintenance technology and compliance spending from growth investment.

Addus is a consolidating local-service platform, but the lifecycle is not
software-like. Growth requires caregivers, branches, compliance, payer
contracts, and acquisition integration. Reinvestment creates value when local
density improves filled-hour productivity, referral access, quality, and cash
margin after the full labor burden.

Valuation should use normalized owner cash after caregiver labor,
working-capital variation, maintenance technology, debt, acquisition cadence,
and dilution. It should not capitalize adjusted EBITDA without those claims.

## Management incentives and thesis breakers

Management has reason to emphasize revenue, adjusted EBITDA, service-line mix,
rate support, acquisition growth, leverage reduction, and demand for lower-cost
home care. Pair each with filled hours, wage cost, turnover, collections,
quality, branch productivity, capex, debt, acquisitions, and diluted shares.

The thesis weakens if:

- revenue grows mainly through rate increases or acquisitions while filled
  hours and organic demand weaken;
- caregiver wages, turnover, overtime, or recruiting costs absorb rate gains;
- authorized hours rise but delivered and paid hours do not;
- receivables, denials, state settlements, or managed-care withholds weaken
  cash conversion;
- hospice or home-health growth increases clinical and compliance cost without
  adequate collected contribution;
- acquisitions add branches but not referral density, labor productivity,
  margin, or cash per share;
- quality failures, missed visits, or compliance events damage payer access; or
- leverage, buybacks, or acquisition spending reduce resilience while labor and
  reimbursement conditions remain uncertain.
