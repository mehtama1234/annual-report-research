# Adobe — subscription workflow, AI monetization, and stock-compensation-adjusted cash

## The investment question

Adobe sells creative, document, and customer-experience software through recurring subscriptions. Its control point is embedded workflow: customers store work, rely on file formats, train employees, and connect Adobe tools to business processes. That can make revenue durable, but software durability is not automatic. Customers can reduce seats, shift to lower-priced plans, substitute new AI tools, or decide that parts of the workflow are no longer worth the subscription.

The investment question is whether Adobe can convert recurring subscription demand and AI usage into owner cash after product development, sales, marketing, acquisitions, customer incentives, stock compensation, buybacks, and the loss of pricing power that can occur when software capabilities become easier to reproduce.

## Filed operating bridge

Adobe reported FY2025 revenue of $23.77B, GAAP diluted EPS of $16.70, non-GAAP diluted EPS of $20.94, annual recurring revenue of $25.20B, and operating cash flow of $10.03B. Capital expenditures were approximately $179M, producing headline free cash flow of about $9.85B.

| Fiscal year | Revenue ($B) | Operating cash flow ($B) | Capital expenditures ($B) | Headline FCF ($B) | Stock compensation ($B) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2023 | 17.61 | 7.30 | 0.36 | 6.94 | 1.72 |
| 2024 | 21.51 | 8.06 | 0.18 | 7.87 | 1.83 |
| 2025 | 23.77 | 10.03 | 0.18 | 9.85 | 1.94 |

Subtracting stock compensation from headline free cash flow produces an illustrative cash-after-dilution screen of approximately $5.22B in 2023, $6.04B in 2024, and $7.91B in 2025. This is not a claim that every dollar of stock compensation should be expensed again in every valuation. It is a way to show the cost that is otherwise reflected through share issuance and future dilution. The investor should compare compensation expense with actual share-count reduction and repurchase spending.

## Operating economics

Adobe's subscription model collects revenue over the service period rather than at the first sale. Deferred revenue and remaining performance obligations provide visibility, but they are not the same as permanent demand. A customer can prepay and later not renew. The relevant chain is paid subscription, active usage, renewal, price realization, support and cloud cost, product investment, and cash per diluted share.

ARR rose from $25.20B in Q4 FY2025 to $26.06B in Q1 FY2026 and $27.10B in Q2 FY2026. RPO remained around $22B, while AI-first ARR more than tripled year over year and exceeded $500M in Q2 FY2026. These measures show commercial momentum, but they leave open questions: Is AI revenue incremental or replacing existing plan value? Are customers paying for usage or receiving AI features inside existing plans? What is the inference, storage, support, and customer-success cost of the new usage?

Adobe has three different economic layers. Creative tools depend on professional and individual workflows, document tools depend on business and regulatory processes, and Experience Cloud depends on enterprise marketing budgets and implementation work. A single consolidated ARR number can hide different retention, seat expansion, pricing, and competitive conditions across those layers.

## Earnings quality and forensic questions

1. **ARR versus recognized revenue:** Reconcile ARR growth to billings, deferred revenue, renewal rates, price, seat count, usage, and reported revenue. ARR is a management operating metric, not a GAAP line.
2. **AI monetization:** Separate AI-first ARR, incremental price, usage-based revenue, customer adoption, and gross-margin impact. AI usage can increase compute and support costs before it produces acceptable contribution margin.
3. **Stock compensation:** FY2025 stock compensation of approximately $1.94B was added back in operating cash flow. Compare it with repurchases, diluted shares, employee retention, and the value transferred to employees.
4. **Buyback accounting:** Adobe repurchased roughly $11.76B of common stock in FY2025, more than headline free cash flow, while also repaying 2025 notes. Determine whether buybacks reduced shares at attractive prices or mainly offset compensation dilution.
5. **Acquisition and termination costs:** The Figma transaction termination fee affected prior-period cash and earnings comparisons. Acquisition strategy must be judged by the return on deployed capital, not by the removal of a one-time charge from adjusted results.
6. **Capitalized development and infrastructure:** Low physical capex does not mean zero reinvestment. Track research and development, cloud hosting, data infrastructure, acquired technology, capitalized software, and the cost of maintaining security and reliability.
7. **Customer concentration and enterprise budgets:** Experience Cloud and large enterprise accounts can make revenue visible but expose Adobe to procurement cycles, seat consolidation, and implementation delays.
8. **Management incentives:** Read the proxy for the weight assigned to ARR, revenue, non-GAAP operating income, EPS, free cash flow, relative return, share-count reduction, and AI targets. Incentives can encourage plan bundling or buybacks even when per-user value or cash after dilution is weakening.

## Management incentives and capital allocation

The proxy statement should establish whether annual and long-term awards reward
ARR, revenue, non-GAAP operating income, EPS, free cash flow, net retention,
customer adoption, AI usage, share price, return on invested capital, or cash
per diluted share. Record definitions, weights, threshold/target/maximum
levels, and exclusions for stock compensation, acquisitions, termination fees,
restructuring, and other unusual items.

The incentive tests are:

- Does ARR distinguish marketable price increases and plan bundling from
  customer retention, seat growth, and paid usage?
- Does an AI target charge inference, storage, hosting, support, and customer-
  success cost?
- Does adjusted EPS or free cash flow include stock compensation and the cost
  of repurchasing shares to offset dilution?
- Are acquisitions judged by retained revenue, incremental margin, integration
  cost, and return on capital rather than strategic fit or product count?
- Are buybacks evaluated against cash needed for R&D, security, reliability,
  infrastructure, debt, taxes, and a possible enterprise-budget downturn?

Adobe repurchased roughly $11.76B of stock in FY2025, above headline free cash
flow, while stock compensation was approximately $1.94B. This does not prove
poor capital allocation. It makes the repurchase price, funding source, share-
count change, and cash after dilution central evidence. A compensation system
based on adjusted EPS can reward buybacks even when per-user economics or AI
contribution margin are weakening.

For each major AI or product investment, compare management's stated success
with paid adoption, retention, pricing, gross margin after compute, R&D,
support, and diluted shares. For each acquisition or repurchase, compare the
capital deployed with normalized owner cash and the return required to justify
it.

## Balance-sheet resilience

Adobe's low physical capex does not eliminate balance-sheet risk. The company
must fund R&D, cloud hosting, data and security infrastructure, acquisitions,
taxes, debt, and repurchases. Deferred revenue supplies cash before service
delivery, but it also creates a future obligation. The balance-sheet review
should track cash and investments, debt maturities and interest, deferred
revenue, remaining performance obligations, contract assets, acquisition
commitments, capitalized software, and stock-compensation dilution.

The stress sequence is:

`slower renewal or AI monetization -> lower recurring cash growth -> continued R&D and infrastructure needs -> reduced buyback capacity -> diluted-share and valuation pressure`

The relevant question is whether Adobe can preserve product quality and
customer retention through a slower enterprise or creative-spending cycle
without relying on debt or buying back shares at an unattractive value. A cash-
rich balance sheet provides time; it does not make a weakening subscription
relationship durable.

## Lyn Alden lens

Adobe has low physical capital intensity and recurring contracts, which supports resilience during ordinary capital-market cycles. But long-duration software cash flows are sensitive to discount rates and enterprise budget conditions. The company also depends on customers continuing to fund digital creative, document, and marketing workflows while free alternatives and AI-native competitors improve.

The balance sheet and recurring cash provide flexibility, but aggressive repurchases reduce that flexibility if growth slows or if the company must invest more heavily in AI infrastructure and acquisitions. A stronger macro environment supports enterprise budgets; a weaker one can expose the difference between contract visibility and actual renewal behavior.

## Damodaran lens and valuation

The narrative is “high-margin subscription software with embedded workflows and a new AI growth layer.” The valuation must specify renewal, net retention, pricing, seat growth, AI conversion, gross margin after compute, sales efficiency, R&D, stock compensation, buybacks, tax, and terminal growth.

At a market value of approximately $101.5B, Adobe trades at about 10.3 times FY2025 headline free cash flow and about 12.8 times the illustrative cash-after-stock-compensation screen. A simple scenario screen is:

| Normalized annual owner cash | Multiple | Implied equity value |
|---:|---:|---:|
| $6.5B bear case | 14x | $91B |
| $8.0B base case | 18x | $144B |
| $10.5B strong case | 22x | $231B |

These are scenario outputs, not price targets. The base case assumes Adobe preserves renewal and pricing, converts AI usage into incremental high-margin revenue, controls compute cost, and keeps dilution from absorbing too much of the cash returned through repurchases.

## Thesis breakers and next evidence

The thesis weakens if ARR grows mainly through price or bundling while renewal and active usage weaken; if AI-first ARR grows but gross margin falls; if customer acquisition and support costs rise faster than recurring revenue; if stock compensation remains near $2B while buybacks primarily offset dilution; if Experience Cloud depends on weak marketing budgets; or if free alternatives and AI-native products reduce willingness to pay.

The next filings should answer: Did ARR growth convert into revenue and cash per diluted share? Did AI create incremental paid usage? What happened to gross margin after AI cost? Did diluted shares fall after the buybacks? Did deferred revenue and RPO support actual renewals? Did R&D and infrastructure spending rise at a rate consistent with maintaining the product advantage? And did management incentives reward durable cash after dilution rather than only adjusted EPS?
