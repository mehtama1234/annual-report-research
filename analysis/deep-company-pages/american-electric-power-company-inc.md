# American Electric Power: Large-Load Demand, Regulated Construction, and Financing Risk

Date baseline: `2026-09-14`

Evidence base: AEP fiscal `2025` Form 10-K, fiscal `2025` Q4, fiscal `2026`
Q1 and Q2 results materials, and the company packet. The annual cash figures
below use operating cash flow and construction expenditures from the filed
cash-flow statements. The dated scenario register preserves a September 14,
2026 equity-value input; it is an expectations screen, not a live quote or
price target.

## Bottom line

AEP owns a large regulated electric system serving about `5.6 million` customers
across `11` states. It operates transmission, distribution, generation, and
customer relationships. Its current investment narrative is unusually tied to
large new loads: management reported signed incremental load of about `69 GW`
by 2030, a five-year capital plan of `$78B`, and further investment potential
beyond that plan.

The important distinction is between demand evidence and owner economics. A
signed load agreement is not collected revenue. A customer commitment does not
automatically mean a project is approved, built, energized, recovered through
rates, and profitable after financing. AEP is therefore a strong test of whether
AI and industrial load growth improves a regulated utility or mainly expands its
capital and balance-sheet requirements.

## 1. What AEP controls

AEP controls a regulated power-delivery system: transmission lines,
distribution lines, substations, generation resources, interconnection work,
and the customer relationships tied to those assets.

Its control point is not simply ownership of electricity generation. It is the
combination of:

- essential local and regional grid access;
- the ability to connect large customers;
- reliability and restoration performance;
- regulatory approval for prudent investment; and
- the financing and construction capacity to turn demand into service.

Rate base is the regulated asset value on which a regulator may allow a return.
It is not cash profit. AEP spends first, places assets in service, obtains
recovery, and collects the return over future customer bills.

## 2. Three-year cash and construction bridge

| Year | Operating cash flow | Construction expenditures | Mechanical cash after construction |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 2023 | `$5.012B` | `$7.378B` | `($2.366B)` |
| 2024 | `$6.804B` | `$7.631B` | `($0.827B)` |
| 2025 | `$6.944B` | `$8.453B` | `($1.509B)` |

This is a diagnostic residual, not AEP's official free-cash-flow measure. It
shows that operating cash did not fund construction in any of the three years.
The gap must be covered by debt, equity, asset sales, customer contributions,
securitization, or existing liquidity.

The economic bridge is:

`large-load agreement -> interconnection and generation investment -> in-service asset -> rate recovery -> earned return -> common cash per share`

The failure points are project delay, customer cancellation, regulatory
disallowance, construction inflation, financing cost, and affordability
pressure.

## 3. Current demand and capital sequence

The packet records the following sequence:

- signed incremental load rose from `28 GW` in late 2025 to `56 GW`, then to
  approximately `63 GW`, and later `69 GW` by 2030;
- the five-year capital plan rose from `$72B` to `$78B`;
- management said new additions could drive nearly `11%` annual rate-base
  growth;
- AEP secured about `13 GW` of gas-fired turbine capacity and was evaluating
  another `10 GW`; and
- management highlighted up to `$16B` of customer cost offsets and about
  `$1.4B` of projected customer savings from federal loans and grants.

The demand increase is meaningful, but each number measures a different stage.
Signed load is customer intent or contracted expectation. Turbine procurement
is supply preparation. A capital plan is management's intended spending. Rate
base is an asset value subject to regulation. None is the same as cash received
from a customer or return earned by shareholders.

## 4. Earnings quality and accounting risks

### Operating earnings versus GAAP earnings

AEP reported fiscal 2025 GAAP earnings of `$3.58B`, or `$6.70` per share, and
operating earnings of `$3.19B`, or `$5.97` per share. The difference requires a
line-by-line review of tax items, regulatory effects, asset sales, impairment,
storm cost, financing, and other exclusions.

Adjusted operating earnings can be useful for understanding the regulated
business. They should not be used to ignore costs that recur because AEP must
operate a large and exposed system. Storm restoration, environmental work,
regulatory proceedings, and financing are part of the economic burden even when
the accounting presentation labels an item unusual.

### Regulatory assets and customer cost offsets

Regulatory assets represent costs that regulators may allow the company to
recover from customers in future periods. Grants, loans, securitization, and
customer contributions can reduce the amount shareholders must finance, but
they also bring eligibility requirements, timing risk, and political conditions.

The forensic test is whether the announced cost offsets reduce actual common
equity funding and interest expense, or merely reduce the headline project cost
while the shareholder still carries construction and execution risk.

### Working capital and fuel inventory

The 2025 annual materials identified working-capital pressure from fuel,
materials and supplies, accounts receivable timing, and tax credits. These are
normal utility cash movements, but a strong operating-cash number should be
tested for temporary tax and collection timing before it is capitalized into a
valuation.

## 5. Alden lens: durability and liquidity

AEP benefits from durable electricity demand and regulated customer access. The
large-load pipeline may improve utilization and spread fixed network costs if
customers actually connect and pay.

Liquidity risk is high relative to a software control company because the
company must fund construction well before the full return is collected. The
main stress points are:

- interest rates and debt refinancing;
- equity issuance and dilution;
- turbine, fuel, labor, and construction inflation;
- project and interconnection delays;
- customer credit and cancellation risk;
- state and federal regulatory approval; and
- customer affordability when large capital programs raise bills.

The durable case requires both load growth and funding discipline. Demand that
forces the company to issue large amounts of debt or equity at unattractive
terms can increase accounting earnings while reducing the return per existing
share.

## 6. Damodaran lens: translate the load narrative

The narrative is that AEP is positioned for unprecedented demand and long-term
rate-base growth. The required numbers are:

1. signed load that becomes executed, approved, and energized load;
2. customer contributions, deposits, and credit support;
3. capital spending by transmission, distribution, generation, and customer;
4. in-service timing and regulatory recovery by project;
5. allowed ROE versus earned ROE;
6. operating cash after fuel, receivables, taxes, and working capital;
7. debt and equity required to fund the plan;
8. interest expense and credit metrics; and
9. common cash per diluted share after dividends and construction spending.

The lifecycle question is whether AEP is entering a long demand cycle or
capitalizing a short period of data-center enthusiasm. A large pipeline has
value only if the customer remains creditworthy, the project is built, and the
regulator permits an adequate return.

## 7. Control point versus burden carrier

| Layer | AEP captures | AEP carries |
|---|---|---|
| Regulated grid | Essential access and customer relationship | Reliability, safety, storms, restoration, and public scrutiny |
| Large-load connection | Customer demand and potential cost sharing | Interconnection, generation, transmission, and customer credit |
| Rate base | Allowed return on prudent assets | Construction cash, regulatory lag, disallowance, and financing |
| Capital plan | Future earnings visibility | Debt, equity dilution, labor, materials, permits, and execution |
| Federal and customer support | Lower net project burden | Eligibility, timing, political conditions, and compliance |

AEP captures a protected relationship, but shareholders finance much of the
transition from demand to service. Large customers may fund part of the cost;
the remaining burden can still fall on ratepayers, lenders, and common equity.

## 8. Valuation readiness

The registered scenario screen uses a preserved current equity-value snapshot
of approximately `$67.9B`. Utility valuation should begin with the capital and
recovery bridge:

`equity value -> rate-base growth -> allowed and earned return -> financing and dilution -> sustainable common earnings`

The registered bear, base, and bull common-earnings cases are `$1.8B`, `$3.0B`,
and `$4.5B`, valued at `12x`, `15x`, and `18x`, for implied values of `$21.6B`,
`$45.0B`, and `$81.0B`. This is a risk-adjusted rate-base screen, not a
post-construction owner-cash calculation. The base case requires signed load
to become credit-supported and energized, customer contributions to reduce
common-equity funding, and the capital plan to earn recoverable returns.

Bear, base, and bull cases should vary signed-load conversion, in-service dates,
capital spending, customer contributions, allowed ROE, debt cost, equity
issuance, and affordability outcomes. A valuation based only on `69 GW` of
expected load would treat customer intent as shareholder cash.

## 9. Next-filing watchlist and thesis breakers

1. How much of the `69 GW` load is executed, credit-supported, approved, and
   energized rather than merely signed?
2. Does the `$78B` capital plan remain within cost and schedule?
3. Does rate-base growth become in-service assets earning approved returns?
4. Do customer contributions and federal support reduce common-equity funding?
5. Does operating cash grow after fuel, receivables, tax credits, and inventory?
6. Does debt grow faster than rate base and operating earnings?
7. Is equity issued in a way that materially dilutes existing shareholders?
8. Do gas-turbine procurement and generation additions create stranded or
   underutilized capital if load is delayed?
9. Do customer bills and affordability concerns limit regulatory recovery?
10. Does common cash per diluted share support dividends after construction and
    interest?

The thesis breaks if signed load does not become paying load, if construction
spending rises without timely rate recovery, if financing absorbs the allowed
return, or if customer affordability prevents the assumed capital plan from
being recovered.

## Conclusion

AEP is a strong second-order AI-infrastructure case because it controls the
regulated grid required by data centers and industrial customers. Its economic
position is more durable than a merchant-power forecast, but its capital burden
is much heavier than a software control platform.

The core conclusion is conditional: large-load demand can improve the utility's
growth opportunity, but it does not prove value creation. The decisive evidence
will be executed and collected load, capital entering rate base, earned returns,
financing cost, customer affordability, and common cash per diluted share.

### Evidence grades

- `A`: FY2023–FY2025 operating cash flow and construction expenditures from the
  filed annual cash-flow statements.
- `A`: FY2025–FY2026 signed-load, capital-plan, turbine-capacity, and earnings-
  guidance figures from AEP's filed investor materials.
- `B`: Customer cost-offset and federal-support estimates; these require future
  filings to confirm realized funding and recovery.
- `Open`: Project-level earned returns and exact load-contract cancellation and
  credit terms. The current equity-value screen is preserved in the scenario
  register; it is not a live quote.
