# American Express — closed-loop payments, premium membership, and credit-cycle cash

## The investment question

American Express combines a card network, card issuer, merchant-acquisition relationships, premium membership products, travel and dining benefits, and a lending balance sheet. It controls more of the payment relationship than a pure network, which allows it to collect merchant discount revenue, annual card fees, interest income, and data about spending and repayment.

That integration also means the company carries more obligations than a fee-only payment network. It funds receivables and loans, absorbs credit losses, pays rewards, maintains capital, invests in marketing and technology, and must preserve merchant acceptance. The question is whether the Membership Model produces durable common-owner earnings after those costs through a full credit and liquidity cycle.

Related first-principles explanation: [Financial intermediation, fees, float, and liquidity](../first-principles/financial-intermediation-fees-float-and-liquidity.md).

## Filed operating bridge

American Express reported 2025 revenue of $72.2B, net income of $10.8B, diluted EPS of $15.38, and billed business of approximately $1.67T. Net card fee revenue grew double digits for the 30th consecutive quarter. More than 70% of new proprietary accounts were on fee-paying products, and Millennial and Gen Z customers represented roughly 65% of new global consumer-account acquisitions.

| Fiscal year | Net income ($B) | Total revenues net of interest expense after credit-loss provisions ($B) | Key credit-loss provision ($B) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 2023 | 8.37 | 55.59 | 4.92 |
| 2024 | 10.13 | 60.76 | 5.19 |
| 2025 | 10.83 | 66.97 | 5.26 |

Net income is the relevant starting measure here, not industrial free cash flow. Card receivables, loans, deposits, rewards, regulatory capital, and settlement obligations make a conventional operating-cash-minus-capex calculation difficult to interpret. The owner-cash question is whether earnings remain available after credit losses, required capital growth, rewards, technology, marketing, and prudent balance-sheet funding.

## Q2 2026 checkpoint: membership growth still works only if credit and rewards stay controlled

Q2 2026 reported revenue was approximately `$19.6B`, net income was about
`$3.1B`, and diluted EPS was `$4.53`. Management maintained strong premium
spending momentum, raised full-year revenue-growth guidance to `10%`, and
continued to emphasize younger premium customers and fee-paying products.

The quarter supports the demand and membership parts of the thesis. It does
not settle the owner-return question because American Express must fund the
receivables and loans created by that spending, pay rewards and member
benefits, maintain merchant acceptance, and retain capital against credit risk.
The correct quarterly bridge is:

`billed business -> merchant discount and card-fee revenue -> rewards and member costs -> funding cost -> credit losses -> required capital -> common distributions`

The next filing should show billed business, net card-fee growth, loans and
receivables, provision and net write-off rates, Membership Rewards liability,
funding mix, and capital ratios together. New-account growth is not enough if
activation, retention, rewards cost, or lifetime credit contribution weakens.

This is the key contrast with JPMorgan. JPMorgan owns a much broader deposit,
commercial, and capital-markets balance sheet; American Express owns a more
focused premium member and merchant relationship but carries concentrated
consumer-credit, rewards, and spending-cycle exposure. The cleaner relationship
does not automatically mean cleaner common cash.

## Where the economics sit

The economic chain is: card-member spending, merchant acceptance, merchant discount revenue, card fees, interest income, rewards and services, credit losses, funding costs, operating expense, and capital return. The closed-loop system creates data and relationship advantages, but each link has a cost.

Merchant discount revenue was approximately $37.4B in 2025. Card-member rewards, business development, and card-member services were approximately $7.2B in the consumer-services segment and materially larger across the consolidated system. Marketing was approximately $1.3B in that segment. These costs are not optional if the company must keep premium benefits attractive and merchant acceptance broad.

The Membership Rewards liability was approximately $16.5B at December 31, 2025. It represents future points redemption and related obligations, not excess cash. Changes in redemption behavior, partner economics, travel demand, breakage assumptions, and reward cost can affect both earnings and future cash. The liability should be tracked against billed business, points issuance, redemption, and partner payments.

American Express reported stable recent credit metrics, but credit quality is cyclical. Provisions for credit losses were approximately $5.3B in 2025 and net write-offs rose with growth in loans and receivables. A premium customer base can reduce risk relative to some lenders; it cannot eliminate unemployment, household leverage, interest-rate, or consumer-spending risk.

### What the reported earnings number does and does not mean

For a bank-like card issuer, net income is a more useful starting point than a
simple industrial free-cash-flow calculation. Card receivables are assets that
must remain funded, and deposits, debt, securitizations, rewards obligations,
settlement balances, and regulatory capital are part of the operating model.
The common-owner calculation is therefore:

`revenue and interest income - rewards and member benefits - funding cost - credit losses - operating cost - required capital growth`

The last item is not an expense in the income statement, but it is a real limit
on distributable cash. If receivables grow, American Express may need to retain
earnings or raise funding even when reported net income is strong. Dividends and
buybacks are sustainable only after that funding and capital requirement is met.

### Control point and burden carrier

| Layer | What American Express controls | Main burden carried |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Card member | Premium product, annual-fee relationship, rewards, service, and spending data | Rewards cost, benefits, marketing, retention, fraud, and credit exposure |
| Merchant | Acceptance relationship and merchant discount collection | Acceptance investment, pricing, settlement, fraud, and merchant incentives |
| Issuer balance sheet | Receivables, loans, underwriting, funding, and repayment data | Credit losses, liquidity, capital, interest-rate exposure, and regulation |
| Common shareholder | Residual earnings after member, merchant, funding, and capital claims | Down-cycle losses, dilution, lower returns, and capital constraints |

The closed-loop structure gives American Express more information and control
over the transaction than a fee-only network. It also prevents the company from
claiming the fee economics of a network while ignoring the credit and funding
costs of an issuer.

## Earnings quality and forensic questions

1. **Billed business versus revenue:** Spending volume creates merchant fees only after discount rates, rewards, partner economics, fraud, and acceptance costs. Test whether billed-business growth produces revenue and earnings per share after those costs.
2. **Credit-loss assumptions:** Reconcile provisions, reserve balances, delinquency, net write-offs, vintage performance, loan growth, receivable growth, and macroeconomic assumptions. A reserve release can improve earnings without improving underlying credit economics.
3. **Rewards liability:** Test redemption rates, breakage, reward-cost inflation, travel and dining partner settlements, and whether the liability is adequate under higher redemption or lower partner reimbursement.
4. **Premium-card acquisition:** New accounts and fee-paying products are not enough. Measure activation, spend, retention, annual fee realization, benefits cost, acquisition cost, and lifetime contribution.
5. **Funding and liquidity:** Card receivables require funding through deposits, debt, securitization, and other liabilities. Track interest expense, deposit stability, maturity schedules, liquidity buffers, and regulatory capital.
6. **Capital returns:** Buybacks and dividends should be judged after credit-loss reserves, capital requirements, stress-test results, and balance-sheet growth. A high return on equity can reflect leverage and low capital allocation, not only superior operating economics.
7. **Non-GAAP adjustments:** Management may exclude restructuring, operating investments, or transaction effects from adjusted results. Determine whether “investment” costs recur and whether adjusted EPS growth remains after rewards, credit, and marketing spending.
8. **Management incentives:** Read the proxy for weights on revenue, EPS, billed business, new accounts, net income, credit performance, return on equity, and capital. Incentives tied heavily to account acquisition or spending can encourage growth before lifetime credit and rewards economics are known.

| Capital returns are distributable after balance-sheet growth | Net income and capital-return framework are available, but required capital growth must be tested against receivable and loan growth | Unresolved question |

These are reconciliation questions, not allegations of wrongdoing. The most
important missing proof is whether customer growth remains profitable after the
company pays for rewards, funds the receivables, absorbs lifetime credit losses,
and retains the capital required by regulation.

## Balance-sheet resilience and management incentives

The stress sequence matters: consumer spending slows, revolving balances may
remain high, delinquencies rise, credit losses increase, funding becomes more
expensive, and capital requirements restrict buybacks. American Express can
remain strategically strong while common-owner cash becomes less distributable.
The next filing should therefore be read for deposit stability, funding
maturities, liquidity buffers, reserve coverage, receivable growth, net
write-offs, and capital ratios together rather than separately.

Management incentives should be tested against the full customer life cycle.
Revenue, billed business, new accounts, and adjusted EPS can all rise before
the costs of rewards, acquisition, servicing, and credit losses are fully known.
The proxy should show whether performance pay includes credit-adjusted return,
retention, loss performance, capital strength, and diluted-share outcomes. If
the scorecard rewards acquisition and spending volume without those controls,
growth can shift costs into later periods.

## Lyn Alden lens

American Express benefits from nominal spending growth, premium consumption, travel, dining, and global card adoption. Its earnings are also directly exposed to liquidity conditions, employment, household leverage, funding costs, credit losses, and regulatory capital. A strong consumer can support both billed business and repayment; a liquidity contraction can weaken spending while increasing losses and funding pressure.

The balance sheet is the central resilience test. A closed-loop network may remain strategically valuable during a downturn, but common-owner returns are not protected unless the company holds enough capital and reserves to absorb losses while continuing to fund the network and reward obligations.

## Damodaran lens and valuation

The narrative is “premium membership, trusted payments, first-party data, and a high-quality customer base.” The valuation must specify billed-business growth, discount rate, card-fee growth, interest spread, loan growth, credit losses, rewards cost, marketing, operating expense, regulatory capital, payout, and terminal growth.

At a market value of approximately $220.5B, the equity trades at about 20.4 times 2025 net income. A simple earnings-owner screen is:

| Normalized annual common earnings ($B) | Multiple | Implied equity value |
|---:|---:|---:|
| $9B bear case | 15x | $135B |
| $11B base case | 20x | $220B |
| $13B strong case | 24x | $312B |

These are scenario outputs, not price targets. The base case assumes premium-card fees and billed business remain durable, credit losses normalize without a reserve release, rewards remain economically funded, and capital returns do not weaken regulatory resilience.

## Thesis breakers and next evidence

The thesis weakens if billed-business growth comes from balance-sheet expansion rather than profitable spending; if fee growth requires benefits that cost more than the fees; if credit losses rise faster than pricing and fee income; if rewards liability assumptions prove too low; if funding costs or deposit competition compress spreads; if new accounts have weak activation or retention; or if capital returns reduce resilience before a downturn.

The next filings should answer: Did spending convert into revenue after rewards and credit costs? Did new premium accounts activate and retain? Did net write-offs and reserve coverage remain consistent with loan growth? Did the Membership Rewards liability grow at a manageable rate? Did funding and CET1 remain within targets? And does executive pay reward lifetime contribution and credit-adjusted returns rather than only new accounts, billed business, or adjusted EPS?
