# American Water Works — regulated water renewal, acquisitions, and affordability

## The investment question

American Water Works owns and operates regulated water and wastewater systems. Its control point is a local essential-service network: customers need reliable water, treatment, quality testing, and system maintenance, while regulators determine how quickly prudent investment can be recovered through rates.

The investment question is whether the company can turn infrastructure renewal, water-quality compliance, resiliency, and system acquisitions into common earnings and dividends without overloading customers, relying excessively on debt, or assuming that every acquired connection earns an acceptable return.

Related first-principles explanation: [Rate-base growth versus owner return](../first-principles/rate-base-growth-versus-owner-return.md).

## Filed operating bridge

American Water reported 2025 operating revenues of $5.140B, net income of approximately $1.111B, and operating cash flow of $2.059B. Capital expenditures were $3.126B. The company added nearly 40,000 customer connections through acquisitions and organic growth and invested approximately $3.2B in capital.

| Fiscal year | Operating revenue ($B) | Net income ($B) | Operating cash flow ($B) | Capital expenditures ($B) | Post-capex diagnostic ($B) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2023 | 4.234 | 0.944 | 1.874 | 2.575 | (0.701) |
| 2024 | 4.684 | 1.051 | 2.045 | 2.856 | (0.811) |
| 2025 | 5.140 | 1.111 | 2.059 | 3.126 | (1.067) |

The negative post-capex diagnostic is expected in an infrastructure-renewal model, but it is still economically important. The company must finance the difference through debt, equity, customer contributions, rate recovery, and future operating cash. A regulated asset becomes valuable only when it is placed in service, accepted as prudent, included in rate base, and paid for by customers without unacceptable affordability or political resistance.

## Where the economics sit

The chain is: water demand and public-health obligation, treatment and distribution infrastructure, operating and maintenance labor, capital investment, regulatory approval, customer rates, collections, debt service, and common returns. Unlike electric utilities, the growth case is not mainly new load. It is system replacement, water quality, resilience, compliance, and consolidation of fragmented local systems.

American Water describes itself as the largest regulated water and wastewater utility in the United States, serving approximately 14 million people. Management planned $46–48B of investment over the next decade in infrastructure renewal, water quality, resiliency, technology, and acquisitions. The scale creates a long runway, but it also creates a long financing obligation.

Affordability is part of the regulatory compact. Management emphasizes that average residential water bills remain below 1% of median household income. That is relevant evidence, but the average can hide local variation, low-income stress, arrears, disconnections, and the cumulative effect of repeated rate increases. The next filings should connect rate-base growth to actual customer payment behavior and regulatory outcomes.

Acquisitions add approximately 40,000 connections in 2025 and approximately 104,000 connections under agreements at year-end. Acquired systems may provide operating scale and a path to capital investment, but purchase price, deferred maintenance, environmental liabilities, customer rates, and integration costs determine whether connections create value. Connection growth is not equivalent to earnings growth.

## Earnings quality and forensic questions

1. **Rate recovery versus cash:** Reconcile authorized revenue increases, regulatory assets, earned returns, lag, disallowances, and collections. Revenue authorized by a commission is not the same as cash already collected.
2. **Capitalized costs:** Track operation and maintenance expense versus capitalized construction, project work in progress, and depreciation. Capitalizing too much labor or overhead can improve current earnings while increasing future rate and depreciation burdens.
3. **Customer contributions:** The company reported advances and contributions in aid of construction. Separate customer-funded capital from shareholder-funded capital when judging the return on invested equity.
4. **Acquisition returns:** For each acquisition cohort, track purchase price, connections, revenue, operating cost, needed remediation, capex, regulatory approval, and incremental earnings. A roll-up can grow rate base while producing weak returns if purchase prices and repair needs rise.
5. **Water-quality and environmental obligations:** PFAS, lead service lines, wastewater treatment, drought, contamination, and resiliency requirements can create large capital and operating obligations. Regulatory recovery may be delayed or incomplete.
6. **Working capital and receivables:** Receivables and unbilled revenues increased by approximately $145M in 2025. Monitor customer arrears, collection experience, and the provision for losses on accounts receivable.
7. **Debt and interest:** Long-term debt was approximately $12.8B at year-end 2025, and interest paid was approximately $568M. Rising rates and capital spending can pressure coverage before new rate cases become effective.
8. **Dividend coverage:** Dividends paid were approximately $633M in 2025. Compare dividends with operating cash after recurring infrastructure capital, not only with net income or adjusted EPS.
9. **Management incentives:** Read the proxy for weights on EPS, rate base, customer connections, capital deployment, service quality, affordability, safety, and realized returns. Incentives tied mainly to rate-base or acquisition growth can encourage expansion without sufficient return discipline.

## Management incentives and capital allocation

The proxy statement should establish whether annual and long-term awards reward
rate-base growth, adjusted EPS, operating cash flow, free cash flow, return on
equity, customer connections, service quality, safety, affordability, debt
metrics, credit ratings, share price, or dividend growth. Record definitions,
weights, threshold/target/maximum levels, and exclusions for acquisitions,
storm costs, PFAS and other compliance items, regulatory assets, restructuring,
and financing effects.

The incentive tests are:

- Does a connection or rate-base target count only investment placed in service
  and approved for recovery?
- Does adjusted EPS exclude recurring water-quality, environmental,
  remediation, storm, or regulatory costs?
- Does a growth target charge debt, interest, equity issuance,
  dividend-reinvestment dilution, regulatory lag, and customer affordability?
- Are acquired systems evaluated by post-integration cash, repair needs,
  earned return, and customer retention rather than connection count?
- Are dividends and repurchases evaluated after infrastructure renewal,
  compliance, liquidity, and credit requirements?

A rate-base or acquisition target can increase reported scale while reducing
per-share returns if the company pays too much, inherits deferred maintenance,
or funds the timing gap with debt and equity. A cash-per-diluted-share and
earned-return measure is more complete only if it charges customer-funded
capital, regulatory assets, required maintenance, and unrecovered compliance
spending. The proxy must show which design is actually used.

For each acquisition, compare purchase price and assumed liabilities with
connections, rate base, repair capex, operating cost, rate approval, bill
impact, debt, dilution, and post-integration cash. For each capital return,
compare cash paid with owner cash after renewal capex and customer-support
obligations.

## Balance-sheet resilience

American Water's negative post-capex diagnostic means that the rate-base plan
requires funding before all recovery arrives. Track debt maturities, interest
expense, credit ratios, equity issuance, customer contributions, regulatory
assets, construction work in progress, cash, and available liquidity. A
customer contribution reduces shareholder funding but does not remove the
utility's obligation to operate and maintain the system.

The stress sequence is:

`construction and compliance spending -> regulatory lag or delayed recovery -> debt and interest growth -> higher customer bills or equity funding -> lower common-owner return`

Stress the model for higher construction cost, slower rate cases, PFAS or
environmental disallowance, customer arrears, acquisition repair, and higher
interest rates. The utility may remain solvent while common-owner economics
weaken through dilution, lower earned returns, or reduced dividend capacity.

The next filing should reconcile capital placed in service, rate-base approval,
regulatory-asset recovery, customer contributions, debt, interest, cash
collections, and diluted shares. A large approved capital plan is not proof of
cash available to common owners.

## Lyn Alden lens

Water demand is essential and relatively defensive, but the capital required to maintain safe systems is sensitive to inflation, interest rates, municipal budgets, and household affordability. Regulated recovery provides visibility, but it does not guarantee that real purchasing power or shareholder returns rise faster than the cost of construction and financing.

The balance sheet is the key resilience test. American Water can fund investment with debt, equity, customer contributions, and operating cash, but each source has a cost. Debt increases fixed claims, equity dilutes existing holders, customer contributions can reduce the shareholder-funded base, and operating cash may be insufficient during heavy renewal years.

## Damodaran lens and valuation

The narrative is “essential water monopoly with a long infrastructure-renewal and consolidation runway.” The valuation must specify rate-base growth, allowed return, regulatory lag, customer affordability, debt cost, equity funding, acquisition price, system repair cost, operating efficiency, dividend payout, and terminal growth.

At a market value of approximately $27.0B, the equity is about 24 times 2025 net income. A simple earnings and rate-base screen is:

| Normalized annual common earnings | Multiple | Implied equity value |
|---:|---:|---:|
| $800M bear case | 20x | $16B |
| $1.1B base case | 25x | $27.5B |
| $1.5B strong case | 30x | $45B |

These are scenario outputs, not price targets. The base case assumes American Water converts its capital plan into allowed and collected revenue, maintains customer affordability, controls acquisition costs, and funds dividends without compromising credit quality.

## Thesis breakers and next evidence

The thesis weakens if rate cases lag construction spending, affordability limits recovery, customer arrears rise, environmental obligations exceed allowed returns, acquisition prices increase, acquired systems require recurring remediation, debt grows faster than rate base, or capitalized costs conceal weak operating cash.

The next filings should answer: How much capital entered rate base? What return was actually earned? Did customer contributions reduce shareholder funding? Did acquired connections produce incremental cash after repair and integration? Did receivables and arrears normalize? Did debt and interest remain manageable? And did water-quality compliance spending improve service without creating unrecovered liabilities?
