# Aon plc Full Forensic Analysis Memo

Date baseline: 2026-09-13  
Primary source: [Aon 2025 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/315293/000162828026008116/aon-20251231.htm)  
Companion packet: [Aon company packet](/home/mehtama1/git-repo/annual-report-research/extracted/financial/insurance-brokers/aon-plc/company-packet.md)

## Bottom line

Aon is a fee-led advisory and insurance-broker platform. It helps corporate,
institutional, and individual clients navigate commercial risk, reinsurance,
health, benefits, wealth, and talent decisions. Unlike an insurer, Aon does
not primarily warehouse the underwriting risk associated with the policies it
places. Its control point is the client relationship, risk advice, analytics,
placement workflow, and recurring renewal process.

The 2025 filing supports a strong operating read: revenue was approximately
$17.2B, operating income $4.344B, net income attributable to shareholders
$3.695B, and operating cash flow $3.481B. Organic revenue growth was 6% for a
second consecutive year, and operating margin improved to 25.3% from 24.4%.

The skeptical read is equally important. Aon is not capital-free: the company
has substantial debt, acquisition and integration exposure, pension
requirements, restructuring costs, fiduciary flows, foreign exchange, and
client-collection risk. 2025 cash flow benefited from non-cash amortization,
share compensation, and a $1.201B gain from business sales, while Aon spent
$394M on acquisitions and $263M on capital expenditures.

Current judgment: Aon is a cleaner relationship-owner than an underwriter,
but the investment case depends on retention, fee-rate and organic-growth
quality, acquisition returns, operating-cost discipline, and whether recurring
cash remains strong after restructuring, debt, pensions, and dilution.

## What the company actually is

Aon is an institutional decision-support and distribution system:

`client risk or workforce problem -> analytics and advice -> insurance / benefits / capital-market placement -> renewal and recurring advisory relationship`

The company monetizes complexity. Its services are embedded in annual renewal
cycles, benefit design, retirement planning, reinsurance placement, M&A risk,
and workforce decisions. Aon generally earns fees, commissions, and advisory
revenue without taking the same direct underwriting exposure as the insurer.

That positioning is valuable but not unassailable. Clients can insource parts
of risk analytics, move between brokers, consolidate procurement, or pressure
commission rates. The company's bargaining power comes from expertise, global
coordination, data, market access, and the cost of making a bad risk decision.

The trend contains both strength and noise:

- 2025 revenue rose 9%, while organic revenue growth was 6%; the balance was
  largely acquisitions and currency.
- Operating income rose 13%, supported by organic growth and $160M of net
  restructuring savings.
- Amortization and impairment of intangible assets rose to $778M in 2025 from
  $503M in 2024, reflecting the acquisition-heavy model.
- Operating cash flow of $3.481B was below net income plus non-cash charges in
  an ordinary sense but remained substantial; working-capital timing and
  business-sale gains require normalization.
- The 2025 result is cleaner than a mark-driven financial institution's result,
  but not free of acquisition and restructuring accounting.

## 2026 first-half filing update

The six months ended June 30, 2026 provide a more precise cash and acquisition
test. Aon reported $986M of operating cash flow, $140M of capital expenditures,
and $322M of acquisition cash, producing approximately $524M after those
listed uses. Management reported $846M of free cash flow before acquisitions,
but that measure is explicitly not residual cash available for all discretionary
uses.

The cash-flow statement also shows $1.394B of net sales of non-fiduciary
short-term investments and $21M of business-sale proceeds. Those are liquidity
movements, not recurring advisory earnings, and should not be annualized into
owner cash. The six-month cash-flow bridge also includes a $20M gain from sales
of businesses and a $519M “other assets and liabilities” movement, so working
capital and disposition effects remain important reconciliation fields.

Operating performance remains supportive but not conclusive. Organic revenue
growth was 5% in the second quarter and 5% for the first half; adjusted
operating margin was 28.9% in the quarter and 34.4% for the first half, with
$25M and $50M of net restructuring savings, respectively. The filing says
first-half acquisitions totaled eight with $431M of aggregate consideration,
including $376M cash and $55M deferred or contingent consideration. The results
of those acquisitions were not materially different if reported from the start
of the period, but that statement does not yet establish their return on
capital or client retention.

The first-half evidence therefore supports the operating-growth case while
keeping the cash-quality grade at B: recurring growth and savings are visible,
but investment sales, acquisition cash, restructuring savings, and the large
“other assets and liabilities” movement must stay outside a simple annualized
owner-cash number.

## Earnings quality and possible financial engineering

### 1. Organic growth is useful, but its exclusions must be understood

Aon defines organic revenue growth by excluding foreign exchange, fiduciary
investment income, acquisitions and divestitures, certain transfers, and
hedging effects. Organic growth was 6% in both 2025 and 2024. This is a useful
measure of ongoing client demand, but it is not GAAP revenue and may include
the organic contribution of acquired businesses for part of a comparable
period.

The tests are:

- reconcile reported revenue to organic revenue by segment;
- distinguish client retention, new business, pricing, exposures, and market
  impact;
- monitor whether organic growth persists after an acquired business is fully
  included in the comparison; and
- compare organic growth with headcount, compensation, technology, and
  receivables growth.

6% organic growth with stable or improving cash per employee is stronger than
6% organic growth purchased with rising compensation and collection risk.

### 2. Restructuring savings are real only if the cost program ends

Aon’s Accelerating Aon United program generated an estimated $450M of
annualized expense savings by the end of 2027. It incurred $365M of program
costs in 2025 and had realized $270M of cumulative annualized savings since
the beginning of the program.

This is not automatically an accounting problem. The forensic question is
whether “one-time” restructuring costs recur as technology, headcount, and
real-estate programs continue. If the company repeatedly adds back program
charges while reporting adjusted margin gains, adjusted earnings may be
overstating the steady-state cost of operating the platform.

The next filings should show cumulative cash spent, remaining commitments,
actual annualized savings, employee and technology impacts, and whether
service quality or retention changes.

### 3. Business-sale gains flatter GAAP cash and earnings comparisons

2025 operating cash flow began with net income and adjusted for a $1.201B gain
from sales of businesses. Investing cash flow included $2.349B of proceeds from
business sales, compared with $394M spent on acquisitions. This produced
positive investing cash flow even though the underlying company is an
acquisition and divestiture platform.

The correct normalized view should separate:

`recurring advisory cash -> acquisition cash -> divestiture proceeds -> debt reduction / buybacks -> shareholder cash`

Business sales may improve portfolio quality and fund deleveraging, but they
are not recurring operating income. Aon’s 2025 earnings and cash should not be
annualized as if a $1.2B gain or $2.3B sale proceeds will repeat.

### 4. Fiduciary assets are not Aon’s unrestricted assets

Aon reported $17.889B of fiduciary assets and an equal $17.889B of fiduciary
liabilities at year-end. The company collects insurance premiums and claims on
behalf of clients and remits them to insurers. Those funds pass through Aon in
a fiduciary capacity.

This is a classic denominator and balance-sheet trap. Fiduciary assets can make
the balance sheet look much larger without representing shareholder capital.
They can also create timing, operational, regulatory, and collection risk. The
analysis must exclude fiduciary cash when assessing corporate liquidity while
monitoring the corresponding fiduciary liabilities and controls.

## Cash conversion and capital requirements

### Simplified 2025 cash bridge

| Approximate cash after capex and acquisitions | 2.824 |
| Share repurchases | (1.000) |
| Dividends | (0.629) |

The approximate $2.824B residual is not unrestricted owner cash. It is before
debt repayment, pension contributions, restructuring cash, taxes, and any
working-capital normalization. Aon contributed $99M to pension plans in 2025
and expects approximately $93M of cash contributions in 2026.

The core capital requirements are comparatively light versus an insurer or
industrial manufacturer: technology, offices, data infrastructure, talent,
acquisitions, and working capital. However, “light” does not mean zero. Aon
must keep investing to preserve analytics, client service, cybersecurity,
cross-border capabilities, and regulatory compliance.

## Balance-sheet resilience and liquidity

At December 31, 2025, Aon reported $1.195B of cash and cash equivalents,
$1.603B of short-term investments, $14.660B of long-term debt, and $589M of
short-term debt/current maturities. Total assets were $50.784B, but $17.889B
of fiduciary assets was matched by fiduciary liabilities and is not ordinary
corporate liquidity.

The debt load is the main balance-sheet burden. It reflects a company that has
used acquisitions and capital returns as part of its strategy. Interest expense
was $815M in 2025, up from $788M in 2024, while operating income was $4.344B.
The company has meaningful cash generation and divestiture flexibility, but
the quality of the deleveraging path depends on recurring operating cash rather
than asset sales.

The macro/liquidity risks are moderate but visible:

- corporate clients may delay discretionary advisory work in a recession;
- higher rates raise debt service and can pressure M&A-related risk activity;
- insurance pricing and catastrophe conditions affect placement volumes and
  client budgets even if Aon does not underwrite the risk;
- collection cycles can lengthen as clients face stress; and
- currency and international regulation complicate global operations.

## Operating performance and the client relationship

2025 Risk Capital revenue rose 7% to $11.3B, with Commercial Risk Solutions
revenue of $8.5B and Reinsurance Solutions revenue of $2.8B. Human Capital
revenue rose 13% to $5.9B, including Health Solutions revenue of $3.8B and
Wealth Solutions revenue of $2.1B. Organic growth was 6% in Commercial Risk,
6% in Reinsurance, 5% in Health, and 5% in Wealth.

The breadth matters. Aon can deepen a client relationship across commercial
risk, health, retirement, wealth, and talent, making the relationship more
valuable than a single insurance placement. It also creates complexity: each
line has different renewal timing, fee structure, market impact, staffing
requirements, and regulatory exposure.

The next proof is not simply “6% organic growth.” It is whether retention,
new-business wins, pricing, client wallet share, and employee productivity
support growth across multiple lines without rising acquisition or compensation
burden.

## Lyn Alden lens: durability without underwriting exposure

Aon benefits from durable institutional needs: businesses must insure assets,
manage employee health and benefits, navigate retirement and talent systems,
and transfer risk as regulation and complexity increase. The company earns by
helping clients make difficult decisions, which can be more resilient than
selling discretionary goods.

The relationship is not completely defensive. Insurance brokerage depends on
client budgets, commercial activity, risk-market pricing, M&A volume, and the
trustworthiness of advice. In a liquidity squeeze, clients may retain a broker
but reduce consulting scope or pressure commissions.

The balance-sheet position is stronger than an insurer's because Aon generally
does not retain the policy underwriting risk. But the company has meaningful
corporate debt and acquisition integration risk. The macro conclusion is
therefore “durable service demand with financial leverage,” not “recession
proof.”

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers and lifecycle

Aon's narrative has four separate value drivers:

1. recurring renewals and client retention;
2. organic growth from new business, pricing, and market exposure;
3. cross-selling across Risk Capital and Human Capital; and
4. acquisitions and restructuring that improve density, technology, and
   operating leverage.

These drivers have different reinvestment and valuation characteristics.
Organic brokerage growth may require incremental talent and technology;
acquisition growth requires capital and integration; restructuring savings may
improve margins but cannot be assumed forever.

### Reinvestment questions

- Does each acquisition add durable client relationships or merely reported
  revenue and amortizable intangibles?
- Can Aon integrate NFP and other acquisitions without damaging retention or
  increasing compensation faster than revenue?
- Is technology reducing service cost and improving advice, or shifting cost
  into restructuring and capitalized systems?
- Are health, wealth, and risk clients buying multiple services, or are growth
  rates simply reflecting market impact and acquisitions?
- What is the cash return on acquired client relationships after debt cost,
  integration cost, and future retention risk?

### Reverse-valuation questions

The dated scenario register preserves a September 14, 2026 equity-value input
for this screen. It is an expectations screen, not a live quote or price
target. Reverse the market's expectations for:

- long-run organic growth after the current restructuring program;
- durable operating margin excluding temporary savings;
- retention and pricing under a softer insurance market;
- acquisition volume and purchase multiples;
- debt reduction and interest expense;
- pension and restructuring cash needs;
- share compensation and repurchases; and
- terminal growth after the global brokerage market matures.

A high-quality advisory platform can still be a poor investment if acquisition
returns, debt reduction, and margin expectations are already fully priced.

## Management incentives and governance questions

Aon recorded $432M of share-based compensation expense in 2025, including
$297M of restricted share units and $124M of performance share awards. The
performance plan's largest component is based on cumulative adjusted diluted
EPS over a three-year period, with payouts ranging from 0% to 200% of target.

This design aligns management with per-share earnings, but the forensic review
should ask whether adjusted EPS can benefit from:

- add-backs for restructuring and integration;
- acquisition-driven amortization;
- debt-funded repurchases;
- business-sale gains and portfolio changes; or
- cost reductions that impair service quality or retention.

The 2025 statement of shareholders' equity shows $1.0B of repurchases and
approximately 2.7 million shares repurchased. Share-based compensation,
repurchases, and employee share issuance should be evaluated together rather
than treating buybacks as automatic value creation.

## Who controls the relationship and who carries the burden

| Layer | Aon | Insurers, employers, employees, and capital providers |
|---|---|---|
| Control point | Risk advice, analytics, placement, renewals, benefits, and talent workflow | Risk capital, policy claims, workforce cost, and funding |
| Cleaner economics | Commissions, advisory fees, recurring renewals, and cross-sell | Insurance underwriting returns and client operating outcomes |
| Capital burden | Debt, acquisitions, technology, talent, offices, pensions, and restructuring | Insurer capital, catastrophe risk, employer benefit obligations |
| Main risk | Retention, fee pressure, debt, integration, collections, and credibility | Claims, rates, regulation, labor, and asset performance |

Aon controls a valuable relationship and workflow while avoiding much of the
underwriting burden. Apollo is the useful contrast: Apollo also controls
origination and distribution, but its retirement-services spread layer retains
substantial asset-liability risk. Aon is cleaner, but its debt and acquisition
program are the costs of maintaining that position.

## Thesis breakers and next-filing watchlist

### Thesis breakers

- Organic growth falls because retention or new-business wins weaken, rather
  than because of temporary currency.
- Fee rates compress as clients consolidate or insource analytics and advice.
- Compensation and technology costs grow faster than organic revenue after the
  restructuring savings are exhausted.
- Acquisitions fail to retain clients or produce cash returns above debt and
  integration costs.
- Receivables and collection periods deteriorate as clients face stress.
- Debt reduction depends on repeated divestitures rather than operating cash.
- Share-based compensation and buybacks obscure weak per-share cash economics.
- Pension, restructuring, regulatory, cyber, or fiduciary failures create
  recurring costs that adjusted measures exclude.
- Fiduciary controls fail, causing regulatory, reputational, or liquidity
  consequences even though fiduciary funds are not ordinary corporate assets.

### Next-filing fields

1. Organic revenue growth by segment, retention, new business, pricing, and
   market impact.
2. Revenue and margin by Risk Capital and Human Capital, including NFP effects.
3. Compensation per employee, headcount, technology expense, and service
   quality indicators.
4. Receivables, collection days, fiduciary assets/liabilities, and client cash
   timing.
5. Restructuring cash costs, cumulative savings, remaining commitments, and
   whether “one-time” costs recur.
6. Acquisition spend, purchase accounting, amortization, integration costs,
   client retention, and realized returns.
7. Operating cash flow less capex, acquisitions, pensions, and restructuring.
8. Debt balance, interest expense, maturities, leverage, and divestiture use.
9. Share compensation, diluted shares, repurchases, and performance-plan
   outcomes.
10. Pension contributions and funded status.
11. Cybersecurity, fiduciary, regulatory, and professional-liability events.
12. Cross-selling evidence across risk, health, wealth, and talent services.

## Final judgment

Aon is a strong example of a company that monetizes institutional complexity
through a trusted advisory and distribution relationship rather than through
underwriting or owning heavy physical assets. Its 2025 organic growth and cash
generation support the quality case, while the acquisition, debt, pension,
restructuring, and compensation disclosures define the risks that a simple
asset-light label would hide.

> Aon appears to own a cleaner financial workflow than an insurer, but the
> shareholder result still depends on retention, acquisition discipline,
> recurring cash after restructuring and debt, and incentives that reward cash
> returns rather than adjusted EPS alone.

## Evidence grade and unresolved questions

- **Filing fact:** FY2025 and first-half 2026 organic growth, cash flow, capex,
  acquisitions, pensions, restructuring, fiduciary balances, debt, and SBC are
  anchored to the annual and quarterly filings.
- **Disclosed/packet-backed:** client-retention, cross-sell, and integration
  claims are management-reported until acquired-client economics and cash returns
  are demonstrated.
- **Inference:** Aon controls an institutional advisory and placement workflow,
  while clients and carriers retain the underlying risk; its burden is talent,
  trust, pension, acquisition, and debt execution rather than underwriting loss.
- **Unresolved:** the next filing must show organic growth after restructuring,
  acquisition cash returns, pension funding, and cash after SBC and debt. If
  adjusted EPS improves without client retention and owner cash, the quality
  thesis weakens.
