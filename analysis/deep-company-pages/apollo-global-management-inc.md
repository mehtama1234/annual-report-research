## Bottom line

Apollo is not a simple alternative-asset manager. It is a combined capital-
origination, asset-management, retirement-services, and insurance spread
platform. The company presents three economically different earnings layers:

1. fee-related earnings from managing outside capital;
2. spread-related earnings, substantially connected to Athene and insurance
   balance-sheet economics; and
3. principal-investing income and performance fees, which are more exposed to
   realization timing, asset marks, and incentive allocation.

The 2025 and first-half 2026 supplement show a powerful platform: 2025 fee-
related earnings were approximately $2.5B, spread-related earnings $3.4B, and
adjusted net income about $5.2B; Q2 2026 fee-related earnings were $785M,
spread-related earnings $877M, and adjusted net income $1.314B. Total AUM grew
from about $938B at year-end 2025 to $1.05T in Q2 2026, while fee-generating
AUM rose from about $709B to $858B.

The clean version of the thesis is that Apollo controls a valuable origination
and funding workflow: it manufactures credit and retirement products, places
capital, and earns fees and spreads without owning every underlying asset
directly. The skeptical version is that “recurring” spread earnings still
carry insurance liabilities, credit losses, duration and liquidity risk, while
adjusted net income excludes a long list of marks, compensation, taxes,
financing costs, and unusual items.

Current judgment: Apollo has a differentiated and increasingly durable
financial control point, but it must be analyzed closer to an insurer and
credit intermediary than to a pure fee manager. The key investment risk is
not whether AUM is growing; it is whether fee and spread earnings survive a
credit cycle after losses, funding costs, asset marks, capital requirements,
and policyholder obligations.

## What the company actually is

Apollo's business is a capital-routing system:

`origination and underwriting -> asset formation -> institutional / retirement funding -> insurance balance sheet -> management fees + net investment spread + performance income`

Apollo's asset-management business originates, manages, and distributes
private credit, equity, infrastructure, and other investments. Retirement
Services, primarily Athene, receives premiums and retirement assets and invests
them to earn a spread over policyholder and funding obligations. The combined
platform can originate assets for both third-party funds and insurance
accounts, creating distribution and sourcing advantages.

That integration also makes the company harder to analyze. AUM, fee-generating
AUM, insurance invested assets, gross alternative investments, net invested
assets, fee-related earnings, spread-related earnings, and GAAP revenue are
different denominators. Treating them as interchangeable is the first major
analytical error.

## Earnings architecture from the Q2 2026 supplement

The supplement reports the following quarterly progression:

| Metric | 4Q25 | 1Q26 | 2Q26 | YTD 2026 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Fee-related earnings | 690 | 728 | 785 | 1,513 |
| Spread-related earnings | 865 | 719 | 877 | 1,596 |
| Fee + spread earnings | 1,555 | 1,447 | 1,662 | 3,109 |
| Principal-investing income | 227 | 75 | 16 | 91 |
| Segment income | 1,782 | 1,522 | 1,678 | 3,200 |
| Adjusted net income | 1,540 | 1,208 | 1,314 | 2,522 |

The composition is more informative than the total:

- Fee-related earnings grew sequentially from $690M in 4Q25 to $785M in 2Q26.
- Spread-related earnings fell in 1Q26 and rebounded in 2Q26; this volatility
  is a reminder that spread income is not equivalent to a contractual asset-
  management fee.
- Principal-investing income fell from $227M in 4Q25 to $16M in 2Q26. Apollo's
  adjusted income remained strong because the fee-and-spread layers were much
  larger than principal returns.
- HoldCo interest and other financing costs rose from $40M in 4Q25 to $53M in
  2Q26, and taxes and related payables rose from $202M to $311M.

The business is broadening beyond realization-dependent private equity, but
the spread layer itself needs insurance and credit analysis rather than being
treated as a free substitute for fees.

## Earnings quality and financial-engineering tests

### 1. Adjusted net income is a useful operating view, not GAAP cash

Apollo's reconciliation from GAAP to adjusted net income removes or adjusts
for equity-based profit sharing, equity-based compensation, noncontrolling
interests, unrealized performance fees, unrealized profit sharing, HoldCo
financing costs, unrealized principal-investment results, unrealized investment
gains and losses, transaction-related costs, insurance investment gains/losses,
non-operating insurance-liability changes, integration items, and equity-based
compensation.

Several adjustments are reasonable for period-to-period comparability. They are
not all economically irrelevant:

- Equity-based profit sharing is a real cost of generating performance income.
- Equity-based compensation is a real cost to shareholders through dilution.
- Unrealized investment gains can later become realized returns, but losses
  can also become real impairments.
- Insurance liability and derivative changes are not noise if the company is
  retaining policyholder risk.
- Transaction and integration expenses can recur in a consolidating platform.

The correct approach is to maintain both a management-defined adjusted series
and a skeptical normalized series that restores recurring costs and through-
cycle losses.

### 2. The 11% alternative-investment-return assumption is an explicit model risk

The supplement reports an “alternative net investment income delta to long-term
expectation,” based on management's long-term expectation of an 11% average
annual return. In 2Q26 the delta was $76M; in 1Q26 it was $188M. Management
explicitly says there is no assurance that the long-term average will be
achieved.

This is a valuable disclosure because it reveals how a private-asset mark and
return assumption enters the earnings framework. The forensic test is to
compare actual realized cash returns, impairments, valuation changes, and
investment income against the assumed 11% path. A model that capitalizes the
full long-term expectation as recurring earnings may be overstating normalized
profit during an unusually favorable asset-mark environment.

### 3. Unrealized gains, marks, and insurance liabilities can dominate GAAP

Apollo's GAAP income statement includes large swings in investment-related
gains and losses, market-risk-benefit remeasurement, future-policy benefits,
and investment income. For example, the Q1 2026 supplement shows a $1.7B
one-time tax expense arising from the revocation of ACRA's Bermuda tax
election and a full valuation allowance against Bermuda deferred tax assets.
Q1 GAAP net income attributable to common stockholders was negative $1.930B,
while adjusted net income was $1.208B.

This does not mean adjusted income is useless. It means Apollo's economics
cannot be summarized by a single quarter's GAAP EPS or adjusted EPS. The
analyst must track the direction and quality of each bridge item over several
years.

### 4. Noncontrolling interests and Athene ownership matter

The supplement's reconciliation includes substantial net income attributable to
noncontrolling interests. Its invested-asset schedules separately show ACRA
noncontrolling interests reducing gross to net invested assets. The parent
therefore does not own every dollar of the consolidated insurance economics.

The test is to reconcile:

`consolidated insurance results -> noncontrolling interests -> Apollo attributable earnings -> cash available at HoldCo`

AUM and consolidated segment income can grow faster than cash economically
available to Apollo shareholders if outside owners retain a larger share of the
underlying returns.

## Q2 2026 earnings-call evidence: origination is not yet cash

The preserved Q2 2026 earnings-call transcript adds management's explanation of
the growth numbers. Management reported $74B of second-quarter origination and
said approximately $50B of signed or announced transactions would benefit later
periods because Apollo records results when transactions close. It also reported
$60B of organic inflows during the quarter, including $38B in asset management
and $22B in Athene.

The call described a $35B financing associated with Broadcom's AI expansion and
gave spread information for the underwriting activity: investment-grade
origination was described at approximately 280 basis points over Treasuries,
while sub-investment-grade origination was approximately 440 basis points over
Treasuries. A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point. These are
management disclosures about new activity, not realized Apollo common cash.

The transcript therefore improves the research in two ways. First, it explains
why fee and spread earnings may rise in future quarters without being recognized
immediately. Second, it identifies a concrete concentration and credit test. The
Broadcom financing and other large transactions must be followed from signed
terms to closing, funding source, collateral, credit rating, amortization,
counterparty exposure, loss experience, and fee or spread income attributable to
Apollo after co-investors, Athene, insurance liabilities, and financing costs.

Management also emphasized daily valuation, ICE identifiers, settlement, and
greater disclosure for private credit. Those changes may improve transparency,
but they do not remove valuation uncertainty or liquidity risk. The next filings
should test whether the new reporting produces more realized cash and better
loss information, rather than treating increased data availability as proof of
investment performance.

## Fee-related earnings: the clean layer, with its own burden

Q2 2026 fee-related revenues were $1.343B, composed of $1.001B of management
fees, $277M of capital-solutions fees and other net revenue, and $65M of fee-
related performance fees. Fee-related compensation was $343M and non-
compensation expenses $215M, resulting in $785M of fee-related earnings.

This is the closest layer to a traditional asset-management business. It is
supported by fee-generating AUM, capital raising, management contracts, and
origination capabilities. But even this layer needs quality testing:

- Are fees based on committed capital, invested capital, NAV, or performance?
- Are fee rates falling as AUM moves into larger institutional mandates?
- Are capital-solutions fees transactional or durable?
- Is fee-related compensation growing slower than fee revenue because of
  operating leverage, or is compensation being shifted into equity-based
  profit sharing and other adjustments?
- Do fundraising and fee-generating AUM growth translate into cash collections
  after fund expenses and noncontrolling interests?

The Q2 2026 fee-related earnings margin on fee-related revenue was approximately
58.5%. That is attractive, but the numerator and denominator are management-
defined segment measures and should be reconciled to GAAP compensation,
general and administrative expense, and cash paid.

## Spread-related earnings: insurance economics hidden inside the platform

Q2 2026 spread-related earnings were $877M. The supplement shows fixed-income
and other net investment income of $3.686B, alternative net investment income
of $348M, strategic capital-management fees of $37M, and cost of funds of
$2.942B before other operating expenses and interest/financing costs.

The spread is therefore a function of:

`asset yield + alternative returns + fees - policyholder/funding cost - operating cost - credit losses - capital cost`

This is closer to insurance and structured-credit economics than to a pure
management fee. The important questions are:

- Are asset yields earned on assets with acceptable duration and credit risk?
- What is the actual cost of funds across annuities, funding agreements, and
  reinsurance?
- How much of alternative investment income is realized cash versus marks?
- Are allowances for credit losses sufficient under weaker credit conditions?
- How much liquidity is required to meet policyholder withdrawals, collateral,
  derivatives, and reinsurance obligations?
- Does the spread remain attractive after regulatory capital and hedging costs?

The 2Q26 supplement reports gross invested assets of about $413.6B and net
invested assets of about $314.1B after adjustments including reinsurance,
unrealized gains/losses, ACRA noncontrolling interests, and other items. Those
adjustments are not optional footnotes; they define what part of the asset
pool belongs economically to Apollo, Athene, and other stakeholders.

## Cash conversion and capital requirements

Apollo is difficult to evaluate with a conventional industrial free-cash-flow
formula because much of the economic cash movement occurs inside funds,
insurance entities, consolidated variable-interest entities, and investment
portfolios. The correct cash work has several layers:

1. cash paid by fee-paying funds and insurance businesses;
2. cash retained for policyholder obligations, reserves, collateral, and
   regulatory capital;
3. cash distributed to Apollo HoldCo;
4. compensation, financing, taxes, and preferred dividends at HoldCo; and
5. cash actually available for common-shareholder dividends and reinvestment.

The supplement shows cash and cash equivalents, including restricted cash, of
$23.540B at 2Q26 and net invested assets of approximately $314.090B. These
figures demonstrate liquidity resources but do not mean all cash is freely
distributable. Restricted cash, insurance assets, collateral, and VIE balances
must be kept separate from parent liquidity.

The main capital requirements are:

- regulatory and economic capital for Athene;
- policyholder and funding obligations;
- collateral and derivatives;
- acquisitions and integration;
- compensation and profit sharing;
- fund commitments and principal investments;
- technology and distribution infrastructure; and
- HoldCo interest, taxes, preferred dividends, and common dividends.

The high-level forensic bridge should be updated every quarter rather than
using adjusted net income as a proxy for distributable cash.

## Balance-sheet resilience and macro/liquidity risks

Apollo's platform is more resilient than a realization-dependent private-equity
manager because fee-related and spread-related earnings continue even when
exits slow. The same integration creates sensitivity to credit, rates, and
liquidity:

- Higher rates can improve reinvestment yields but raise funding costs and
  pressure private-asset valuations.
- A recession can create credit losses and reduce realizations, fundraising,
  and asset-management fees at the same time.
- Insurance liabilities may require cash or asset sales during market stress.
- Private-credit marks can lag public-market deterioration.
- Reinsurance and collateral arrangements can move risk outside the simple
  consolidated income statement.
- Private-equity and real-asset exits can remain delayed, reducing performance
  fees and principal-investing income.
- Concentration in retirement and insurance channels creates regulatory and
  counterparty exposure even if AUM is diversified by fund strategy.

The Alden-style conclusion is that Apollo has a powerful funding and sourcing
position, but its durability depends on liquidity discipline and credit-cycle
survival. The business is not immune to monetary tightening; it is an
intermediary whose assets, liabilities, and funding channels respond to it.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers and lifecycle

Apollo's narrative contains at least four separate growth engines:

1. fee-generating AUM and fee-rate durability;
2. private-credit origination and deployment;
3. retirement-services premiums and spread capture; and
4. performance fees and principal-investing returns.

They have different lifecycles and valuation multiples. Fee-related earnings
deserve a more recurring framework. Spread-related earnings require an
insurance/credit return framework. Performance fees require a realization and
vintage-cycle framework.

### Reinvestment questions

- Does each additional dollar of fee-generating AUM require proportional
  personnel and distribution cost?
- Can Apollo deploy $300B-plus of annual origination without relaxing credit
  standards or accepting weaker spreads?
- Is Athene's asset origination producing returns after capital, hedging, and
  policyholder costs?
- Are retirement inflows sticky, or are they sensitive to rates and product
  competition?
- Does private credit grow because Apollo has better sourcing, or because the
  market is accepting more leverage and less compensation for risk?
- What portion of reinvestment is true growth versus keeping regulated and
  operational infrastructure safe?

### Reverse-valuation questions

The dated scenario register preserves a September 14, 2026 equity-value input
for this screen. It is an expectations screen, not a live quote or price
target. Reverse the market's expectations separately for:

- sustainable fee-related earnings and fee rates;
- normalized spread earnings after credit losses and funding costs;
- performance fees across a full realization cycle;
- the share of earnings attributable to common shareholders after NCI and
  preferred dividends;
- required regulatory capital and HoldCo liquidity;
- dilution and equity-based compensation;
- valuation of insurance assets and liabilities; and
- terminal growth after private-credit and retirement channels mature.

The most common valuation error would be to capitalize the entire $5B-plus
adjusted earnings stack at a pure asset-manager multiple. The second would be
to value the insurance spread as if it carried no balance-sheet risk. Apollo's
proper valuation likely requires a sum-of-the-parts framework with explicit
haircuts for marks, capital, liquidity, and attribution.

or adjusted net income alone.

### What the market price requires

The synchronized September 11, 2026 market snapshot used by the owner-cash
register placed Apollo's equity value at approximately **$78.7B**. That value
falls above the provisional base SOTP outcome of $73.8B and below the bull
outcome of $115.4B. The comparison is not a target price. It identifies the
operating claims that the current price already requires:

| Comparison | Result | Interpretation |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Bear SOTP versus market equity value | $44.8B versus $78.7B | The market does not support a case where normalized FRE and SRE fall materially and claims remain high |
| Base SOTP versus market equity value | $73.8B versus $78.7B | The market is close to a case requiring durable fee growth, resilient spread earnings, and no large unrecognized claim |
| Bull SOTP versus market equity value | $115.4B versus $78.7B | Additional value requires sustained growth and a smaller-than-assumed haircut for insurance, partner, NCI, and liquidity claims |

The practical reading is that Apollo is not priced as if its insurance and
credit platform will fail, but the price also does not by itself prove that
spread-related earnings are equivalent to fee income. The next filings should
therefore be judged against the base-case requirements: stable fee rates and
fee-generating AUM, controlled credit losses, adequate insurance capital,
funding-cost discipline, and common-shareholder attribution after NCI,
compensation, taxes, and HoldCo financing.

## Management incentives and governance questions

Apollo's supplement explicitly shows equity-based profit sharing expense tied to
realized performance in principal investing, equity-based compensation, and
unrealized profit-sharing adjustments. These create several alignment tests:

1. Are managers rewarded for realized cash returns or for valuation marks and
   AUM growth?
2. Does compensation rise with fee-related revenue even when net flows or fee
   rates deteriorate?
3. Are private-credit origination goals balanced by through-cycle loss metrics?
4. Does the insurance platform reward spread growth before capital and
   liability risk are fully charged?
5. Are acquisitions and strategic investments judged on cash return after
   integration, not on reported segment scale?
6. Do stock awards and preferred dividends reduce the common shareholder's
   share of the stated adjusted earnings?

The presence of a clawback or detailed reconciliation is not proof of good
incentives. The next proxy and supplement should be read alongside actual
vesting outcomes and realized returns.

## Who controls the relationship and who carries the burden

| Layer | Apollo | Investors, policyholders, and capital providers |
|---|---|---|
| Control point | Origination, underwriting, capital formation, product design, distribution, and retirement relationships | Capital supply and risk-bearing claims |
| Cleaner economics | Management fees, advisory/origination fees, and platform-level spread capture | Asset returns net of fees and policy obligations |
| Balance-sheet burden | Athene insurance assets/liabilities, hedging, capital, credit losses, and liquidity | Policyholder claims, fund commitments, and counterparty exposure |
| Main timing risk | Marks, realizations, fee rates, and adjusted-income exclusions | Liquidity needs, withdrawals, defaults, and asset-sale conditions |
| Key dependency | Continued trust in Apollo's underwriting and retirement products | Ability and willingness to keep allocating capital |

Apollo owns the relationship and origination/control layer more than a passive
asset owner does, but it also retains materially more balance-sheet exposure
than a pure broker or fee-only advisor. This is the important contradiction to
the archive's clean-intermediary pattern.

## Thesis breakers and next-filing watchlist

### Thesis breakers

- Fee-generating AUM grows while fee rates, net flows, or fee-related margins
  fall materially.
- Spread-related earnings weaken because cost of funds, credit losses, or
  capital requirements rise faster than asset yields.
- Private-credit growth is accompanied by rising nonaccruals, impairments,
  restructurings, delayed marks, or weaker realized returns.
- Alternative net investment income repeatedly depends on the 11% long-term
  expectation rather than realized cash performance.
- Insurance liquidity, reinsurance, collateral, or capital requirements force
  asset sales or constrain distributions to HoldCo.
- Noncontrolling interests or preferred claims absorb a growing share of
  consolidated economics.
- Principal-investing and performance fees fall through a full exit cycle,
  exposing the fragility of the earnings mix.
- Equity compensation, profit sharing, and transaction costs remain recurring
  despite being treated as adjustments.
- A tax, legal, regulatory, or accounting change produces large unanticipated
  charges or makes the platform's funding structure less efficient.

### Next filing fields

1. Total AUM, fee-generating AUM, gross inflows, net flows, fee rates, and
   management-fee mix.
2. Fee-related revenue, compensation, non-compensation expense, and cash
   conversion.
3. Spread-related earnings: asset yield, cost of funds, credit losses, capital
   charges, hedging, and liability growth.
4. Net investment income split between realized, unrealized, and alternative
   assets.
5. Gross and net invested assets, reinsurance, ACRA NCI, derivatives,
   collateral, and restricted cash.
6. Private-credit nonaccruals, restructurings, impairments, payment-in-kind
   interest, and valuation marks.
7. Performance-fee realizations, unrealized performance fees, and carried
   interest attribution.
8. HoldCo cash, financing costs, preferred dividends, taxes, and distributions.
9. Regulatory-capital ratios and liquidity stress disclosures for Athene.
10. Equity-based compensation, profit sharing, dilution, and vesting metrics.
11. Acquisitions, integration costs, goodwill, and intangible amortization.
12. Actual alternative-investment return versus the 11% long-term expectation.

## Final judgment

Apollo is a particularly valuable company for this cross-sector archive because
it proves that “asset-light financial intermediation” is not one category. The
company controls a valuable capital-origination and retirement-distribution
workflow, and its fee-related earnings provide a cleaner layer than direct
asset ownership. But Athene and spread-related earnings retain insurance,
credit, funding, liquidity, and regulatory burdens.

The current evidence supports a differentiated platform, not a simple
recurring-earnings conclusion. The right future proof is whether fee and spread
earnings remain strong after a full credit and realization cycle, with capital
requirements, noncontrolling interests, compensation, marks, and liquidity
fully charged.

> Apollo may control one of the strongest financial relationship and origination
> layers in the archive, but the investment case depends on underwriting and
> liquidity discipline—not merely on AUM growth or adjusted net income.

## Evidence grade and unresolved questions

- **Filing/supplement fact:** FRE, SRE, principal income, ANI, AUM, insurance
  assets and liabilities, marks, NCI, compensation, and capital requirements are
  anchored to the 2025 10-K and Q2 2026 supplement.
- **Disclosed fact:** origination, retirement inflow, and platform-growth claims
  are management-reported and must be tested through a credit and realization
  cycle.
- **Inference:** Apollo controls capital origination and distribution, but Athene
  and spread earnings carry insurer, duration, funding, credit, and regulatory
  burdens that do not belong in a pure fee multiple.
- **Unresolved:** the next filing must reconcile spread earnings after credit
  losses, funding costs, policyholder claims, capital, NCI, and compensation. AUM
  growth with weaker fee rates, marks, or common-shareholder attribution would
  weaken the integrated-platform thesis.
