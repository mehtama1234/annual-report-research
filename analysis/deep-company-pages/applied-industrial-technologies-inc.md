# Applied Industrial Technologies, Inc. — technical distribution, uptime, and acquisition-funded cash

As of 2026-09-13. Fiscal 2025 ended June 30, 2025. Dollar amounts are in millions unless stated otherwise.

## How to read this memo

In this memo, a control point means the specific customer relationship, asset,
or process that gives a company the ability to earn money. Operating cash flow means cash generated by normal business operations. Capital
expenditure means cash spent on equipment and other productive assets. An
acquisition is cash paid to buy another business; it is a growth investment,
even when management excludes it from a free-cash-flow calculation. The
post-growth residual below subtracts both capital expenditure and acquisitions
so the cost of expansion remains visible.

## Investment question

Applied is a value-added industrial distributor rather than a plain catalog
seller. It controls customer access to motion, fluid power, flow control,
automation, calibration, and MRO systems where downtime is costly and internal
technical labor is scarce. The question is whether that technical control point
creates durable owner cash after inventory, receivables, acquisitions, service
labor, debt, and the cyclicality of industrial capital spending.

## What the filing and IR record reveal

- Applied’s control point is technical uptime. Customers are not only buying
  bearings or hoses; they are outsourcing selection, availability, repair,
  calibration, engineering, and system integration.
- Engineered Solutions approached 40% of sales versus 18% ten years earlier,
  showing a deliberate shift toward technical applications, automation, and
  service depth.
- Fiscal 2025 demand was muted in heavy industry and off-highway markets, but
  the company cited datacenters, semiconductor manufacturing, process
  infrastructure, robotics, and calibration as areas of opportunity. These are
  second-order beneficiaries of AI and reshoring rather than direct software
  beneficiaries.
- Service Center and Engineered Solutions are distinct demand streams. Break-
  fix MRO can be resilient during a capital-spending pause, while engineered
  projects are more sensitive to customer investment timing and order conversion.
- Acquisitions build local density and technical breadth, but the $293.4M 2025
  cash outflow means the return must be proven at the cohort level.

## Forensic earnings-quality questions

### 1. Is management FCF measuring the owner’s residual?

The company reports fiscal 2025 FCF of approximately $465M, while GAAP OCF less
capex less acquisitions is only $171.8M. Neither number is automatically the
right valuation denominator: the first may exclude growth acquisitions, while
the second may penalize productive bolt-ons before they mature. Track both and
show the acquisition cash, integration cost, debt, and incremental cash return.

### 2. Does technical mix create incremental ROIC?

The Engineered Solutions mix shift is strategically attractive, but it can also
increase project timing, engineering labor, inventory complexity, and customer
concentration. Test gross margin, operating margin, working capital, and return
on invested capital by segment rather than assuming “higher value” means higher
cash return.

### 3. Is working-capital quality holding?

Receivables of $769.7M and inventory of $505.3M are the physical cost of service
availability. Measure days sales outstanding, inventory turns, reserves,
obsolescence, supplier rebates, LIFO effects, and the cash impact of a sudden
industrial recovery or slowdown.

### 4. Are acquisitions buying density or buying reported growth?

Hydradyne and other bolt-ons may deepen fluid-power and service capability, but
the test is organic growth after anniversary, retention, cross-sell, gross-margin
contribution, integration expense, goodwill, and post-acquisition cash return.
Repeated “one-time” integration costs should be treated as part of the
platform’s normal growth cost.

### 5. Are shareholder returns covered after growth investment?

Fiscal 2025 repurchases and dividends exceeded the conservative residual by
approximately $44.7M. That is not alarming for a healthy balance sheet, but it
means buybacks should not be used as evidence of excess cash while acquisition
capital remains central to the strategy.

## Incentives, valuation, and the cohort conclusion

Management's public framing emphasizes record free cash generation, more than
$560M deployed across capital expenditure, acquisitions, buybacks, dividends,
and debt reduction, and continued bolt-on expansion. That framing can be
economically reasonable while still creating an incentive to emphasize the
larger management FCF number rather than the smaller residual after
acquisitions. The control test is simple: report both measures, charge every
acquisition and integration cost to the growth plan, and require the acquired
businesses to produce incremental cash after the working-capital investment.

The shareholder-return test is also per-share, not just total dollars. Buybacks
should be judged against share-based compensation, debt-funded purchases,
acquisition goodwill, and the number of shares outstanding after each period.
If total FCF rises but diluted cash per share does not, the economic benefit has
not reached the common owner. The current filing set provides the SBC and
capital-return figures, but it does not yet provide a sufficiently detailed
acquisition-by-acquisition return schedule. That remains a required disclosure
checkpoint rather than an assumption.

Applied belongs in the [industrial distribution infrastructure comparison](../cross-sector/industrial-distribution-infrastructure-forensic-comparison-2026-09-13.md)
and in the first-principles essay on [industrial procurement, inventory, and
service density](../first-principles/industrial-procurement-workflow-inventory-and-service-density.md).
Across the cohort, the recurring question is who controls product availability
and technical knowledge, while the differentiating burden is who funds
inventory, branch labor, receivables, project execution, and acquisitions.
Applied's technical mix can earn better economics than ordinary distribution,
but only if its higher service content produces enough gross profit and cash to
cover that added labor and capital.

## Lyn Alden / Damodaran / forensic synthesis

- **Lyn Alden lens:** technical distribution benefits from essential uptime and
  labor scarcity, but industrial production, rates, inventory cycles, and
  customer capex determine resilience.
- **Damodaran lens:** separate mature service-center cash, engineered-solutions
  growth, and acquisition cohorts. The lifecycle question is whether each added
  technical capability earns more than its inventory, labor, and acquisition
  capital.
- **Forensic lens:** the key category errors are treating record sales as organic
  demand, management FCF as post-growth owner cash, mix shift as proven ROIC,
  and local bolt-ons as value creation before their cash return is visible.

## Valuation framing

The 2026-09-11 market snapshot was approximately $12.20B of equity value at
$323.78 per share. That is about 71x the $171.8M conservative FY2025
post-acquisition screen. A normalized range of $180M / $350M / $550M at 25x /
30x / 35x implies $4.5B / $10.5B / $19.3B. The market therefore requires
organic reacceleration, durable technical mix economics, and acquisition returns
that lift owner cash well above the FY2025 post-growth residual.

## Thesis breakers and next checks

1. Industrial weakness causes inventory aging, receivable stress, and margin
   discounting before MRO demand recovers.
2. Engineered Solutions growth requires disproportionate engineering labor,
   project working capital, or warranty/service support.
3. Hydradyne and other acquisitions fail to produce incremental cash after
   integration, goodwill, and debt.
4. Datacenter, semiconductor, and robotics demand is too small or too project-
   lumpy to offset legacy heavy-industry cyclicality.
5. Buybacks and dividends continue to exceed post-growth residual cash.
