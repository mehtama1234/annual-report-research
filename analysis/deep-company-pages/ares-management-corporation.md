## Bottom line

Ares is a fee-paying-AUM and origination platform centered on credit, real
assets, secondaries, infrastructure, and private equity. Compared with
Blackstone, its current read is more management-fee and fundraising led;
compared with Apollo, it is less visibly dependent on an insurance-funded
spread engine. The attractive control point is durable institutional capital
plus origination access. The central forensic risk is that AUM, dry powder,
and fee-related earnings can grow while deployment returns, compensation,
acquisition cash, and credit losses remain unproven.

FY2025 revenue was $5.601B and GAAP net income was $527M. Company-only
operating cash flow was $2.113B, furniture/equipment/leasehold spending was
$72M, and acquisitions were $1.731B, leaving only approximately **$309M after
those listed uses**. The filing also reports $1.775B of FRE and $1.848B of
Realized Income. Those non-GAAP measures are economically useful, but they are
not interchangeable with common-owner cash.

## Q2 2026 checkpoint: strong fee growth, unresolved deployment burden

The Q2 update confirms a powerful fundraising and fee-paying-AUM engine. AUM
reached `$671.3B`, FPAUM reached `$409.9B`, available capital reached `$170.0B`,
and management reported `$36.4B` of capital raised and `$35.9B` of capital
deployed during the quarter. It also reported `$92.6B` of AUM not yet paying
fees and `$4.1B` of development assets not yet stabilized. These are useful
forward indicators, but they are not equivalent to current collected owner
cash.

Q2 GAAP net income attributable to Ares was `$150.6M`, while FRE was `$491.1M`,
Realized Income was `$521.5M`, and after-tax realized income was `$467.6M`.
The spread is the central accounting question, not a defect by itself. FRE is
designed to show operating earnings from recurring revenue; Realized Income
adds realized performance and investment economics; GAAP includes
consolidation, marks, compensation, amortization, and other accounting items.
The valuation must preserve those distinctions.

Management fees were `$1.018B` in Q2 and unconsolidated management fees and
other fees were `$1.122B`. Fee-related performance revenue was `$40.5M`. That
means the fee engine is not only contractual management fees: a smaller but
material portion of FRE comes from performance-linked revenue that must be
tested for repeatability and later cash settlement.

The current quarter also makes the capital-allocation issue sharper. Ares's
record dry powder and forward investment pipeline can support future fees, but
the firm must still show that new credit and real-asset deployment earns an
adequate return after borrower losses, leverage, servicing, employee and
partner compensation, acquisition costs, and the cost of holding capital
before it is deployed. The Q2 release reports another record fundraising
quarter; that confirms distribution strength, not the return on the next
investment cohort.

The Q2 10-Q also records `$182.8M` of equity-based compensation for the quarter
and `$386.4M` for the first six months. It reports `$13.6M` of non-cash expense
in the quarter and `$27.9M` year to date from increases in the fair value of
GCP acquisition contingent consideration. Those items directly support a
per-share and acquisition-return test: headline FRE can grow while common
shareholders bear employee equity and acquisition-related claims.

## The control point

`institutional capital -> specialized origination and underwriting network ->
private-credit / real-asset / infrastructure / secondaries product -> fees,
performance income, and repeat fundraising`

Ares owns relationships with institutional investors and borrowers, product
design, sourcing, underwriting, servicing, and distribution. In private credit
the manager can become deeply embedded in financing workflows, but the fund
investors and insurance partners bear much of the direct asset risk. Ares still
carries reputational, fiduciary, valuation, liquidity, concentration, and
conflict risk when a portfolio performs poorly.

## Narrative-to-numbers bridge

The packet reports approximately $622.5B AUM, $384.9B FPAUM, $156B available
capital, $113.2B gross fundraising, $145.8B deployment, $1.775B FRE, and $1.848B
Realized Income in 2025. Each metric answers a different question:

- **AUM:** scale and possible future fees; can include uncalled capital, debt,
  and assets not yet paying fees.
- **FPAUM:** the actual fee base; test fee rate, permanence, mix, and whether
  fee growth comes from new capital, acquisitions, or repricing.
- **Available capital:** dry powder and future deployment capacity; it is not
  current earnings and may earn no fees until deployed.
- **Fundraising:** distribution and relationship strength; it can precede weak
  investment returns if later vintages disappoint.
- **Deployment:** origination capacity; it raises the question of underwriting
  discipline and whether the firm is forced to put capital to work.
- **Realized Income:** a cash-oriented metric, but performance-related and
  timing-sensitive; it should not receive the same multiple as recurring FRE.

The 2025 segment table shows the concentration clearly: Credit generated about
$1.825B FRE, Real Assets $465M, Secondaries $208M, Private Equity $58M, Other
$27M, while Operations Management was negative $808M. Credit is the economic
engine, so the portfolio is not as diversified as the product list implies.

## Earnings quality and financial-engineering risks

### FRE contains a management-defined recurring-performance component

Ares defines FRE as recurring revenue, primarily management fees and fee-related
performance revenues, less operating expenses. Fee-related performance revenue
from perpetual vehicles is included because it is measured and eligible to be
received on a recurring basis. That is a reasonable operating measure, but it
means FRE is not purely contractual management fee income. Holdback provisions,
performance conditions, and later performance can affect the cash realization.

### Realized Income is not free cash flow

Realized Income adds realized performance and investment income, subtracts
realized performance compensation and investment costs, and includes other
adjustments. It can be closer to distributable economics than GAAP income, but
the filing explicitly says FRE and RI are not liquidity measures. Company-only
OCF of $2.113B versus $1.848B RI and $1.775B FRE shows why the three views need
reconciliation rather than substitution.

### Compensation is a primary owner claim

Allocated share-based compensation was $741M in 2025. Acquisition-related
compensation was another $105M in the FRE/RI reconciliation. The platform also
uses partner and carried-interest economics outside SBC. AUM and fundraising
growth can therefore increase reported scale while a large share of the value
flows to employees, partners, sellers, or acquired teams rather than common
shareholders.

### Consolidated funds create a gross-up trap

The filing reports $3.267B consolidated operating cash flow, but only $2.113B
was company operating cash; the remaining fund cash was held in consolidated
fund structures. The filing says consolidated funds generally have liabilities
that are non-recourse and no net effect on stockholders' equity, but their gross
assets, liabilities, and cash flows can make the consolidated statements look
larger. The common-owner bridge must use the company-only column.

### Q2 forensic tests

- **Fee-base test:** track FPAUM, management-fee rates, fee-paying versus
  non-fee-paying AUM, and per-share FRE. AUM and dry powder are not current
  revenue.
- **Performance-revenue test:** separate the `$40.5M` of Q2 fee-related
  performance revenue from contractual management fees, then test whether it
  recurs and is collected after performance conditions and holdbacks.
- **Deployment test:** compare each new capital cohort's fee rate, deployment
  pace, borrower quality, realized losses, nonaccruals, amendments, and
  eventual distributions. Fundraising confirms distribution, not investment
  returns.
- **Acquisition test:** compare acquired FPAUM and incremental fee profit with
  cash paid, equity issued, contingent consideration, integration cost,
  amortization, and retention requirements.
- **Common-owner test:** reconcile FRE and Realized Income to company-only cash
  after equity compensation, partner economics, preferred dividends, debt,
  acquisitions, and recurring capital needs. Consolidated fund cash must not
  be treated as unrestricted corporate cash.

### Acquisition accounting and credit risk

Goodwill acquired during the year was $2.285B, and goodwill ended at $3.454B.
Intangible assets gross excluding goodwill were $2.116B. The acquisition may
expand the fee base, but it also creates amortization, integration, retention,
and impairment risk. Credit's $1.825B FRE contribution means nonaccruals,
realized losses, covenant stress, valuation marks, and weaker refinancing can
damage both returns and fundraising even when management fees remain reported.

## Alden macro/liquidity lens

Ares benefits from institutional demand for private credit, alternative income,
infrastructure, and real assets when banks retreat or borrowers need bespoke
capital. Higher rates can support floating-rate credit income, but they can
also increase borrower stress, defaults, refinancing gaps, and private-asset
valuation pressure. Liquidity is transferred to funds and investors, not
eliminated.

The durable platform is the origination and servicing network. The fragile
part is the assumption that dry powder can be deployed at attractive returns.
If fundraising remains strong while exits and borrower liquidity weaken, Ares
may show growing FPAUM but lower realized returns, more amendments, slower
distributions, and greater reputational risk.

## Damodaran lens

The valuation model should separate:

`recurring management fees + conservative fee-related performance revenue -
compensation and corporate costs + probability-weighted realized performance
income - acquisition, debt, tax, partner, NCI, and credit/liquidity claims`

The critical reinvestment variable is return on new FPAUM and deployed capital.
The company is in a growth phase, but lifecycle risk arises if it expands into
lower-fee products or takes more credit risk to keep deployment and fundraising
moving. A high multiple requires fee-paying capital to compound per share,
credit losses to remain controlled, and acquisition returns to exceed the cost
of equity and debt.

## Valuation and thesis breakers

At the September 11, 2026 market snapshot, ARES was approximately $131.64 per
share with an equity value near $29.791B.

| Case | Normalized owner cash / earnings proxy | Multiple | Implied equity value |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Bear | $1.4B | 18x | $25.2B |
| Base | $1.8B | 22x | $39.6B |
| Bull | $2.3B | 26x | $59.8B |

The base case is above the market, but it requires FRE growth to convert into
common-owner value despite the 2025 acquisition outlay and high compensation.

Thesis breakers:

- FPAUM or fee rates slow while fundraising and distribution costs rise;
- available capital remains high but deployment returns, realizations, or
  distributions weaken;
- credit losses, nonaccruals, amendments, or borrower stress impair trust and
  fundraising;
- acquisition-related compensation, integration, goodwill, or dilution grows
  faster than per-share FRE;
- FRE's fee-related performance component proves less recurring than claimed;
  or
- company-only cash remains materially below FRE and distributions require
  leverage, asset sales, or capital issuance.

These are accounting and economic denominator tests, not allegations of
misconduct. Ares may own a valuable origination platform, but the return on
growth capital and the allocation of value between employees, funds, and common
shareholders remain decisive.
