# Arrow Electronics — technology distribution, working-capital financing, and thin-margin channel control

Date baseline: 2026-09-13  
Primary annual filing: [Arrow Electronics FY2025 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/7536/000110465926012765/arw-20251231x10k.htm)  
FY2025 results: [Arrow Electronics FY2025 results](https://news.arrow.com/news-releases/news-details/2026/Arrow-Electronics-Reports-Fourth-Quarter-and-Full-Year-2025-Results/default.aspx)  
Companion packet: [Arrow Electronics company packet](/home/mehtama1/git-repo/annual-report-research/extracted/technology/electronics-wholesale/arrow-electronics-inc/company-packet.md)

## Bottom line

Arrow Electronics distributes electronic components and enterprise-computing
solutions for manufacturers, industrial customers, cloud and enterprise IT
buyers, and technology suppliers. It operates more than 140 sales facilities
and 39 distribution and value-added centers across more than 85 countries.

The control point is availability and coordination. Arrow aggregates many
suppliers, holds inventory, provides design and technical support, manages
logistics, and helps customers procure components and systems without building
the entire supply chain themselves. The control point is valuable, but the
company operates on thin margins and carries a large balance sheet to make the
channel function.

FY2025 sales were $30.853B, operating income was $822M, and operating margin
was 2.7%. Operating cash flow was only $64M, down from $1.130B in 2024, as the
company increased inventory and supported future growth. Capital expenditures
were $101M. Accounts receivable were $19.739B, inventory was $5.082B, and cash
was $306M at year-end.

Arrow also sold $1.670B of receivables through its EMEA securitization program
and $1.131B through factoring programs during 2025. It had $970M of borrowings
under a North American receivables securitization program. These are ordinary
financing tools, but they show that working capital is part of the economic
product and that operating cash cannot be read without examining receivables
sales, supplier terms, inventory protections, and debt.

Current judgment: Arrow has real channel and procurement value, especially as
electronic systems become more complex. The owner-cash case is fragile until
inventory turns, customer collections, supplier protections, financing costs,
segment margins, and cash through a complete technology cycle are proven.

## What Arrow controls

`component or system demand -> supplier aggregation -> inventory and design support -> customer delivery -> payment and replenishment`

Arrow controls supplier relationships, distribution centers, local sales
coverage, inventory availability, design support, enterprise-computing
solutions, logistics, credit processes, and customer procurement data.
Suppliers control product pricing and allocation. Customers control demand,
payment, and sourcing decisions. Manufacturers and cloud companies can
sometimes buy directly or consolidate suppliers.

The control point is stronger when Arrow is involved early in a product design,
holds hard-to-source inventory, manages a complicated bill of materials, or
provides technical and supply-chain services that are difficult to replace.
It is weaker when customers can compare products easily, suppliers sell
directly, or a cycle leaves Arrow holding obsolete inventory.

## Segment economics

### Global Components

Global Components sold $21.501B in 2025, about 70% of consolidated sales. It
connects semiconductor and component suppliers with original-equipment
manufacturers, contract manufacturers, industrial customers, and other
buyers.

The economics depend on turns, gross margin, supplier price protection,
inventory rotation, design wins, allocation during shortages, customer credit,
and demand forecasting. A distributor can grow sales while earning little
incremental cash if it funds more inventory and receivables than its margin
can support.

Track inventory days, gross margin, return on working capital, supplier
protection, stock rotation, customer concentration, design-in revenue, and
cash released when demand normalizes.

### Global ECS

Global ECS sold $9.352B in 2025, about 30% of sales. It provides enterprise
computing solutions, infrastructure software, hardware, integration, and
related channel services.

The segment can carry more service and relationship value than simple
component movement, but it is exposed to enterprise IT budgets, project mix,
vendor incentives, systems integration, warranties, and lower-margin hardware.
Q2 2026 sales growth did not produce comparable operating-income growth in
ECS, so revenue acceleration must be tested against margin and cash flow.

## Earnings quality and forensic tests

### 1. 2025 operating cash showed the financing burden

Arrow reported $64M of operating cash in 2025 despite $822M of operating
income. Receivables used $6.342B of cash, inventory used $286M, and accounts
payable provided $6.108B. The company is an intermediary in supply-chain
transactions, so receivables and payables may move together, but the net
financing burden remains material.

Track cash conversion by segment, customer payment, supplier settlement,
inventory turns, working-capital days, and cash after debt and financing fees.
Reported margin is not enough evidence of a good distributor cycle.

### 2. Receivables sales bring cash forward

Arrow sold $1.670B of EMEA receivables through securitization and $1.131B
through factoring in 2025. Receivables sold are excluded from the balance
sheet and cash receipts appear in operating cash flow. The transactions may
reduce credit and collection risk, but they also convert future collections
into current cash and carry fees, collateral, covenants, and counterparty risk.

Reconcile operating cash with receivables sold, uncollected sold receivables,
factoring fees, recourse, collateral, credit losses, and what cash would have
been generated without selling receivables. Do not call securitization
proceeds recurring margin.

### 3. Receivable securitization is secured financing exposure

The North American program does not qualify for sale treatment. Arrow had
$970M of borrowings under it at December 31, 2025, secured by approximately
$3.0B of receivables. The receivables and debt remain on the balance sheet.

Track borrowing capacity, collateral, covenant headroom, interest cost,
customer aging, renewal of the program, and the effect of a credit or
inventory downturn on liquidity. The existence of collateralized funding is
not wrongdoing; it is evidence that the distribution model requires funding.

### 4. Inventory protection is conditional

Supplier protections covered about 56% of inventories for price reductions and
59% for repurchase arrangements at year-end 2025. Protection is valuable only
if contractual terms apply, suppliers remain solvent, products qualify, and
the timing of a demand decline does not exceed the protection window.

Review aging, turnover, obsolescence reserves, product generation, supplier
terms, price reductions, stock rotation, claims made, and realized recovery.
An inventory balance supported by supplier protection is not equivalent to
cash in the bank.

### 5. Revenue scale can hide a low-return business

Arrow’s 2025 sales were $30.853B, but operating margin was only 2.7%. The
company must turn large volumes while funding credit, inventory, facilities,
employees, logistics, and technology.

Track gross margin, operating expense, return on working capital, cash per
dollar of sales, customer concentration, supplier concentration, and the
portion of revenue that includes value-added services. The strongest economics
should appear in higher retention, margin, turns, and cash—not just sales.

### 6. Foreign exchange and investment gains can affect the period

Arrow operates globally and uses hedging. The 2025 cash flow also included a
$110M gain on investments and the company received $100M from selling an
equity investment. Separate these items from distribution operating earnings.

Track currency effects, hedge settlements, investment gains, tax effects, and
whether capital returns depend on asset sales or non-operating gains.

### 7. Acquisitions and restructuring need return evidence

The company requires liquidity for acquisitions, capital expenditures,
working capital, debt repayment, shareholder returns, and its operating
expense-efficiency plan. These actions can improve the platform, but recurring
restructuring and acquisition costs should be included in normalized owner
cash when they are part of the strategy.

Track purchase price, acquired sales and gross profit, working capital,
integration cost, supplier overlap, customer retention, cost savings, and
cash return after integration.

### 8. Stock compensation and repurchases affect cash per share

Stock-compensation amortization was $27.9M in 2025, $34.6M in 2024, and $41.6M
in 2023. Arrow repurchased $149.9M of stock in 2025 and $250.0M in 2024.

Compare diluted shares, awards, repurchase price, tax withholding, debt,
working-capital needs, and the cash cost of keeping the workforce. A buyback
can be poorly timed if the company is simultaneously financing inventory and
receivables.

## Alden macro and durability lens

Arrow is exposed to semiconductor cycles, industrial production, cloud and
enterprise IT spending, transportation electronics, inventory corrections,
interest rates, foreign exchange, tariffs, export rules, and supplier
capacity. A recovery can create a cash use before it creates a cash return
because the distributor must stock inventory and extend credit.

Liquidity is the central resilience test. Cash was only $306M against very
large receivables, inventory, debt, and supplier obligations. Securitization,
factoring, bank facilities, supplier terms, and inventory protection provide
flexibility, but they can tighten when customers, suppliers, or lenders become
more cautious.

## Damodaran narrative-to-numbers lens

The narrative is “Arrow is a global technology supply-chain and channel-control
platform.” The model must specify:

1. component and ECS revenue, gross margin, and operating margin;
2. inventory turns, product obsolescence, price protection, and rotation;
3. receivable days, customer credit, factoring, and securitization cost;
4. supplier terms, payables, concentration, and settlement timing;
5. design-support, integration, and value-added service retention;
6. enterprise, industrial, cloud, transportation, and geographic mix;
7. acquisitions, restructuring, cost savings, and integration cash;
8. debt, collateral, covenants, interest, and liquidity facilities;
9. stock compensation, diluted shares, repurchases, and dividends; and
10. normalized common cash after working-capital requirements through a cycle.

Arrow is a mature distribution business with a cyclical recovery overlay. A
higher valuation requires improved return on working capital and sustained
cash conversion, not merely faster sales growth.

## Valuation screen

The September 11, 2026 market snapshot showed equity value of approximately
$11.84B. The cases below use normalized common owner cash after routine
capital spending, working-capital funding, financing costs, inventory losses,
and dilution.

| Case | Normalized common owner cash | Equity cash multiple | Implied common equity value |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Bear | $250M | 10x | $2.5B |
| Base | $450M | 14x | $6.3B |
| Bull | $650M | 18x | $11.7B |

The current value is near the bull case in this conservative screen. That does
not prove the company is overvalued, but it means the valuation requires a
substantial recovery in cash conversion and return on working capital. The
bull case requires sustained technology demand, healthy inventory turns,
limited credit losses, stable supplier protection, and a larger share of
value-added ECS economics.

## Thesis breakers and next filings

The thesis weakens if:

- sales grow while operating cash remains weak or negative;
- receivables and inventory rise faster than sales;
- securitization and factoring become necessary to support ordinary operations;
- supplier price protection or stock rotation fails during a product correction;
- ECS sales grow without operating-income and cash flow improvement;
- customer or supplier concentration increases bargaining pressure;
- acquisitions and restructuring remain recurring without measurable cash returns;
- debt and collateral requirements tighten during a technology downturn; or
- buybacks continue while working-capital funding and liquidity remain strained.

The next filings should show cash conversion by segment, inventory aging,
protection claims, receivable aging, factoring and securitization volume and
cost, supplier-finance terms, debt collateral, customer concentration, ECS
margin, acquisitions, diluted shares, and cash per continuing share.

## Evidence grade

- Control point: **B+** — supplier aggregation, availability, technical support, and procurement workflow create value, but direct sourcing remains possible.
- Operating economics: **B-** — scale is significant, but operating margin is thin and working capital is enormous.
- Cash conversion: **C** — FY2025 operating cash was $64M against $822M of operating income, with major receivable financing and inventory investment.
- Balance-sheet resilience: **C+** — facilities and receivable programs provide liquidity, but cash is small relative to the funding system.
- Capital allocation: **B-** — repurchases and acquisitions are possible, but should follow working-capital and debt needs.
- Valuation: **C** — current value requires a strong recovery in cash conversion and return on working capital.
