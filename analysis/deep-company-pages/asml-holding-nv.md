# ASML: lithography control point with exceptional technical scarcity and concentrated risk

## Bottom line

ASML supplies the lithography systems that semiconductor manufacturers use to
print circuit patterns on wafers. Its strongest position is in extreme
ultraviolet lithography, where the system, optics, light source, software,
precision mechanics, and supplier network must work together inside a qualified
customer process.

This is one of the clearest technical control points in the semiconductor
industry. It is not a normal equipment company and should not be analyzed as
one. The scarcity is real. The valuation burden is also real.

ASML's 2025 net sales were €32.667B, gross margin was 52.8%, net income was
€9.609B, and US-GAAP operating cash flow was €12.659B. After property,
plant, equipment, and intangible purchases, the cash-after-capex screen was
about €11.027B. The company ended 2025 with a €38.8B backlog. But the largest
customer represented 23.9% of 2025 revenue, the two largest represented 38.0%,
China represented 29.1%, and sales require continuing research, supplier
coordination, capacity investment, and export-policy compliance.

At the September 14, 2026 market snapshot, equity value was approximately
$632.6B. The market therefore assumes that ASML's technical scarcity will
translate into many years of high system growth, high margins, service growth,
and successful next-generation products. The central investment question is
not whether ASML is strategically important. It is whether the price already
captures most of the economic value of that importance.

## Business economics: how to read the company

`customer node requirement -> lithography system and process qualification -> wafer capacity and yield -> installed-base upgrades and service -> cash`

ASML sells two economically different layers:

- **Systems:** new lithography tools, including EUV and DUV systems, plus
  metrology and inspection products.
- **Installed Base Management:** service, field options, upgrades, and related
  support for systems already operating at customer fabs.

In 2025, system sales were €24.474B and service and field-option sales were
€8.193B. Service and field-option sales grew 26.2%, faster than system sales.
That is evidence of a growing installed base and upgrade opportunity, but it
still depends on customer tool utilization, technology transitions, and the
continued economic value of older systems.

ASML does not control the semiconductor cycle, customer fab budgets, memory
pricing, export permissions, or every critical supplier. It controls a highly
qualified system architecture and the integration knowledge required to make
that architecture usable in high-volume manufacturing.

## Operating evidence

| Year ended December 31 | Net sales | Gross margin | Net income | US-GAAP OCF | Cash after capex and intangibles |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2023 | €27.6B | 51.3% | €7.8B | about €7.6B | about €5.4B |
| 2024 | €28.3B | 51.3% | €7.6B | €11.166B | €9.083B |
| 2025 | €32.7B | 52.8% | €9.6B | €12.659B | €11.027B |

The 2024 cash improvement was unusually affected by customer down payments and
the timing of supplier payments. That does not make the cash invalid, but it
means a three-year average is more informative than treating 2024 as a normal
run rate.

ASML's 2025 annual report identified 535 systems sold, including 48 EUV, 279
DUV, and 208 metrology and inspection systems. Its 2025 results were driven by
leading-edge logic, high-bandwidth memory, DDR5, and AI-related capacity
investment. The company reported €38.8B of year-end backlog, including written
system orders not yet recognized as revenue.

For the first half of 2026, Q1 revenue was €8.767B and Q2 revenue was €9.326B.
Q2 installed-base-management sales were €2.762B, gross margin was 54.0%, and
net income was €2.918B. Management raised 2026 revenue guidance to €43B–€45B
and gross-margin guidance to 54%–56%. Q2 order intake remained strong and the
company discussed capacity additions for low-NA EUV and DUV immersion systems.

## Earnings quality and forensic review

### 1. Scarcity is supported by the system architecture, not only by market share

EUV lithography requires a coordinated system of light generation, optics,
mechanical positioning, vacuum, software, contamination control, and process
integration. The value is created only when the full system operates reliably
inside the customer's fab. This creates a high qualification burden for new
entrants and supports pricing power.

The test is whether the company can preserve productivity, availability, and
cost-per-wafer advantages as customers move to new nodes and new packaging
methods. A technical lead can weaken if system productivity stalls, customer
process requirements change, or a competing approach becomes economically
acceptable even if it is not identical.

### 2. Backlog provides visibility, not guaranteed owner cash

The €38.8B backlog is large relative to 2025 sales. It contains accumulated
system-order values with accepted written authorizations that have not yet been
recorded as sales. It does not eliminate shipment, customer acceptance,
production, export-license, supplier, or cancellation risk.

The filing identifies timing risk around the number and timing of systems
ordered, shipped, and recognized as revenue; order cancellations or push-outs;
and restrictions on shipment under export controls. The proper test is backlog
conversion into collected cash and gross profit, not backlog growth by itself.

### 3. Customer concentration is unusually important

The largest customer represented €7.797B, or 23.9% of 2025 revenue. The two
largest customers represented 38.0%. The three largest customers accounted for
35.4% of accounts receivable and finance receivables at year-end.

This concentration has two effects. It creates bargaining and timing risk, but
it also reflects that only a small number of customers can buy leading-edge
systems at scale. The investment question is whether customer concentration is
an economic sign of scarcity or a financial risk that can overpower scarcity
when one customer delays a fab program.

### 4. China is a direct revenue and policy variable

Customers in China represented 29.1% of 2025 net sales, down from 36.1% in
2024. China demand was primarily connected to DUV and mature-node capacity;
EUV sales to China are restricted. ASML's filings state that export controls,
licensing requirements, tariffs, and countermeasures can restrict system sales
and services, increase supply-chain cost, create conflicting legal obligations,
and change the location of customer investment.

The risk is not simply lost China revenue. Restrictions can also cause order
push-outs, backlog changes, product reconfiguration, service limitations,
customer retaliation, and faster development of competing domestic tools.

### 5. Working capital can make reported profit and owner cash diverge

ASML's system sales can involve customer advances, milestone payments,
inventory, supplier commitments, and customer acceptance. In 2024, the cash
increase was materially helped by down payments and supplier-payment timing.
That is normal in a complex equipment business, but it is not a permanent
source of cash unless new orders continue and the related obligations are
fulfilled profitably.

Track customer advances, contract liabilities, inventory, accounts receivable,
finance receivables, supplier payments, backlog cancellations, and cash from
operations across an order cycle. A strong backlog accompanied by rising
inventory, delayed acceptance, or weak collections would reduce the quality of
the headline growth.

### 6. R&D and supplier investment are sustaining costs

2025 R&D was €4.699B, or 14.4% of revenue. ASML continues to fund NXE and EXE
systems, upgrades, metrology and inspection, software, and next-generation
lithography. Property, plant, equipment purchases were €1.574B and intangible
asset purchases were €57.6M in 2025.

The cash-after-capex screen is not the full maintenance cost. It must also
include the R&D and supplier ecosystem required to keep the control point
valuable. Cutting that spending may improve near-term cash while damaging the
future system roadmap. The correct valuation therefore treats a substantial
part of R&D and supplier-capacity investment as required reinvestment, even
though it is expensed or classified in different parts of the statements.

## Alden lens: strategic scarcity inside a semiconductor liquidity cycle

ASML benefits from long-term increases in computing, memory, advanced logic,
and lithography intensity. That supports durable demand. But customers still
finance fabs, decide technology timing, manage inventory, and respond to
interest rates, government subsidies, trade restrictions, and end-market
liquidity.

The macro stress test is a simultaneous slowdown in semiconductor capital
spending, a reduction in memory profitability, higher financing costs,
customer-specific fab delays, supply-chain disruption, and tighter export
controls. ASML may remain strategically indispensable while its revenue,
bookings, margins, and cash decline temporarily or structurally.

The company also has an unusual geopolitical exposure: it is a Dutch company
whose products depend on global suppliers and customers, but the relevant
export decisions involve Dutch, European, US, and potentially Chinese policy.
That creates regulatory uncertainty that cannot be modeled as a normal sales
territory adjustment.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers and valuation

The valuation chain is:

`lithography intensity -> system placements and price -> installed-base service and upgrades -> gross margin -> R&D and supplier investment -> owner cash`

The 2025 cash-after-capex screen was approximately €11.027B. At the September
14, 2026 market snapshot of about $632.6B, the equity value is roughly 57 times
that euro-denominated cash screen before adjusting for currency. That is not a
price target or a currency-adjusted valuation; it is a warning that the market
requires very high future cash and long duration.

Illustrative normalized owner-cash cases, converted at an assumed $1.10 per
euro only for comparison, are:

| Normalized owner cash | Multiple | Implied equity value |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| €7B bear | 28x | about $216B |
| €11B base | 38x | about $460B |
| €15B bull | 45x | about $743B |

The base case does not reach the current market value. The bull case requires
cash to exceed 2025 levels by a wide margin, sustained high margins, successful
next-generation systems, continued service growth, and no lasting impairment
from export restrictions or customer concentration.

This is the Damodaran question: does the narrative about AI, EUV scarcity, and
2030 revenue opportunity produce enough reinvestment-adjusted cash to justify
the price? Management's long-term 2030 opportunity of approximately €44B–€60B
revenue and 56%–60% gross margin is a useful scenario boundary, not a realized
cash flow.

## Possible financial-engineering questions

These are tests, not accusations:

- Are customer advances and milestone payments making current cash stronger
  than normalized cash after delivery obligations?
- Are backlog values being treated as sales even when shipment, acceptance,
  license, and cancellation risks remain?
- Are system upgrades shifted into installed-base revenue in ways that make
  service growth appear more recurring than it is?
- Does inventory rise ahead of demand or product transitions and later require
  write-downs?
- Are buybacks occurring at prices that assume a long peak cycle while R&D and
  supplier investment remain essential?
- Does customer concentration create pricing concessions or order timing that
  are hidden by company-wide margin and backlog figures?
- Are export restrictions recurring structural constraints being presented as
  temporary timing effects?

## What would prove this wrong

Downgrade the strongest version of the ASML thesis if future filings show that:

1. EUV productivity or system availability fails to improve enough to support
   customer economics.
2. Backlog converts slowly, cancellations increase, or customer advances stop
   supporting cash.
3. Installed-base sales weaken with tool utilization rather than providing a
   stable service and upgrade layer.
4. Customer concentration leads to material price concessions, order delays,
   or a sustained decline in fab investment.
5. China restrictions reduce sales, service, or supplier scale more than the
   non-China market can replace.
6. R&D, capacity, and supplier investment rise faster than gross profit.
7. A credible alternative lithography or patterning approach reduces ASML's
   pricing power or qualification advantage.
8. Buybacks and dividends consume cash needed for technology leadership or are
   funded during a peak earnings period.
