# Avnet — electronics distribution, inventory velocity, and the financing cost of availability

## The investment question

Avnet distributes semiconductors, interconnect, passive components, and other
electronics. It also provides design support, programming, testing, labeling,
point-of-use replenishment, supply-chain management, and materials-management
services. Its customers include manufacturers and engineering teams that need
components to be available at the right time, not merely listed in a catalog.

The question is whether supplier access, inventory availability, design support,
and working-capital discipline create durable owner cash after inventory,
receivables, vendor terms, obsolescence, price erosion, warehouses, technology,
debt, and the semiconductor cycle.

Avnet is a useful comparison with Arrow Electronics. It is also a technology
version of the broader distribution pattern: the intermediary may control the
relationship and timing while carrying the physical and financing burden.

## What Avnet controls and what it carries

| Layer | Avnet's role | Burden that remains with Avnet |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Component access | Aggregates suppliers and provides availability to OEMs and engineers | Inventory, obsolescence, price erosion, supplier dependence, and allocation risk |
| Design workflow | Provides engineering, programming, testing, labeling, and materials support | Technical labor, tooling, service cost, and uncertain return on attachment |
| Distribution network | Moves components through Electronic Components and Farnell | Warehouses, freight, systems, credit, and regional execution |
| Customer timing | Helps customers buy before their own production need is urgent | Receivables, inventory days, demand forecasting, and cash tied up in the channel |
| Common shareholder | Receives the residual after the network is funded | Thin margins, cycle risk, debt, working capital, and dilution |

The control point is reliable supply plus customer workflow. A customer can
change distributors, but doing so may require new supplier relationships,
engineering support, product substitutions, inventory policies, and production
planning. The switching cost is operational rather than absolute.

The quarter sequence shows why inventory days matter. Avnet did not merely grow
sales; it grew sales while reducing days of inventory. That supports better
return on working capital. It does not prove that every future growth dollar
will require the same amount of cash, especially if suppliers tighten terms or
customers slow production.

## How distribution becomes owner cash

The operating chain is:

`component demand -> supplier access and allocation -> inventory and customer
credit -> delivery and engineering support -> gross margin -> warehouse,
freight, labor, technology, and financing -> owner cash`

A distributor sells availability and coordination, but availability requires
capital. Inventory is an asset and a risk at the same time. Receivables are
revenue not yet collected. Vendor rebates and return privileges can reduce risk,
but they do not remove demand, aging, or supplier-dependence risk.

Farnell adds a more catalog- and engineering-attached layer. It may carry higher
service value and margin, but it also has a different inventory-turn and
customer-mix profile. The company should not be valued as if its two segments
have identical economics.

## Earnings quality and forensic questions

### 1. Did sales growth require more cash?

In Q3 FY2026, approximately `$800M` of sales growth used `$54M` of operating
cash support. That is not inherently poor; distribution growth often needs
inventory and receivables. The test is whether the cash support remains bounded
as growth continues, or whether the company must finance an ever-larger balance
sheet to maintain a thin spread.

### 2. Are inventory-return rights real protection?

Franchised agreements may allow inventory returns or provide protection from
obsolescence and price erosion. Read the terms, eligibility, timing, supplier
concentration, and historical utilization. A right that works only for selected
products or after strict conditions is useful but not equivalent to a risk-free
inventory asset. Track inventory aging, reserves, write-downs, and the portion
covered by supplier agreements.

### 3. Does margin improvement come from price, mix, or velocity?

FY2025 gross margin fell to `10.7%`, then operating margins improved sharply as
the cycle recovered. The next filings should separate supplier rebates, product
mix, regional mix, pricing, freight, warehouse productivity, and inventory
effects. A higher margin is stronger evidence when it arrives with lower
inventory days and stable collection, not merely with higher product prices.

### 4. Can Farnell and service attachment change the denominator?

Farnell represented about `6.5%` of FY2025 sales, while Electronic Components
represented about `93.5%`. Design support, programming, testing, and replenishment
may deepen the customer relationship, but the company must show incremental
gross profit and cash after technical labor and systems investment. A service
description is not proof of a higher return on capital.

### 5. Is the rebound being mistaken for a new normal?

FY2026 sales growth of `25%` and Q4 growth of `47.7%` are strong, but the prior
year was weak. Compare current revenue and margin with the full cycle, not only
the low base. The key test is what happens when semiconductor and industrial
customers stop rebuilding inventory and suppliers again offer excess capacity.

### 6. Does adjusted EPS reflect owner economics?

Adjusted diluted EPS reached `$5.67` in FY2026. Reconcile it to GAAP income,
stock compensation, restructuring, acquisition effects, inventory charges,
debt, and working-capital funding. A repurchase or adjusted-EPS increase is not
automatically valuable if the company is buying shares while inventory and debt
remain elevated.

## Balance-sheet resilience

The stress sequence is:

`electronics demand weakens -> inventory turns slow -> price erosion and reserves
rise -> receivables collect more slowly -> supplier terms or debt fund the gap ->
thin operating margin is consumed by financing and obsolescence`

Avnet's resilience depends on liquidity, supplier protection, inventory velocity,
and customer credit. Track cash, debt, interest, receivables, inventory days,
vendor payables, inventory reserves, customer concentration, and the amount of
growth supported by operating cash flow.

The company can look asset-light to a customer while being balance-sheet-heavy
to its shareholders. That is the central forensic point. The product is
availability, and availability is funded before the customer pays.

## Lyn Alden lens: the industrial cycle and liquidity

Avnet is exposed to industrial production, automotive electronics, defense and
aerospace, communications, data centers, and global manufacturing. The end
markets are diverse, but the common risk is that customers and suppliers change
inventory behavior together. In a tightening cycle, customers conserve cash and
suppliers reduce flexibility; the distributor can then absorb more working-capital
pressure precisely when margin is weakest.

Asia and global regional breadth can diversify demand, but it also adds currency,
geopolitical, trade, and regional-production risk. The durable model is one that
maintains inventory turns and supplier access through both shortage and excess
capacity, not one that only performs when allocation is scarce.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers

The narrative is “Avnet is a global electronics supply-chain intermediary whose
scale, service attachment, and inventory discipline produce a durable return on
working capital.” The numbers must specify sales volume, component price and
mix, gross margin, inventory days, receivables, vendor terms, return rights,
warehouse and service cost, capex, debt, taxes, dilution, and the cash required
to support the next recovery.

This dossier does not invent a live market-value multiple. A defensible valuation
must normalize owner cash across the electronics cycle and charge the capital
needed to maintain availability. The base case must show that inventory days
stay controlled without understocking customers, margins remain above the weak
cycle level, and service attachment earns more than its labor and systems cost.

An illustrative cash screen can be framed as `$400M`, `$700M`, and `$1.0B` of
normalized owner cash at `12x`, `18x`, and `24x`, implying `$4.8B`, `$12.6B`, and
`$24.0B`. These are not price targets. They show the sensitivity to a thin-margin
business whose cash depends on velocity and cycle position.

## Management incentives and capital allocation

Read the proxy for revenue, adjusted operating income, EPS, return on working
capital, inventory days, free cash flow, share price, and long-term return on
capital. A revenue target can reward inventory build. An EPS target can reward
buybacks while debt or receivables rise. An inventory-days target can improve by
understocking customers or shifting terms rather than creating a durable return.

For each acquisition or facility investment, compare added sales and gross profit
with inventory, receivables, labor, lease, technology, and integration cash. For
each repurchase, compare the price with mid-cycle owner cash and the capital
required to support customers through the next shortage or correction.

## Thesis breakers and next evidence

The thesis weakens if:

- sales recover while inventory days and receivables rise disproportionately;
- supplier return rights fail to cover aging or obsolete product;
- gross margin improvement reverses when semiconductor pricing normalizes;
- customer credit losses, bad debt, or chain concentration rise;
- Farnell and service programs consume cash without improving return on working capital;
- debt and interest grow faster than operating profit; or
- management continues repurchases while liquidity and inventory risk increase.

The next filings should show inventory aging and reserves, vendor-return usage,
receivable aging, supplier and customer concentration, segment margin, return on
working capital, capex, leases, debt, stock compensation, and cash per diluted
share through the next demand change.
