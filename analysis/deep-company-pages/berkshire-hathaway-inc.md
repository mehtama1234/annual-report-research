# Berkshire Hathaway: permanent capital, insurance float, and the successor capital-allocation test

## What the company is

Berkshire Hathaway is a holding company with four different economic layers:

1. insurance underwriting and investing, including GEICO and reinsurance;
2. operating businesses such as BNSF, Berkshire Hathaway Energy, manufacturing,
   service, retailing, McLane, and Pilot;
3. a large portfolio of marketable securities and cash equivalents; and
4. a parent company that decides how retained cash, insurance float, debt, and
   investment proceeds are allocated.

The control point is not one product or customer relationship. It is the ability
to retain capital inside a diversified group, fund liabilities conservatively,
and redeploy cash when an operating business, security, or acquisition offers a
satisfactory return. That control point is valuable only if the capital remains
available after insurance claims, regulated-utility needs, railroad renewal,
debt service, taxes, and operating-company reinvestment.

Berkshire should not be analyzed as a simple collection of subsidiaries. The
parent-level allocation decision changes the value of every operating dollar:
cash retained at a low return is a drag, while cash deployed into a durable
business or attractive security can compound per-share value.

## FY2025 financial baseline

| Measure | FY2023 | FY2024 | FY2025 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Net earnings attributable to Berkshire shareholders | $96.2B | $89.0B | $67.0B |
| Operating earnings | $37.4B | $47.4B | $44.5B |
| Net operating cash flow | $39.0B | $42.3B | $46.0B |
| Consolidated capital expenditures | $19.4B | $19.0B | $20.9B |
| Cash and U.S. Treasury bills at year-end | approximately $354B | approximately $334B | approximately $373B |
| Consolidated insurance claim liabilities | — | — | approximately $152B |

Net earnings include unrealized changes in the value of equity securities and
other investment gains or losses. Operating earnings exclude those market-value
changes and are the better starting point for recurring performance, although
they still include insurance investment income, underwriting results, and
operating subsidiaries with different capital needs.

Operating cash flow of $46.0B is also not unrestricted parent cash. Insurance
cash flows include premiums received before claims are paid. Operating
subsidiaries need working capital and capital expenditures. BNSF and Berkshire
Hathaway Energy have large asset bases. The parent cannot distribute all
consolidated operating cash without reducing resilience or increasing debt at
the subsidiaries.

## Segment economics

### Insurance underwriting and investment

Insurance generates two linked but distinct earnings streams. Underwriting
profit is premium earned less claims and operating expenses. Investment income
comes from assets funded by shareholder capital and insurance liabilities, often
called float.

The float is not free cash in the ordinary corporate sense. It is capital that
can be invested while Berkshire owes future claims. Its value depends on the
amount, duration, cost, liquidity, and risk of the liabilities. Berkshire’s
combined insurance operations generated pre-tax underwriting gains in each of
2023, 2024, and 2025, and the reported average cost of float was negative in
each year. That is strong evidence of underwriting quality, but catastrophe
losses and reserve development can reverse results over time.

The insurance portfolio also creates interest-rate, credit, equity-market,
currency, and liquidity exposure. A high cash balance can reduce investment
return, while a reach for yield can increase loss severity during a stress
period.

### BNSF railroad

BNSF owns a capital-intensive freight network. Its economics depend on volume,
mix, pricing, service, labor, fuel, safety, and track and equipment renewal.
Railroad operating earnings were approximately $7.4B in 2025 according to the
annual segment data, while capital expenditures were approximately $3.8B.

BNSF therefore contributes less owner cash than operating earnings alone imply.
The test is whether service quality and the railroad’s physical condition are
being maintained after the capital program. Deferred maintenance can make a
period of high cash conversion look better than the economic reality.

### Berkshire Hathaway Energy

BHE operates regulated utilities and energy businesses. Its economics depend
on rate recovery, allowed returns, regulatory relationships, fuel and power
markets, transmission investment, wildfire and environmental obligations, and
access to financing.

FY2025 capital expenditures were approximately $10.6B, making BHE the largest
capital user in the group. Its reported earnings should not be compared with a
capital-light service subsidiary. Rate-base growth can create future earnings,
but it first requires debt, equity, customer funding, and regulatory approval.

### Manufacturing, service, and retailing

This group includes a broad set of businesses with different economics. The
2025 segment generated approximately $13.6B of operating earnings and used
approximately $5.3B of capital expenditures across manufacturing, service,
retailing, McLane, and Pilot according to the segment data.

The analytical risk is aggregation. A strong company can hide a weak one, and
reported segment earnings can obscure working capital, inventory, warranty,
labor, and competitive differences. The holding-company discount is partly a
price for this lack of comparability and transparency.

## Earnings quality and financial-engineering review

### 1. Operating earnings are useful but not a complete owner-cash measure

Berkshire emphasizes operating earnings because GAAP net income changes with
market prices of securities. That is appropriate for judging operating trends.
It does not eliminate the need to fund BNSF, BHE, insurance reserves, working
capital, taxes, debt, and acquisitions.

The correct reconciliation is by segment:

- insurance underwriting and investment income after required capital;
- BNSF earnings after maintenance and growth capital;
- BHE earnings after utility and energy capital and regulatory funding;
- manufacturing and service earnings after inventory, receivables, and capital;
- parent cash and securities after taxes, debt, and acquisition commitments.

### 2. Insurance float can be misread as corporate cash

The insurance group had approximately $152B of consolidated claim liabilities
at year-end 2025. Premium cash can be invested before claims are paid, but the
liability remains. A catastrophe, reserve strengthening, or reinsurance
recoverability problem can require cash when asset markets are weak.

The relevant measures are float growth, average cost of float, underwriting
profit, reserve development, catastrophe losses, investment duration, credit
quality, and liquidity—not the gross size of the insurance investment portfolio.

### 3. Capital spending is large and unevenly distributed

Consolidated capital spending was approximately $20.9B in 2025. That number
includes different economic categories. BHE and BNSF require physical renewal;
manufacturing and service businesses require equipment and facilities; Pilot
and leasing operations require fleet investment.

Depreciation is not a complete maintenance-capital measure. The test is whether
cash capital spending is sustaining capacity and safety or creating profitable
growth. A fall in capital spending could increase short-term cash while
reducing long-term earning power.

### 4. Marketable securities make GAAP earnings volatile

Changes in equity-security values flow through consolidated net earnings even
when Berkshire does not sell the securities. A market decline can reduce GAAP
earnings without reducing the long-term value of a high-quality holding, while
a market increase can raise GAAP earnings without creating cash.

The risk is not that market-value accounting is improper. The risk is using
reported net income as if it were recurring operating cash, or using operating
earnings while ignoring the economic return on the large retained cash and
securities portfolio.

### 5. Acquisitions and retained cash test the successor

Berkshire acquired OxyChem for approximately $9.7B in early 2026 and agreed to
acquire Taylor Morrison for approximately $8.5B, according to the company’s
2026 announcements. These transactions provide an early test of the post-
Buffett capital-allocation process.

The relevant test is not whether the acquisition is understandable. It is
whether the acquired operating cash, required capital, cyclicality, debt, and
integration risk produce a return above the opportunity cost of the cash used.
The same test applies to large public-equity purchases and to the decision to
hold hundreds of billions in Treasury bills.

## Balance-sheet resilience

Berkshire’s strength is financial flexibility. The group had substantial cash
and Treasury bills, diversified operating cash flows, and access to capital.
Its weakness is that much of the balance sheet is attached to policyholder,
regulated, operating, or subsidiary obligations.

The balance-sheet review should separate:

- parent cash and Treasury bills available for allocation;
- insurance investments supporting claims and required capital;
- regulated utility assets and debt;
- railroad assets and renewal needs;
- subsidiary debt and guarantees;
- marketable securities subject to price volatility;
- goodwill and intangible assets created by acquisitions.

The parent guaranteed approximately $20.1B of subsidiary debt at December 31,
2025. That is manageable relative to Berkshire’s size, but it demonstrates that
the parent is connected to obligations below the holding-company level.

## Lyn Alden macro, liquidity, and durability lens

Berkshire is unusually exposed to nominal growth, inflation, interest rates,
credit conditions, catastrophe frequency, regulation, and asset-market prices.
It can benefit from inflation through pricing and nominal asset values, but it
also bears higher replacement costs, wage pressure, insurance claims, and
capital requirements.

The liquidity advantage is the ability to hold cash when other owners need to
sell. The opportunity cost is that a large Treasury-bill portfolio may earn less
than operating businesses or equities over a long period. The decision to hold
cash is therefore an active capital-allocation choice, not a risk-free increase
in intrinsic value.

The durability test is whether decentralized operating managers continue to
produce cash while the parent allocates capital without weakening underwriting,
utility credit, railroad service, or subsidiary reinvestment.

## Damodaran narrative-to-numbers lens

The narrative is that Berkshire is permanent capital with a culture of rational
capital allocation, conservative finance, and diversified operating cash flows.

That narrative requires numerical links:

1. Operating earnings per share must grow after maintenance capital and required
   insurance capital are funded.
2. The cash and securities portfolio must earn an acceptable return relative to
   its opportunity cost and liquidity value.
3. Acquisitions must add per-share value rather than simply increase revenue or
   reported operating earnings.
4. BNSF and BHE must earn acceptable returns after large renewal and expansion
   capital.
5. Greg Abel and the successor team must allocate capital with the same margin
   of safety without relying on the prior CEO’s reputation.

The lifecycle is mature. Berkshire’s value comes less from rapid consolidated
revenue growth and more from retained earnings, underwriting discipline, capital
deployment, and the ability to buy attractive assets during periods of stress.

## Valuation screen

The market feed showed an equity value of approximately $719B. A simple
operating-earnings multiple is incomplete because it does not separately value
cash, marketable securities, insurance float, or the capital required by the
operating subsidiaries. The following screen uses normalized operating earnings
as a first pass and does not add cash again; a full sum-of-the-parts model must
avoid double counting.

| Case | Normalized operating earnings | Multiple | Implied equity value | Required interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Bear | $38B | 15x | $570B | Lower insurance and operating earnings, weak capital deployment |
| Base | $45B | 17x | $765B | FY2025 earnings persist and cash earns a reasonable return |
| Bull | $52B | 19x | $988B | Successful successor allocation, durable underwriting, strong operating growth |

The current market value is close to the base screen. That means the market is
already crediting Berkshire for balance-sheet quality and future allocation
capacity. The valuation becomes less attractive if cash remains idle for years,
operating returns weaken, or successor acquisitions require excessive capital.

## Thesis breakers and future filing tests

| Question | Evidence that would support the thesis | Evidence that would weaken it |
|---|---|---|
| Is insurance float economically valuable? | Negative or low average cost of float, underwriting gains, stable reserves | Catastrophe losses, reserve strengthening, or higher cost of float |
| Is operating cash durable? | Operating cash grows after normal working capital and maintenance capital | OCF rises while BHE/BNSF renewal is deferred or working capital reverses |
| Is BHE creating value? | Allowed returns and rate-base growth exceed funding and regulatory burdens | Capital spending rises while earned returns and cash conversion weaken |
| Is BNSF competitive? | Volume, pricing, service, and operating efficiency improve after renewal capital | Service and productivity lag while capex remains high |
| Is retained cash productive? | Acquisitions and securities earn more than Treasury-bill opportunity cost | Cash remains idle or acquisitions dilute per-share value |
| Is succession working? | Abel allocates capital with comparable discipline and transparency | Large acquisitions, buybacks, or investments occur without adequate return evidence |
| Is the conglomerate structure useful? | Diversification lowers financing and distress risk while capital moves efficiently | Complexity hides weak subsidiaries or creates a persistent value discount |

## Bottom line

Berkshire’s real franchise is permanent capital combined with underwriting,
operating autonomy, financial strength, and capital-allocation judgment. The
company controls the allocation decision, but it carries every underlying
burden: insurance claims, regulated-utility capital, railroad renewal, factory
and service investment, taxes, debt, and successor execution.

FY2025 supports the durability case: operating earnings were $44.5B, operating
cash flow was $46.0B, insurance underwriting remained profitable, and liquidity
was exceptional. It does not prove that future cash will be allocated as well as
in the past. The next phase must be judged by per-share operating earnings,
required capital, acquisition returns, cash utilization, reserve quality, and
the discipline of the new capital-allocation team.

**Evidence grade:** Strong for FY2025 segment earnings, operating cash flow,
capital spending, insurance liabilities, and liquidity; medium for normalized
owner cash and subsidiary return on capital; unresolved for successor-era
acquisition returns and long-term capital deployment.
