# Boston Scientific — procedure adoption, acquisition growth, and owner cash

As of 2026-09-13. FY2025 figures are filed GAAP figures unless explicitly
identified otherwise; dollar amounts are in millions.

## How to read this memo

Boston Scientific develops and sells medical devices used in cardiovascular,
endoscopy, urology, neuromodulation, and related procedures. A procedure-led
device company earns revenue when a hospital or physician uses a product in a
patient procedure. The economic control point comes from clinical evidence,
physician training, hospital approval, product reliability, and the ability to
fit the device into a repeat care pathway.

GAAP is the accounting rule set used in the filed statements. Adjusted EPS
removes items such as amortization, impairment, acquisition costs,
restructuring, litigation, and selected tax items. Those adjustments may help
describe the operating portfolio, but they do not remove the cash required to
buy a business, replace a product, defend quality, or compensate employees.

## Investment question

Boston Scientific has a real control point in specialized procedure workflows.
Its products can become part of physician preference, hospital stocking,
training, and clinical protocols. The investment question is whether that
clinical position creates durable common cash after research and development,
sales training, inventory, regulatory work, quality obligations, acquisition
payments, integration, amortization, stock compensation, and hospital budget
pressure.

The company is not a broad medical distributor. It is also not a pure software
business. Its growth depends on developing and qualifying physical products,
earning physician adoption, manufacturing reliably, and expanding the number
of procedures in which its products are used.

whether those purchases are optional growth or a recurring requirement to
replace mature products and maintain growth.

## What Boston Scientific controls

The company reports two broad reportable segments:

- **Cardiovascular** includes electrophysiology, structural heart, coronary,
  peripheral, and related cardiovascular technologies. The current growth
  examples include the FARAPULSE pulsed-field ablation system and WATCHMAN
  left-atrial-appendage-closure devices.
- **MedSurg** includes endoscopy, urology, neuromodulation, and other medical
  specialties. These products are used in recurring hospital and physician
  procedures, but demand depends on clinical evidence, staffing, reimbursement,
  and procedure capacity.

The control point is a set of linked approvals and habits:

1. Clinical evidence supports use in a particular patient population.
2. Regulators authorize the product for defined uses.
3. Physicians learn the procedure and become comfortable using it.
4. Hospitals approve the product, stock it, and train staff.
5. The procedure becomes part of a repeat care pathway.
6. The company supplies reliable products, updates the platform, and supports
   the users.

This position can create switching friction without making the product
irreplaceable. Hospitals can negotiate price, physicians can change technique,
competitors can introduce a better device, and regulators can limit use. The
company therefore has to keep proving clinical benefit while defending its
commercial and manufacturing economics.

The 2025 10-K reports 19.9% sales growth, of which 15.8% was organic after
foreign currency and specified acquisition/divestiture effects. That is strong
organic growth, but acquisition spending still reached $1.593B. In 2024,
acquisitions included Axonics and Silk Road Medical and cost $4.640B. A clean
growth judgment therefore requires both the organic denominator and the return
on the purchased portfolio.

## Procedure and hospital economics

Boston Scientific's revenue depends on a completed procedure, but the company
does not control every condition needed for that procedure. Hospitals must have
staff, operating-room or catheter-lab capacity, reimbursement, physician time,
and a budget for the device. A product can be clinically attractive while
procedure volume remains constrained by staffing or payer policy.

The economic proof is product-level or franchise-level utilization. Track units
used, price per unit, gross margin, physician adoption, hospital account
penetration, reimbursement changes, product returns, and inventory. Revenue
growth without rising utilization may reflect price or acquisitions. Rising
utilization without adequate price may transfer the value to hospitals or
payers.

## Acquisition and lifecycle burden

The company uses acquisitions to enter new categories, add technology, and
expand its clinical portfolio. In 2025, acquisitions included Bolt Medical,
SoniVie, Cortex, Intera, and Anrei Medical. This broadens the possible growth
path but also adds integration work, acquired intangible amortization,
contingent consideration, product-quality review, and commercial overlap.

The filing's 2025 adjusted reconciliation excluded $897M of amortization, $245M
of acquisition/divestiture-related net charges, $343M of restructuring and
related charges, and $46M of goodwill and intangible impairment. Those items
are not all equivalent. Amortization may be non-cash in the current period, but
the acquisition that created the intangible required cash. Restructuring may be
non-recurring for one transaction while recurring for a company that continually
rebuilds its portfolio.

The lifecycle question is central: can existing products fund their own
replacement through R&D and clinical development, or must Boston Scientific
continually buy growth because products mature faster than internally developed
products replace them?

## Cash conversion and balance sheet

Operating cash flow increased from $2.503B in 2023 to $4.534B in 2025. That is
strong, but cash was also used for $876M of PP&E and internal-use software,
$1.593B of acquisitions, and $254M of investments and technology acquisitions
in 2025. The company also paid $62M of previously established contingent
consideration in 2025.

Receivables increased from $2.558B at the end of 2024 to $2.926B at the end of
2025, and inventory increased from $2.810B to $2.943B. This can be consistent
with growth, launches, and acquisitions. It also means that the cash bridge
must test collection, inventory turns, reserves, product transitions, and
acquired working-capital quality.

Cash recovered to $1.965B at year-end 2025. The company issued $1.558B net of
costs from euro-denominated senior notes in 2025 while repaying maturing debt
and reducing short-term borrowings. Liquidity is therefore not the same as
unlevered owner cash. Debt service, finance leases, acquisition funding, and
future product investment remain claims on the operating system.

## Earnings quality and forensic questions

### 1. How much growth is truly organic?

Reconcile reported sales growth to operational growth and organic growth by
business. Identify the contribution from Axonics, Silk Road Medical, Bolt,
SoniVie, Cortex, Intera, Anrei, and other acquired businesses. Test whether
organic growth remains high after the acquired portfolio reaches a full-year
comparison.

### 2. Are adjusted earnings charging the economic cost of acquisitions?

Reconcile GAAP profit to adjusted EPS line by line. Track amortization,
impairments, acquisition charges, restructuring, contingent consideration,
litigation, and inventory step-up. Compare those charges with acquisition cash
over several years. A repeated acquisition program can make “one-time” charges
part of the normal cost of maintaining the reported growth rate.

### 3. Is clinical adoption creating pricing power or only volume?

Measure units, price, concessions, gross profit, hospital approvals, procedure
volume, physician training, and repeat use. A successful product can increase
clinical use while hospitals negotiate away much of the economic benefit.

### 4. Are quality and regulatory costs fully visible?

Read product recalls, field actions, complaints, legal proceedings, regulatory
communications, reserve changes, and insurance recoveries. A device failure can
affect future adoption, regulatory status, inventory, litigation, and brand
trust long after the initial revenue was recorded.

### 5. Is R&D replacing the installed portfolio?

R&D rose to $2.052B in 2025 from $1.615B in 2024 and $1.414B in 2023. Test the
return on this spending through approvals, launches, adoption, gross margin,
and product longevity. R&D is necessary investment, but not every program
creates a durable product or recovers its cost.

### 6. Is dilution being offset?

Stock-based compensation was $299M in 2025, up from $266M in 2024 and $233M in
2023. Compare shares issued under compensation plans with actual repurchases
and diluted share count. Adjusted EPS growth is weaker for owners if the share
base expands while acquisition goodwill and intangibles also rise.

## Lyn Alden, Damodaran, and forensic synthesis

- **Lyn Alden lens:** procedure demand is supported by aging and medical need,
  but hospital budgets, reimbursement, labor shortages, and interest rates
  determine how quickly clinical demand becomes paid activity. Balance-sheet
  resilience and product reliability matter during periods when hospitals
  defer capital and procedures.
- **Damodaran lens:** translate category leadership into procedure volume,
  market share, price, gross margin, R&D reinvestment, and return on acquired
  capital. Value the mature portfolio, internal pipeline, and acquisition
  program separately rather than applying one growth multiple to all sales.
- **Forensic lens:** the main presentation risks are treating adjusted EPS as
  owner cash, treating organic growth as independent of acquisition strategy,
  treating amortization as irrelevant without charging replacement cost,
  treating innovation claims as returns before clinical adoption, and treating
  buybacks or share issuance without a diluted-share analysis.

## Valuation framing

The September 11, 2026 market snapshot was approximately $63.387B of equity
value at $42.98 per share. The 2025 simple residual of $2.065B implies roughly
31 times that cash screen; the screen falls to about $1.811B after strategic
investments.

A normalized range of $1.2B / $2.0B / $2.8B at 25x / 30x / 35x implies
approximately $30B / $60B / $98B of equity value. The range is deliberately
wide because the current price requires continued high organic growth, strong
clinical adoption, and successful acquisition integration. The base case does
not assume that 2024's $4.640B acquisition year repeats every year, but it
does assume that some external spending remains necessary to sustain category
expansion.

## Evidence grades and thesis breakers

| Question | Current grade | What would upgrade or weaken it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Procedure and clinical control point | A- | Repeat utilization, physician adoption, hospital approvals, and durable product evidence |
| Organic growth | A- | Organic growth remains high after acquired businesses reach full comparison |
| Cash conversion | B | 2025 was strong, but 2023–24 were acquisition-heavy and negative on the simple screen |
| Acquisition returns | C+ | Franchise-level revenue, margin, cash, and integration evidence after purchase |
| Product lifecycle and R&D | B- | New products replace mature revenue with adequate return on R&D |
| Quality and regulatory burden | B- | Stable recalls, legal reserves, field actions, and product reliability |
| Dilution and capital allocation | B- | Share count, SBC, debt, and repurchases remain aligned with common owners |

Thesis breakers:

1. Organic growth falls materially when recent acquisitions are removed.
2. Acquired businesses require repeated restructuring, impairment, or additional
   investment without producing the planned procedure growth and cash.
3. Clinical adoption grows but price concessions and hospital bargaining reduce
   incremental gross profit.
4. Product recalls, regulatory action, litigation, or quality failures damage
   adoption or create recurring cash costs.
5. R&D and sales investment rise faster than procedure utilization and durable
   gross profit.
6. Receivables or inventory rise faster than sales, indicating weaker
   collections, launch overstock, or acquired working-capital problems.
7. Stock compensation, debt, and acquisition spending consume the benefit of
   reported EPS growth for common shareholders.
