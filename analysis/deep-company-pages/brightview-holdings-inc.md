# BrightView Holdings — recurring landscape maintenance, seasonal labor, and the hidden fleet burden

Date baseline: 2026-09-13  
Primary filing: [BrightView FY2025 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1734713/000119312525288109/bv-20250930.htm)  
Company IR: [FY2025 results and outlook](https://investor.brightview.com/press-releases/press-release-details/2025/BrightView-Posts-Q4-and-FY-2025-Earnings-With-Record-Adjusted-EBITDA-Projects-Fiscal-Year-2026-Revenue-Adjusted-EBITDA-and-Margin-Growth-Increases-Existing-Share-Repurchase-Authorization/default.aspx)  
Companion packet: [BrightView company packet](/home/mehtama1/git-repo/annual-report-research/extracted/industrial-goods/business-services/brightview-holdings-inc/company-packet.md)

## Bottom line

BrightView provides commercial landscaping and related outdoor-site services.
Its two reported businesses are Maintenance Services and Development Services.
Maintenance covers recurring care of commercial and institutional properties;
Development covers installation and larger landscape projects.

The company has a real recurring-service base and scale across campuses,
offices, hotels, airports, hospitals, schools, municipalities, retail,
restaurants, and golf courses. It also has an unusually direct labor-supply
exposure: the FY2025 filing says BrightView expects to continue using the U.S.
H-2B temporary visa program for part of its seasonal labor needs. It employed
about 1,500 H-2B seasonal workers in FY2025 versus 2,000 in FY2024.

The important financial finding is that labor is only one part of the burden.
FY2025 operating cash flow was $291.8M, but capital expenditures were $254.2M,
including $205.0M in Maintenance Services. BrightView reported adjusted free
cash flow of $65.2M after capex and equipment-sale proceeds. The business can
look cash generative before fleet and equipment renewal while producing much
less cash for debt reduction or common shareholders after that renewal.

Current judgment: BrightView has a useful maintenance relationship and
operating scale, but its economics depend on labor availability, contract
pricing, weather, fleet replacement, self-insurance, project timing, and high
leverage. The investment question is whether maintenance contracts can earn a
satisfactory return after the people and equipment needed to deliver them.

## What BrightView controls

`property-upkeep need -> maintenance or development contract -> crews, fleet, materials, and supervision -> site condition -> renewal, expansion, or rebid`

BrightView controls branch density, crew scheduling, local customer
relationships, equipment deployment, landscape design and installation, and
the execution of recurring upkeep. Customers control property budgets,
occupancy, capital projects, rebids, and the scope of work. Weather and labor
policy are outside BrightView’s control.

The relationship has value because poor site upkeep is visible to property
owners and occupants and because changing providers requires a new operating
team. That switching friction is weaker than a legal or technical monopoly.
Customers can rebid, reduce scope, delay development, or bring work in-house.

## Reported economics

Fiscal years ended September 30; dollars in millions.

| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2023 | — | (7.7) | 129.9 | 71.3 | 80.2* | 22.1 |
| 2024 | 2,767.1 | 66.4 | 205.6 | 78.4 | 145.3 | 5.1 |
| 2025 | 2,672.8 | 56.0 | 291.8 | 254.2 | 65.2 | 0.5 |

`*` 2023 is calculated as operating cash flow less capex plus property-sale
proceeds; the filing’s reported adjusted FCF table covers 2025 and 2024.

Revenue fell 3.4% in FY2025. Maintenance revenue fell 3.7% to $1,891.3M,
while Maintenance segment adjusted EBITDA rose 3.9% to $245.5M and margin rose
to 13.0%. Development revenue fell 2.4% to $789.1M, while Development segment
adjusted EBITDA rose 20.6% to $106.8M and margin rose to 13.5%.

The combination requires care. Margin improved while revenue declined, partly
because cost-management actions reduced personnel, vehicle, and equipment
costs. That may be a genuine productivity improvement, or it may indicate a
lower spending level that must be reversed to preserve capacity. The large
FY2025 capex increase makes the second question material.

## Labor and the H-2B constraint

BrightView reported approximately 18,600 employees at September 30, 2025,
including seasonal workers. It had approximately 15,400 Maintenance full-time
employees, 2,500 Development full-time employees, and 300 corporate staff.
The company says it uses H-2B labor in certain markets to satisfy part of its
seasonal need and warns that insufficient seasonal workers could cause labor
shortages, lower service quality or timeliness, and higher recruiting,
training, and wage costs.

This makes immigration policy a direct operating variable. A reduction in
visa availability can force BrightView to choose among paying more, accepting
lower growth, using less experienced workers, delaying work, or accepting
lower margins. The filing does not establish that H-2B workers are paid below
market or that any misconduct occurred. It does establish dependence on a
labor channel whose capacity is not fully controlled by management.

Track H-2B workers, total seasonal staffing, vacancies, wage rates, overtime,
turnover, recruiting cost, productivity, safety incidents, workers’
compensation, and the ability to pass labor costs through customer pricing.

## Maintenance versus Development

Maintenance is the more recurring engine. It is affected by property budgets,
renewal, weather, fuel, labor, and route density. Development is more exposed
to project timing, customer construction budgets, materials, estimates, and
the timing of project completion.

FY2025 demonstrates why the split matters. Development revenue declined, but
development margin improved. The improvement may reflect project mix and
execution rather than a durable change in the whole company. Maintenance
revenue also declined while margin improved after cost management. The next
filings should show whether price, volume, mix, productivity, or deferred work
produced the margin change.

## The fleet and equipment test

Management describes the model as having limited capital expenditures, but
FY2025 capex was $254.2M, up from $78.4M in FY2024. Maintenance Services used
$205.0M and Development Services used $49.2M. BrightView also received $27.6M
from equipment sales.

The relevant owner-cash question is not whether capex is “growth” or
“maintenance” by label. It is how much spending is required to keep crews
productive, replace worn equipment, meet service standards, and support
revenue. Equipment sales reduce the reported burden but do not eliminate the
need to replace the fleet.

The next filing should reconcile fleet age, replacement cycle, used-equipment
proceeds, finance leases, maintenance capex, growth capex, and equipment
utilization. A return to lower capex is positive only if service capacity and
revenue do not deteriorate.

## Cash quality and financial engineering tests

### 1. Operating cash benefited from working-capital timing

FY2025 operating cash flow included a $12.2M source from accounts receivable,
a $28.4M source from unbilled and deferred revenue, and an $8.0M source from
accounts payable and other operating liabilities. These movements are not
necessarily problematic, but they show that cash conversion depends partly on
billing, collection, and payment timing.

In FY2025 BrightView’s net working capital fell to $151.8M from $236.8M. The
decline was driven partly by lower cash, unbilled revenue, and receivables.
Track whether future operating cash comes from sustainable earnings or from
temporary working-capital release.

### 2. Adjusted EBITDA and adjusted FCF exclude real recurring burdens

FY2025 adjusted EBITDA was $352.3M and adjusted net income was $113.2M, while
GAAP net income was $56.0M. The company excludes items such as business
integration costs and selected transformation initiatives from non-GAAP
measures. If acquisitions, restructuring, and operating redesign recur, they
belong in a long-term owner-cash assessment even if they are individually
described as unusual.

Adjusted FCF is more useful than adjusted EBITDA because it charges capex, but
it still does not answer whether preferred dividends, finance-lease principal,
debt repayment, and common-stock distributions leave enough cash for the
common shareholder.

### 3. Debt and preferred claims sit ahead of common equity

At September 30, 2025, BrightView reported $790.2M of total debt and $74.5M
of cash. Financing cash flow included $48.4M of finance-lease repayments,
$35.8M of Series A preferred dividends, $29.1M of receivables-financing
repayments, and $24.2M of common repurchases and distributions.

The common-equity valuation must therefore use cash after fleet renewal,
interest, lease principal, preferred dividends, and required debt repayment.
The preferred shares are also convertible and affect the fully diluted share
count used in adjusted EPS. A buyback is not evidence of excess owner cash if
the company is simultaneously relying on receivables financing or adding
debt.

## Alden macro and durability lens

BrightView is sensitive to commercial property occupancy, hotel and airport
activity, construction and development budgets, snowfall and weather, fuel,
wages, immigration policy, interest rates, and credit availability.

Maintenance demand can be more stable than development, but stability does
not make profit fixed. A weak property market can reduce scope or delay
projects. A labor shortage can raise wages and reduce service capacity. Heavy
snow can increase revenue and also increase labor, equipment, and insurance
costs. High leverage makes these operating changes more important because
cash must first support interest, leases, working capital, and fleet renewal.

Liquidity depends on cash from operations, the revolving facility, receivables
financing, and asset sales. The company states that these sources are expected
to fund current obligations and capital needs, but that statement should be
tested against capex, debt maturities, interest rates, and seasonal working
capital rather than accepted as a durability conclusion.

## Damodaran narrative-to-numbers lens

The narrative is “BrightView is a scaled recurring landscape-maintenance
platform with a project-development arm and better margins after cost control.”
The valuation model must specify:

1. maintenance renewal, pricing, route density, and customer concentration;
2. development volume, project timing, and estimate risk;
3. H-2B availability, domestic labor cost, turnover, and productivity;
4. fuel, insurance, safety, and claims assumptions;
5. fleet replacement and equipment-sale proceeds;
6. working-capital timing and receivables financing;
7. debt, lease principal, preferred dividends, and dilution; and
8. normalized cash available to common shareholders.

BrightView is a mature leveraged service platform attempting to improve
profitability through cost control, mix, and operating scale. A higher value
requires proof that margin improvement survives adequate staffing and fleet
renewal.

## Valuation screen

The September 11, 2026 market snapshot showed common equity value of
approximately $1.036B. The cases below use cash available after a normalized
fleet and operating reinvestment charge; they are not price targets.

| Case | Normalized common owner cash | Equity cash multiple | Implied common equity value |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Bear | $45M | 10x | $450M |
| Base | $75M | 14x | $1.050B |
| Bull | $110M | 18x | $1.980B |

The market value is close to the base screen. The base case requires
maintenance revenue stability, manageable labor cost, lower but adequate
fleet spending, stable collections, and no material deterioration in leverage
or preferred-share dilution. The bull case requires durable margin gains
without underinvesting in equipment or depending on unusually favorable
weather and working-capital timing.

## Thesis breakers and next filings

The thesis weakens if:

- H-2B availability falls and wage or recruiting costs rise without pricing
  recovery;
- maintenance revenue, renewal, or route density weakens while cost cuts
  reduce service capacity;
- capex remains near FY2025 levels without corresponding revenue and cash
  returns;
- operating cash falls when receivables and unbilled revenue normalize;
- adjusted EBITDA rises while GAAP earnings, adjusted FCF, or common cash do
  not improve;
- debt, receivables financing, finance leases, or preferred claims consume
  most cash after fleet renewal; or
- development projects produce repeated delays, claims, margin reversals, or
  lower customer retention.

The next filings should disclose or allow reconstruction of seasonal staffing,
H-2B allocation, wage recovery, turnover, maintenance renewal, development
backlog, fleet age, capex by purpose, equipment proceeds, insurance claims,
working-capital movements, debt and lease payments, preferred conversion,
buybacks, and cash per maintenance employee.

## Evidence grade

- Control point: **B** — local execution and route density create retention,
  but customers can rebid and scope can shrink.
- Operating economics: **B-** — maintenance is recurring, while labor,
  weather, fuel, and development timing create volatility.
- Cash conversion: **C+** — FY2025 operating cash was strong, but capex used
  most of it and working-capital timing helped.
- Balance-sheet resilience: **C+** — debt, finance leases, receivables
  financing, and preferred claims reduce flexibility.
- Capital allocation: **B-** — fleet investment may preserve capacity, but the
  return on the sharp capex increase needs proof.
- Valuation: **B-** — current value is reasonable only if normalized common
  cash is near the base case after all claims.
