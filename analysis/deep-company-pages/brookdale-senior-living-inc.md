# Brookdale Senior Living Inc. — aging demand through occupancy, care labor, and property cash

Research date: 2026-09-14  
Evidence window: FY2025, Q3 2025, Q4 2025, and Q1 2026  
Ticker: BKD  
Evidence grade: A for reported occupancy, RevPAR, revenue, operating income, and adjusted EBITDA; B for resident affordability, labor productivity, property-level cash, lease burden, maintenance capital, and normalized owner cash.

## Investment question

Brookdale is a direct operating test of the aging thesis. It runs senior-living
communities where residents and families pay for housing, daily support, and
care services. The question is not whether the older population is growing.
The question is whether higher occupancy and resident pricing can become common-
owner cash after staffing, food, property upkeep, leases, debt, compliance,
and the capital needed to keep communities useful.

## What the customer actually buys

The customer buys a place to live with an appropriate level of support. The
service can include independent living, assisted living, memory care, and
other elder-care needs. The economic buyer may be the resident, a family
member, or another decision-maker acting for the resident. That makes trust,
location, safety, staffing, and the ability to increase care as needs change
important parts of the product.

Brookdale ended 2025 with `647` communities in `41` states serving about
`58,000` residents. The physical community is part of the product. This is
not a pure property rent model and not a pure healthcare reimbursement model.
Revenue depends on filled units, resident rates, service intensity, staffing,
and the condition and location of the buildings.

## Control point and operating sequence

The operating sequence is:

`resident or family need -> community selection -> move-in -> occupancy and resident fees -> staffing and care delivery -> retention, rate increases, and cash`

Brookdale controls the local community network, the resident relationship, the
move-in process, service delivery, staffing model, pricing, and portfolio
operations. It does not control household income, family decisions, labor
availability, wage rates, local competition, property taxes, insurance,
interest rates, or the resident’s changing care needs.

The valuable relationship is local and recurring, but the burden is also local.
An occupied unit produces better fixed-cost absorption, yet a fuller community
requires adequate staff, food, supplies, maintenance, compliance, and resident
care. Occupancy is therefore a necessary operating measure, not a complete
cash measure.

## Correct operating denominator

The company should be analyzed through a linked set of denominators:

- weighted average occupancy and same-community occupancy;
- RevPAR, or revenue per available resident unit;
- occupied units by community type and care intensity;
- resident fees per occupied unit and move-in/move-out activity;
- labor hours, wage rates, agency usage, and staffing coverage;
- community-level margin after food, labor, supplies, and local operating cost;
- property-level cash after maintenance and renovation capital;
- lease-adjusted obligations where Brookdale operates leased communities;
- interest, maturities, liquidity, and required debt service;
- corporate overhead, acquisition or disposition cost, and diluted shares.

Revenue growth can come from occupancy, pricing, care mix, acquisitions, or
portfolio changes. RevPAR can improve while labor or maintenance costs rise
faster. Adjusted EBITDA can improve while property cash remains weak if
renovation, lease, interest, or working-capital requirements are not included.

## Three-year cash and capital bridge

The filed cash-flow statements show why Brookdale cannot be evaluated through
occupancy or adjusted EBITDA alone:

| Fiscal year | Operating cash flow | Capital expenditures | Asset acquisitions | OCF less listed capital and acquisitions |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2023 | `$162.9M` | `$233.2M` | `$0.6M` | `$(70.9M)` |
| 2024 | `$166.2M` | `$201.3M` | `$108.4M` | `$(143.5M)` |
| 2025 | `$218.0M` | `$201.5M` | `$311.0M` | `$(294.5M)` |

This is a screening bridge, not a final owner-cash measure. Capital
expenditures include property investment whose maintenance and development
components need to be separated; acquisitions are growth spending rather than
maintenance. The result still changes the interpretation of the recovery:
operating cash improved in 2025, but the combination of property spending and
asset acquisitions exceeded operating cash. The company also reported a
continuing-operations net loss of about `$263.5M` in 2025. A higher occupancy
rate therefore has not yet demonstrated self-funded common-owner economics.

The next filing should reconcile the bridge to Brookdale's non-development
capital measure, cash facility lease payments, financing-lease obligations,
lease-adjusted debt service, and community-level cash. It should also show
whether acquisitions improve resident fees and community margin after the
additional labor, renovation, integration, and financing burden.

## Occupancy, pricing, and care intensity

Brookdale’s recent evidence supports a recovery in the basic operating unit:
more occupied units and higher revenue per available unit. That is meaningful
because empty units leave community labor and property costs in place while
producing no resident revenue.

The next question is the source of the improvement. A clean improvement would
show higher same-community occupancy, stable or improving resident retention,
rate realization, and labor cost per occupied unit. A weaker improvement would
come mainly from rate increases that reduce affordability, a temporary move-in
push, favorable care mix, or a comparison against a depressed prior period.

Care intensity can increase revenue and cost at the same time. Memory care or
assisted-living residents may support higher fees, but they also require more
staffing, training, supervision, supplies, and quality controls. The investor
should not treat a higher average fee as pure pricing power until the related
care labor and service burden is shown.

## Labor and service quality

Labor is the central operating constraint. Brookdale needs enough employees at
each community, with the right skills and coverage, to deliver promised care.
The relevant tests are wage inflation, agency labor, open positions, overtime,
turnover, training, staffing coverage, incident rates, and resident or family
complaints where disclosed.

Cost reduction that lowers staffing or service quality can improve a quarter’s
margin and weaken the franchise later through lower occupancy, regulatory
attention, litigation, poor reviews, or higher turnover. Service quality is
therefore an economic variable, not a separate social footnote.

## Property, leases, and capital burden

The community is both an operating location and a capital asset. Brookdale’s
owner-cash calculation must charge maintenance, renovations, technology,
life-safety work, insurance, property taxes, and lease obligations against
community-level earnings. A property-light presentation can transfer burden to
landlords without removing it from the operating relationship: rent still has
to be paid and weak community economics can limit renewal or expansion.

The required capital is not only new-community growth capital. Existing
communities must remain safe, accessible, attractive, and compliant. If
maintenance is deferred, reported cash may temporarily look better while the
economic condition of the portfolio deteriorates.

## Earnings quality and financial-engineering tests

### 1. Occupancy is not owner cash

Occupancy improves fixed-cost absorption, but the investor must follow each
incremental occupied unit through staffing, food, supplies, utilities,
maintenance, marketing, and collection. The clean test is incremental resident
revenue less incremental community cost and required property capital.

### 2. Adjusted EBITDA is not property cash

FY2025 adjusted EBITDA was `$450.3M`, while continuing-operations net loss was
`$(263.5M)`. The difference may contain depreciation, interest, impairment,
lease, restructuring, or other items that are not all equal. The investor must
identify which costs are non-cash, which recur, which represent asset
consumption, and which sit ahead of common owners.

### 3. RevPAR can hide affordability and mix effects

RevPAR growth can reflect price, occupancy, care mix, or all three. If pricing
outpaces resident affordability, move-ins or retention may weaken later. If
care mix rises, the company may carry higher labor and quality obligations.

### 4. Portfolio actions can change the denominator

Community sales, leases, acquisitions, and redevelopment can make reported
growth look better or worse without changing the economics of the continuing
portfolio. Same-community metrics, community count, occupied units, and cash
from operations should be reconciled before capitalizing growth.

### 5. Losses and impairments must be treated as information

The FY2025 continuing-operations loss shows that the asset and liability base
still matters even during an operating recovery. An impairment is not a cash
charge in the current period, but it records that prior capital allocation or
earning expectations were too high. Repeated impairments would weaken the case
that adjusted EBITDA represents durable earning power.

## Lyn Alden lens: demographics, labor, and liquidity

The demographic force is favorable: a large and aging population increases the
need for senior housing and support. That force does not determine the timing
or quality of cash. Brookdale remains exposed to household wealth, housing
transactions, interest rates, wage liquidity, insurance, property financing,
and the availability of workers.

The liquidity test is whether communities can fund staffing, upkeep, and
renovation through a weaker occupancy period while the corporate balance sheet
handles leases, interest, maturities, and capital commitments. Higher rates can
raise financing cost and reduce housing turnover, making it harder for families
to fund a move even if the underlying care need is rising.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers and lifecycle

The bullish narrative is that aging households, occupancy recovery, and pricing
discipline can turn Brookdale’s large community base into a higher-margin,
cash-generating senior-living platform.

That narrative requires measurable assumptions:

- normalized occupancy by community type;
- resident fees per occupied unit and sustainable rate increases;
- labor cost and staffing coverage per occupied unit;
- community-level margin after care intensity and service quality;
- maintenance and renovation capital per unit;
- lease-adjusted operating cost and property obligations;
- interest, debt maturities, liquidity, and refinancing cost;
- portfolio additions, dispositions, and acquisition returns;
- taxes, corporate overhead, diluted shares, and common-owner cash.

Brookdale is in an operating-recovery phase, not a mature steady-state phase.
The key valuation mistake would be to apply a mature multiple to peak occupancy
and peak margin before proving that the portfolio can fund the buildings,
labor, leases, and balance sheet through a full cycle.

### Valuation screen

The September 14, 2026 market snapshot was approximately `$2.905B` of equity
value and `$11.91` per share. FY2025 operating cash flow less capital
expenditures was approximately `$16.5M` before asset acquisitions, lease
claims, financing, and dilution. The market therefore valued the company at
roughly `176x` that pre-acquisition cash screen. This is not a price target and
the ratio is not a complete owner-cash multiple; it shows how much improvement
the market requires before Brookdale can be valued as a durable cash-generating
operator.

The reverse-valuation work should vary occupancy, resident rates, care mix,
labor cost, maintenance capital, lease payments, acquisition cadence, debt
service, and diluted shares. A higher adjusted EBITDA number cannot support a
large equity value unless it becomes cash after property spending and the
company stops relying on acquisitions or financing to expand the claim.

The scenario register uses the following deliberately demanding screen:

| Case | Normalized common owner cash | Equity cash multiple | Implied equity value | Required condition |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Bear | `$25M` | `20x` | `$500M` | Occupancy recovery slows and property, lease, labor, and debt claims keep cash near the recent pre-acquisition level |
| Base | `$100M` | `25x` | `$2.5B` | Occupancy and rates hold, acquisitions moderate, maintenance is funded, and community cash becomes meaningfully self-funded |
| Bull | `$200M` | `30x` | `$6.0B` | Mature communities produce strong cash after labor and property spending, with high-return growth and limited financing dependence |

The current market value is above the base screen and below the bull screen.
That gap is the investment question, not a forecast: Brookdale must move from
an approximately `$16.5M` FY2025 cash-after-capex screen before acquisitions to
a much larger normalized common-cash result without weakening care quality or
the balance sheet.

## Management incentives and thesis breakers

Management has reasons to emphasize occupancy, RevPAR, adjusted EBITDA, and
community-level improvement because these measures show operating progress and
can support refinancing, capital allocation, and valuation. Those measures are
useful, but they need a bridge to GAAP earnings, operating cash flow, property
capital, debt, leases, and diluted common-owner value.

The thesis weakens if occupancy stops improving while rate increases continue;
labor cost per occupied unit rises faster than resident fees; agency labor,
turnover, or quality incidents increase; adjusted EBITDA grows without similar
cash conversion; maintenance and renovation capital remain elevated; lease or
interest obligations absorb the recovery; resident affordability weakens;
portfolio changes obscure same-community results; or losses and impairments
return despite better reported occupancy.

## Next-filing tests

The next quarterly filing and supplemental should answer:

- Did weighted average and same-community occupancy improve, hold, or reverse?
- Did RevPAR growth come from occupancy, rates, care mix, or portfolio change?
- What happened to labor cost, agency usage, turnover, staffing coverage, and
  service-quality indicators?
- Did incremental resident revenue produce incremental community margin and
  operating cash?
- What were operating cash flow, maintenance capital, renovation capital, and
  free cash flow after required property spending?
- Did debt, leases, maturities, liquidity, or refinancing needs change?
- Did resident receivables or bad debt increase as rates rose?
- Were communities sold, acquired, leased, or impaired in a way that changes
  the continuing-portfolio denominator?
