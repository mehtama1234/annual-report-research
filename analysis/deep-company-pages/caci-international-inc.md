# CACI International Inc. — mission systems, backlog conversion, and acquisition-funded defense technology

Date baseline: 2026-09-14  
Primary evidence: [CACI company packet](../../extracted/technology/information-technology-services/caci-international-inc/company-packet.md), [CACI FY2026 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/16058/000162828026054195/caci-20260630.htm), and [CACI source ledger](../../extracted/technology/information-technology-services/caci-international-inc/source-ledger.md)

## Investment question

CACI provides technology, engineering, intelligence, electronic-warfare,
secure-network, space-sensing, and information services primarily to the U.S.
government. It is trying to move toward higher-value mission systems and
software-defined capabilities while retaining a large contract-execution base.

The investment question is whether CACI's backlog and mission proximity create
durable owner cash after cleared labor, contract-performance risk, working
capital, debt, acquisitions, stock compensation, and federal budget or
procurement changes. The business can be strategically important and still
produce weak common-owner returns if acquisitions absorb the cash generated by
the existing contract base.

## What CACI controls

The operating sequence is:

`national-security requirement -> funded program or contract vehicle -> cleared technical labor and mission systems -> acceptance and billing -> collection -> renewal, recompete, or follow-on award -> cash`

CACI controls specialized program knowledge, cleared labor, technical
integration, software-defined mission tools, and proximity to government
customers. The government controls appropriations, requirements, contract
awards, modifications, protests, and payment timing. Prime contractors and
subcontractors can also affect collection and delivery.

The 2026 filing makes customer concentration clear: federal government
contracts represented 95.6% of revenue, the Department of War represented
53.6%, and the Intelligence Community represented 24.6%. This is not ordinary
enterprise software diversification. It is a government-funded mission
execution business with strong demand visibility and concentrated policy risk.

## Definitions

- **Backlog** is the value of remaining contractual work plus certain
  unexercised options. It is visibility, not profit and not cash collected.
- **Funded backlog** is the portion with current funding authorization. It is a
  stronger near-term indicator than total backlog, but it still requires labor,
  execution, billing, and collection.
- **Book-to-bill** compares contract awards with revenue. A ratio above one can
  increase future visibility, but it does not prove that awards earn an
  adequate return.
- **Cost-to-cost revenue recognition** recognizes much contract revenue over
  time using costs incurred relative to estimated total costs. A change in
  estimated costs can change current revenue and profit, so estimate revisions
  and loss provisions must be monitored.
- **Owner cash** here means operating cash after capital expenditures, before
  acquisitions and discretionary capital returns. Acquisitions are shown
  separately because they are central to CACI's growth strategy.

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| FY2024 | `$497.3M` | `$63.7M` | `$433.6M` | `$90.2M` |

The recurring-capital residual improved, but common-owner cash after the
company's chosen growth program was negative in both FY2025 and FY2026. The
question is not whether acquisitions can raise revenue. It is whether acquired
technical capability produces incremental cash returns above the cost of debt,
integration, dilution, and execution risk.

FY2026 operating cash also benefited from a `$145.4M` deferred-tax adjustment,
stronger collections and other working-capital changes, and a domestic R&D tax
deduction described by management. Those items are not automatically improper,
but they require normalization before treating the full increase as recurring
operating productivity.

## Earnings quality and financial-engineering risks

### Backlog is not a margin guarantee

Total backlog increased only 1.9% from FY2025 to FY2026, while funded backlog
rose 28.6%. Funded backlog is better near-term evidence, but it still must be
staffed and executed. Review award type, contract mix, fixed-price exposure,
cost-to-complete estimates, unbilled receivables, modifications, options,
protests, cancellations, and the conversion from funded backlog to collected
cash.

### Cost-to-cost recognition creates estimate sensitivity

For contracts recognized over time using a cost-to-cost method, revenue and
profit depend on estimated total costs. If expected costs rise, profit can be
reduced or a loss can be recognized. If estimates are too optimistic, current
profit may look stronger than eventual cash economics. The key filing tests are
contract-loss provisions, favorable and unfavorable estimate changes, margins
by contract type, and whether accounts receivable and contract assets rise
faster than revenue.

### Acquisition growth can consume the economic return

CACI spent `$1.696B` on acquisitions in FY2025 and `$2.636B` in FY2026. ARKA
expanded space sensing, imaging, geospatial intelligence, and AI capabilities,
but the acquisition also added transaction cost, integration work, debt, and
amortization. A technology thesis is only validated if acquired revenue,
retention, cross-selling, program awards, margins, and cash returns are
disclosed separately enough to evaluate the purchase price.

### Adjusted earnings exclude recurring people and acquisition costs

CACI recorded `$79.5M` of stock compensation in FY2026, versus `$60.2M` in
FY2025. Amortization was `$168.3M`, up from `$125.0M`, reflecting the acquired
asset base. These are not necessarily accounting errors, but they are real
economic costs of retaining labor and buying capabilities. Track cash per
diluted share after stock compensation, debt service, and acquisition spending.

### Receivables and government payment timing matter

Accounts receivable consumed `$199.0M` of FY2026 operating cash and `$269.2M`
in FY2025. The filing warns that delayed payment by the government or prime
contractors can affect liquidity. A backlog can remain intact while collection
slows. Compare billed receivables, unbilled receivables, contract assets,
payment terms, days sales outstanding, and subcontractor exposure.

## Balance-sheet resilience and capital requirements

At June 30, 2026, CACI had `$191.8M` of cash and `$4.931B` of principal debt.
The company paid `$213.6M` of interest in FY2026 and had revolving-facility and
term-loan balances of `$660M` and `$1.234B`, with additional notes and debt
instruments. Principal maturities extend beyond 2030, but the capital structure
places a meaningful claim on cash generation.

The balance sheet also carries about `$483M` of discounted operating lease
liabilities. Cleared labor, technical facilities, secure infrastructure, and
program-specific investments are part of serving the customer even when they
are not classified as traditional capital expenditures.

## Lyn Alden lens

CACI benefits from national-security demand that is less sensitive to ordinary
consumer liquidity. Geopolitical tension, electronic warfare, cyber risk,
space sensing, and secure communications can keep federal priorities funded.
But government spending is not automatically stable. Appropriations, budget
resolutions, procurement reform, program cancellations, contractor protests,
and political reprioritization can delay awards and collections.

The company has liquidity sensitivity through debt and acquisition financing.
Higher rates raise the burden of the acquisition strategy. Strong demand does
not eliminate the need to fund cleared personnel, working capital, program
execution, and debt service before the government pays.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers

The narrative becomes:

`national-security priority × funded programs × award share × qualified labor and technology capacity -> revenue -> direct labor, subcontractors, program execution, overhead, debt, taxes, and acquisition investment -> owner cash`

The valuation should separately model:

1. organic revenue and margin from the existing contract base;
2. funded backlog conversion and book-to-bill quality;
3. fixed-price and cost-to-cost contract risk;
4. ARKA and other acquisition returns;
5. cleared-labor availability and compensation;
6. debt, interest, and refinancing capacity; and
7. stock compensation, dilution, and common-owner cash after acquisitions.

At the September 14, 2026 market snapshot, CACI's equity value was about
`$13.8B`. That is roughly 17.6 times FY2026 recurring-capital residual, before
subtracting debt or adding acquisition spending. The market is valuing durable
mission demand and technical differentiation, but the valuation requires
acquisitions to earn high returns while leverage remains controlled.

## Management incentives and thesis breakers

Management is incentivized to emphasize revenue growth, contract awards,
backlog, funded backlog, book-to-bill, adjusted EBITDA, adjusted EPS, and free
cash flow. Pair each with organic growth, contract margin, estimate changes,
receivables, debt, acquisition return, SBC, and diluted share count.

The thesis breaks if:

- backlog grows without funded conversion, staffing, billing, or collection;
- contract awards rise but margins deteriorate through fixed-price cost growth;
- ARKA and other acquisitions add revenue without incremental cash returns;
- accounts receivable and contract assets consume more cash than revenue growth
  produces;
- debt and interest grow faster than recurring-capital residual;
- cleared-labor shortages delay delivery or force uneconomic subcontracting;
- federal priorities, appropriations, or contract protests reduce program volume;
  or
- adjusted EPS rises while cash per diluted share falls after SBC and M&A.

## Next-filing evidence ladder

| Question | Confirming evidence | Falsifying evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Is backlog high quality? | Funded backlog converts into revenue, margin, billings, and cash without estimate losses | Backlog grows through options or awards while funded work, margins, or collections weaken |
| Is the mission mix improving? | Technology and expertise revenue grows organically with stable or improving margins | Growth depends on acquired scope, low-margin labor, or government timing |
| Did ARKA earn its cost? | Acquired capabilities produce awards, retained staff, cross-sell, margin, and cash returns | Integration charges, amortization, leverage, or dilution rise without incremental owner cash |
| Is cash repeatable? | OCF remains strong after tax timing, receivables, capex, interest, and working-capital normalization | Collections or tax benefits explain most of the improvement |
| Is leverage manageable? | Debt declines while the company funds technical investment and maintains contract flexibility | New acquisitions require additional debt before prior returns are proven |
| Is the government concentration acceptable? | Funded demand is diversified across missions and agencies | A single department, program, or appropriation drives the majority of growth |

## Bottom line

CACI controls a valuable mission-execution relationship where cleared talent,
technical knowledge, secure systems, and government proximity matter. The
business has stronger demand visibility than ordinary consulting because
backlog and funded programs can extend across years.

The main risk is that visibility is mistaken for owner cash. CACI's 2026
operating cash improved, but acquisitions consumed far more than the
recurring-capital residual, debt and interest increased, and receivables used
substantial cash. The durable thesis requires organic contract economics,
qualified labor, clean backlog conversion, high-return acquisitions, declining
leverage, and rising cash per diluted share.
