# The Campbell's Company: pantry resilience, snack weakness, and the cost of value

## What the company does

The Campbell's Company sells packaged meals, soups, sauces, beverages, and
snacks through retailers and foodservice channels. Its portfolio includes
Campbell's, Rao's, Swanson, Prego, Pace, Goldfish, Pepperidge Farm, and other
brands.

The company controls familiar products and the consumer decision to stock a
pantry or prepare a meal at home. It does not control the entire economics of
that decision. Retailers control shelf access and promotion, households can
switch to private label or cheaper alternatives, and the company must fund
ingredients, packaging, plants, freight, advertising, innovation, and working
capital before the owner receives cash.

The central question is whether the recurring need for affordable meals creates
durable cash after those costs, and whether the stronger pantry businesses can
offset weakness in snacks without hiding the difference inside a consolidated
portfolio.

## FY2025 and FY2026 starting point

| Measure | FY2025 | FY2026 Q3 / outlook | What it says |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Net sales | $10.253B | Q3 sales $2.366B, down 4% | The revenue base is large but not expanding |
| Adjusted EBIT | $1.487B | Q3 $274M, down 24% | Margin is exposed to input and volume pressure |
| Adjusted EPS | $2.97 | FY2026 guidance $2.15–$2.25 | Per-share earnings are being repaired, not compounded |
| Organic sales | Down 1% in FY2025 | FY2026 guidance down 2% to 1% | Management expects another declining year |
| FY2025 operating cash flow | $1.131B | FY2026 year-to-date Q3 $839M | Cash generation remains material but must fund the repair |

The fiscal 2025 annual materials formally changed the name from Campbell Soup
Company to The Campbell's Company. The change reflects a broader portfolio,
but the economics remain divided between Meals & Beverages and Snacks.

The annual result showed organic sales down 1%. Management continued to present
Rao's, Campbell's, Swanson, Prego, Pace, Goldfish, and Pepperidge Farm as
leadership brands, while the recent quarters showed why brand familiarity is
not enough to guarantee growth.

## How activity becomes cash

The cash chain is:

`household meal or snack decision -> retailer placement and promotion -> shipment -> gross profit after ingredients and packaging -> plant, freight, marketing, and overhead costs -> operating cash -> maintenance and brand investment -> shareholder cash`

The steps need to be separated.

- A pantry product can have recurring use but still lose volume if the price gap
  to private label becomes too large.
- A retailer order can raise shipments without proving consumer takeaway.
- Price and mix can protect sales while reducing household penetration.
- A snack brand can carry attractive margins but be more sensitive to category
  behavior and discretionary trade-down than a meal component.
- Cost savings can protect adjusted EBIT while the business underfunds the
  product, advertising, quality, or capacity required for future demand.

The relevant measures are organic volume, in-market consumption, household
penetration, market share, price, mix, trade spending, gross margin, segment
profit, inventory, operating cash, capital spending, restructuring cash, debt,
dividends, repurchases, stock compensation, and diluted shares.

## What the recent quarters show

### Q1 FY2026: pantry demand remains visible

Net sales declined 3% and organic net sales declined 1%. Adjusted EBIT fell 11%
to $383M, adjusted EPS was $0.77, and operating cash flow was $224M.

Management said consumers remained intentional in their shopping. At-home
cooking continued to support Meals & Beverages, while Snacks faced category
softness.

This is the cleanest first separation in the quarter chain: a household can
still need affordable cooking ingredients and prepared meals even when snack
frequency weakens. The consolidated result should not be read as a single
consumer signal.

### Q2 FY2026: Rao's and pantry strength do not erase the split

Net sales declined 5% to $2.564B and organic net sales declined 3%. Adjusted
EBIT fell 24% to $282M, adjusted EPS was $0.51, and year-to-date operating cash
flow reached $740M.

Meals & Beverages continued to show in-market consumption growth. Rao's passed
$1B of trailing-twelve-month net sales. Snacks remained weaker, and storm
disruptions affected shipments.

The result supports a narrower conclusion than “the portfolio is resilient.”
The pantry and sauce businesses may be retaining relevance, while snack
weakness, shipment disruption, inflation, and tariffs still reduce consolidated
profit.

### Q3 FY2026: the pressure is still active

Net sales declined 4% to $2.366B on both a reported and organic basis. Adjusted
EBIT declined 24% to $274M, adjusted EPS was $0.50, and year-to-date operating
cash flow reached $839M.

Management reaffirmed fiscal 2026 guidance of organic net sales down 2% to 1%,
adjusted EBIT down 20% to 17%, and adjusted EPS of $2.15 to $2.25.

The current evidence therefore supports pantry demand, not a completed earnings
recovery. A business can sell more meals at home while still reporting weaker
profit because input costs, tariffs, trade investment, volume loss in snacks,
and operating leverage are heavier than the demand benefit.

## Earnings quality and financial-engineering questions

### 1. Is at-home cooking becoming company volume or only a category statement?

Management's at-home-cooking message is plausible, but it must be translated
into in-market consumption, household penetration, distribution, repeat use,
and organic volume. If the category grows while Campbell's volume declines,
the company is not capturing the demand.

Track Meals & Beverages volume and share separately from Snacks. Do not let a
strong Rao's or soup result conceal weakness in the rest of the portfolio.

### 2. Does price protect cash or destroy reach?

Price can cover ingredient and packaging inflation in the short term. It can
also increase the gap to private label and cause households to buy less or
switch brands.

The next filing should show price, volume, mix, promotional intensity, trade
spending, gross margin, and household penetration together. A sales result that
depends on price while volume and share weaken is not a clean recovery.

### 3. Is the snacks problem cyclical or structural?

Snacks may recover with better innovation, distribution, and category demand.
They may also face a lasting consumer shift toward affordability, different
eating occasions, or competing brands.

The test is repeated volume, share, gross margin, innovation returns, and
advertising efficiency across several quarters. A single better shipment month
does not settle the category question.

### 4. Are storm, tariff, and inflation explanations hiding operating weakness?

Storms and tariffs are real costs, but they should be separated from base
volume, retailer ordering, manufacturing productivity, and product mix.

The company should bridge reported change into price, volume, mix, currency,
input cost, freight, trade, productivity, and one-time disruption. Repeated
“one-time” costs belong in the owner-cash analysis if the operating system keeps
producing them.

### 5. Does portfolio breadth improve returns?

The name change and portfolio language describe a broader company, but breadth
does not automatically create value. Rao's, snacks, meals, sauces, and
foodservice have different growth, margin, input, and capital requirements.

Segment growth should be judged after advertising, innovation, capacity,
working capital, acquisitions, and integration costs. A business should not
receive credit for a higher-growth category until it shows that the category
earns an adequate return on the capital required to support it.

## Lyn Alden lens: macro, liquidity, and durability

Campbell sells products with frequent household use, which provides a more
stable base than discretionary home goods. That stability is not the same as
pricing power. In a tight household budget, consumers can reduce quantity,
switch brands, delay a purchase, or choose a retailer label.

The stress sequence is:

`household budget pressure -> trade-down and promotion dependence -> lower volume or gross margin -> more brand and value investment -> pressure on operating cash`

Input costs, packaging, freight, labor, tariffs, interest rates, and retailer
power determine how much of the demand reaches the bottom line. Liquidity
means more than paying debt and dividends. It means preserving enough cash to
fund product quality, advertising, innovation, plant maintenance, and working
capital while the company repairs the portfolio.

The durable part is the routine need for convenient meals and trusted products.
The fragile part is the cost of remaining a preferred brand when the household
can obtain an acceptable substitute for less.

## Damodaran lens: narrative-to-numbers, reinvestment, and lifecycle

Management's narrative is that a broader meals-and-snacks portfolio, strong
brands, at-home cooking, Rao's growth, productivity, and improved execution can
restore the earnings base.

That narrative requires these links:

1. Meals & Beverages volume and household penetration must remain stronger than
   the category without permanent discounting.
2. Snacks must either regain acceptable volume and margin or be treated as a
   lower-value part of the portfolio rather than assumed to recover.
3. Gross margin must hold after tariffs, ingredients, packaging, freight, and
   trade spending.
4. Productivity must create cash without reducing the brand and product
   investment needed to preserve demand.
5. Operating cash must remain after capital spending, restructuring, interest,
   dividends, stock compensation, and required working capital.
6. Rao's and other growth brands must earn returns above the cost of the
   capacity, advertising, and distribution needed to scale them.

Campbell is a mature repair and allocation case, not a high-growth platform.
The valuation should depend on normalized cash after the portfolio is funded,
not on a temporary adjusted-EBIT improvement or on the existence of familiar
brands.

## Illustrative valuation screen

The local packet provides operating cash flow but does not provide a current
market-price snapshot suitable for a live recommendation. An illustrative
owner-cash screen using normalized cases of `$650M`, `$850M`, and `$1.05B` at
`14x`, `17x`, and `20x` produces implied equity values of `$9.1B`, `$14.45B`,
and `$21.0B`.

These figures are not price targets. They show the expectation burden:

- the bear case assumes continued volume weakness, lower margins, and heavy
  repair spending;
- the base case assumes pantry strength, partial snack repair, and normalized
  cash near the current operating scale;
- the bull case assumes volume recovery, durable brand economics, and better
  cash conversion without a recurring cost reset.

Rebuild the screen from the next annual filing using operating cash flow,
capital spending, working capital, restructuring payments, stock compensation,
debt, dividends, repurchases, and diluted shares.

## Management incentives and thesis breakers

The proxy should be read for the balance between sales, adjusted EBIT, EPS,
free cash flow, market share, productivity, and shareholder returns. An EPS
measure can reward buybacks. An adjusted EBIT measure can exclude recurring
portfolio or integration costs. A sales measure can reward shipments without
proving consumer takeaway.

The thesis weakens if:

- Meals & Beverages volume or share weakens after the at-home-cooking benefit;
- snack recovery requires permanent promotion and still fails to improve share;
- price increases protect reported sales while households leave the brands;
- gross margin remains dependent on favorable input timing or cost exclusions;
- restructuring, integration, and productivity charges continue as recurring
  cash costs;
- inventory, receivables, or retailer incentives grow faster than sales;
- Rao's or other growth brands need capital and advertising that do not earn an
  adequate return;
- dividends continue while debt, diluted shares, or required brand investment
  consume the cash base.

## Next filing tests

1. Meals & Beverages and Snacks volume, price, mix, market share, and household
   penetration.
2. In-market consumption versus shipments and retailer inventory.
3. Rao's, Campbell's, Swanson, Prego, Pace, Goldfish, and Pepperidge Farm
   growth, margin, distribution, and promotional support.
4. Gross-margin bridge for ingredients, packaging, tariffs, freight, labor,
   trade spending, productivity, and mix.
5. Advertising, innovation, quality, maintenance, and capacity spending versus
   restructuring and cost-savings claims.
6. Inventory, receivables, payables, operating cash flow, capital spending,
   restructuring cash, interest, dividends, stock compensation, and diluted
   shares.
7. Evidence that fiscal 2026 guidance is met through volume and cash rather
   than shipment timing, storm comparisons, or adjusted exclusions.

## Bottom line

Campbell's evidence supports a narrower and more useful conclusion than
“staples are defensive.” At-home cooking and pantry products can retain demand
when households are under pressure, but the company still has to defend value,
fund brands, absorb input and tariff costs, and repair a weaker snacks business.

The company may control a durable meal-preparation relationship, but it has not
yet proved that this relationship produces stronger owner cash across the whole
portfolio. The next proof is sustained Meals & Beverages volume and share,
credible snack economics, stable gross profit, and cash conversion after the
repair bill.

**Evidence grade:** Strong for fiscal 2025 and Q1–Q3 FY2026 operating trends,
the Meals & Beverages versus Snacks split, management's guidance, and the
identified input and value pressures; medium for normalized owner cash and the
durability of Rao's and pantry growth; unresolved for full-portfolio volume,
margin recovery, and return on reinvestment.
