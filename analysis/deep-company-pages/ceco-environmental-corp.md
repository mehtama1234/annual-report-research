## Bottom line

CECO controls engineered environmental and process infrastructure: industrial
air, water, thermal, emissions, and energy-transition systems installed inside
plants and utility-adjacent projects. It is a valuable physical compliance and
uptime control point, but the economics are project-industrial rather than
software-like. Backlog and orders create visibility before revenue, margin, and
cash are proven.

The FY2025 filing is a useful forensic contradiction to the packet’s adjusted
free-cash-flow framing. GAAP operating cash flow was only $5.861M against
$11.343M of PP&E spending, a negative $5.482M screening residual, despite
$50.051M of net income and $774.381M of revenue. The 2025 packet’s $9.6M FCF
figure is therefore a non-GAAP or differently defined measure that must be
reconciled before valuation. This is not evidence of misconduct; it is exactly
the denominator question the system is designed to surface.

## How the business works in plain terms

CECO designs and supplies equipment that helps factories, utilities, and other
industrial sites control air emissions, water, heat, and process conditions.
The customer usually buys a system for a specific project. CECO may receive an
order long before it records the full sale and may spend cash on engineering,
materials, labor, and subcontractors before the customer pays.

That creates four separate measurements:

1. **Orders:** new work awarded during the period.
2. **Backlog:** awarded work that has not yet been recorded as revenue.
3. **Revenue and profit:** work recorded under accounting rules as delivery and
   performance occur.
4. **Owner cash:** cash left after working-capital needs and the equipment
   required to keep the business operating.

These measures can move in different directions. A larger backlog can coexist
with weak cash if projects require more inventory, customers pay slowly, work is
delayed, or margins are reduced during execution. The correct question is not
whether backlog grew. It is whether backlog becomes collected cash at an
acceptable return after project costs, integration costs, taxes, interest,
acquisitions, and dilution.

## The cash and earnings questions a new reader should ask

Start with reported net income, then make the following checks:

- Did operating cash flow roughly follow profit, or did receivables, inventory,
  contract assets, and other operating balances absorb the cash?
- After PP&E spending, how much cash remains before acquisitions, debt
  repayment, dividends, repurchases, and employee-share issuance?
- Which adjustments create the difference between GAAP cash flow and adjusted
  free cash flow, and are those costs truly one-time?
- Did the company earn a return on the money invested in acquisitions, or did
  revenue and the addressable-market narrative grow faster than cash profit?

For CECO, these questions lead to a clear provisional conclusion: 2025 revenue
and net income improved, but the reported cash bridge did not yet prove that the
growth was durable owner earnings. The $63.7M divestiture gain, the sharp
working-capital movement, the Thermon transaction, and the gap between GAAP
cash flow and adjusted FCF must be resolved before a high multiple is justified.

## Control point and burden carrier

The economic chain is:

`industrial plant / utility project -> air, water, thermal, and process risk -> engineered system -> compliance, uptime, and capacity -> project revenue and service`

CECO’s value is strongest where customers cannot operate or expand without
meeting emissions, water, thermal, or process requirements. The burden carrier
is project execution: engineering, procurement, customer acceptance, contract
assets, working capital, labor, warranty, commissioning, and the timing gap
between orders and collected cash.

The 2026 Thermon combination increases category relevance and backlog, but it
also raises comparability, integration, purchase-accounting, and reporting-
timing risk. A combined-company order or backlog number is not yet recurring
owner cash.

## Earnings quality and possible financial engineering

The core test is backlog-to-cash conversion. CECO reported strong 2025 orders,
revenue, and adjusted EBITDA, but GAAP OCF fell to $5.9M. Accounts receivable
rose to $172.9M and inventory to $54.0M. The next filing must reconcile order
conversion, contract assets, retainage, customer advances, inventory, gross
margin, and cash collections.

The $63.7M gain on sale of Global Pump Solutions also matters. Divestiture gains
can lift net income while removing productive assets, so valuation should not
capitalize reported earnings without excluding the gain and testing the
remaining portfolio. SBC was $13.1M and goodwill was $288.2M before the
Thermon transaction, giving dilution and acquisition accounting a material
role even before the larger 2026 integration.

Questions to test:

- What percentage of backlog is funded, cancellable, fixed-price, or exposed to
  customer acceptance and change orders?
- Why did revenue and net income rise while OCF collapsed?
- Does adjusted FCF add back recurring transaction, integration, or working-
  capital costs that the owner must actually fund?
- Do Thermon synergies appear in gross profit and cash, or only adjusted EBITDA?
- Are acquisitions creating return on invested capital above the cost of debt
  and equity, or merely expanding the addressable-market narrative?

## Lyn Alden durability lens

CECO benefits from regulation, industrial reshoring, energy demand, data-center
power buildout, semiconductor capacity, and the increasing cost of pollution or
process failure. These drivers can make environmental infrastructure strategic,
but liquidity and project financing still determine when customers order.
Backlog is durable only if customers fund projects and CECO converts them
without margin leakage or balance-sheet stress.

## Damodaran narrative-to-numbers lens

The bullish narrative is that CECO is becoming a diversified environmental
infrastructure platform with secular air, water, power, AI, and reshoring
demand, while Thermon adds scale and synergies. The skeptical narrative is
that the company is a project contractor with lumpy working capital, a growing
acquisition burden, and adjusted metrics that capitalize future margin before
cash has arrived.

For a mechanical screen: bear $15M × 20 = $300M, base $40M × 28 = $1.120B,
and bull $75M × 35 = $2.625B, versus a current equity snapshot of
approximately $2.737B. The bull case still barely approaches the market,
meaning the price requires successful Thermon integration, backlog conversion,
and a large cash-recovery step. These are scenario inputs, not a price target.

## Thesis breakers and next filing tests

The thesis weakens if backlog converts into revenue without cash, fixed-price
projects suffer margin claims, contract assets and receivables grow faster than
sales, Thermon integration costs recur, adjusted FCF remains far above GAAP
OCF, leverage rises, or the data-center/reshoring demand narrative does not
translate into high-return projects. The next filing should capture backlog
quality, contract assets/liabilities, cash collections, project margin,
Thermon purchase accounting, synergies, debt, dilution, warranty, and
maintenance investment.
