# Clean Harbors — hazardous-waste capacity, environmental liabilities, and the adjusted-cash question

Date baseline: 2026-09-13  
Primary filing: [Clean Harbors FY2025 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/822818/000082281826000009/clh-20251231.htm)

## Bottom line

Clean Harbors provides environmental and industrial services across North
America. It treats and disposes of hazardous waste, operates incinerators and
treatment facilities, performs industrial cleaning and emergency response,
handles remediation work, and collects used oil through the Safety-Kleen
system for re-refining and recycling.

The company controls specialized permits, treatment and disposal assets,
technical know-how, response capability, customer relationships, and a
network that can handle difficult waste streams. Those capabilities can create
stronger customer dependence than ordinary route-density waste collection.

The burden is also more serious. Clean Harbors carries environmental closure,
post-closure, and remedial liabilities; product-liability and Superfund
exposure; regulatory and insurance requirements; heavy fixed assets; and the
cost of expanding disposal and treatment capacity.

FY2025 revenue was $6.031B, net income was $391.0M, GAAP operating cash flow
was $866.7M, and additions to property, plant, and equipment were $424.9M.
The company reported adjusted free cash flow of $509.3M, but that measure
added back $43.3M of strategic growth investments and $2.6M of third-party
transaction costs. Before those exclusions, cash after property investment
and asset-sale proceeds was approximately $463.4M. The distinction is central
to valuation.

Current judgment: Clean Harbors appears to have a valuable specialized
environmental infrastructure position, with Environmental Services providing
the stronger growth and margin engine. Safety-Kleen adds collection and
re-refining scale but has weaker recent EBITDA performance. The company merits
analysis as a regulated, asset-heavy environmental network rather than a
generic waste hauler. Its value depends on disposal utilization, pricing,
regulatory demand, environmental liabilities, sustaining versus growth
capital, and whether adjusted FCF excludes investments that common owners
must repeatedly fund.

## What Clean Harbors controls

`hazardous or industrial waste -> collection and response -> permitted treatment, disposal, recycling, or re-refining -> compliance and risk reduction -> recurring customer need`

Clean Harbors controls treatment and disposal capacity, permits, operating
procedures, specialized equipment, response teams, and the ability to manage
multiple stages of an environmental problem. Customers include industrial
companies, manufacturers, transportation businesses, government entities, and
other waste generators.

Customers control waste volumes, production activity, environmental budgets,
contract awards, and the timing of remediation. Regulators control permits,
standards, closure obligations, and the legal cost of noncompliance. Commodity
prices affect the value of re-refined oil and other recovered materials.

The strongest control point is permitted capacity combined with technical
execution. A customer cannot instantly create a compliant incinerator,
treatment facility, or emergency-response organization. That creates value,
but it does not remove operating, regulatory, or liability risk.

after selected current-period investments, not necessarily cash available
after all spending required to build and maintain the platform.

## Two operating engines

### Environmental Services

Environmental Services generated FY2025 direct revenue of approximately
$5.193B and adjusted EBITDA of $1.344B, a 25.9% margin. Revenue increased
3.8% from 2024 and adjusted EBITDA increased 6.0%. The segment includes
technical services, industrial services, field and emergency response,
disposal, recycling, and Safety-Kleen Environmental Services.

This segment benefits from hazardous-waste regulation, industrial activity,
emergency events, remediation, PFAS work, and utilization of treatment and
incineration assets. Its quality depends on price, volume, plant utilization,
permitting, safety, maintenance, and environmental liability estimates.

### Safety-Kleen Sustainability Solutions

Safety-Kleen Sustainability Solutions generated FY2025 direct revenue of
approximately $837M and adjusted EBITDA of $137.5M, a 16.4% margin. Revenue
declined 5.4% and adjusted EBITDA declined 6.5% from 2024. The business
collects used oil and other fluids and produces re-refined and recycled oil and
related materials.

This engine carries collection routes, re-refinery assets, product-quality
requirements, used-oil supply, automotive and manufacturing demand, and
re-refined-product pricing. Recent weakness means the consolidated margin
story should not be attributed equally to both engines.

## Earnings quality and forensic tests

### 1. Adjusted FCF excludes strategic growth capital

Beginning in 2025, management excluded significant one-time growth
investments from adjusted FCF because the investments are expected to produce
future benefits. In FY2025 the exclusion was $43.3M. The analyst should keep
that cash outflow in a full owner-cash calculation until the investment earns
an observable return.

The correct questions are: Is the project required to defend the existing
franchise, or is it discretionary expansion? What capacity does it add? When
does it reach utilization? What pricing and margin does it earn? How much
additional working capital, permitting, maintenance, and environmental
liability does it create?

### 2. Depreciation and environmental accretion are not the whole burden

FY2025 depreciation and amortization was $446.0M, while property additions
were $424.9M. The comparison is informative but not conclusive. Depreciation
reflects historical assets and accounting lives; capex reflects current
replacement, expansion, and project timing. Environmental liabilities may
also require cash over long periods even when the related expense is
recognized through accretion and estimates.

At December 31, 2025, Clean Harbors recorded $230.7M of closure,
post-closure, and remedial liabilities. The company says substantially all
were assumed in acquisitions. A lower liability estimate can increase
reported earnings or reduce future cash charges, so changes in estimates need
to be reconciled with site inspections, expected payment schedules, and
regulatory developments.

### 3. Working capital and collections affect operating cash

Operating cash flow rose in FY2025, but the filing attributes changes to
operating performance, working capital, and lower cash paid for environmental
expenditures. The analyst should separate volume and price growth from
collection timing, inventory and used-oil balances, customer advances, and
the timing of environmental payments.

Cash generation is stronger when disposal volume and pricing produce cash
after normal environmental expenditures, not merely when a liability payment
is delayed or working capital releases cash.

### 4. Acquisitions can import liabilities

Clean Harbors has expanded through acquisitions and has assumed environmental
liabilities at acquired facilities. The economic purchase price includes the
cash paid for the business, assumed closure and remedial obligations, permits,
integration, needed capex, insurance, and any later estimate increase.

An acquisition should be evaluated on cash generated after these obligations,
not on adjusted EBITDA before them. Goodwill and permits should be monitored
for impairment, but an absence of impairment is not proof that the acquisition
earned an adequate return.

### 5. Product and Superfund claims are uncertain liabilities

The FY2025 filing describes approximately 76 Safety-Kleen product-liability
proceedings and involvement as a potentially responsible party at about 132
Superfund sites. Insurance covered the product-liability claims settled or
dismissed during 2025, but future claims may not be fully insured. These are
not accusations of misconduct; they are claims and legal obligations that
must be included in downside analysis.

Track claim counts, self-insured retentions, insurance limits, exclusions,
environmental payment schedules, new sites, reserve changes, and any change in
the probability or timing of remediation.

## Capital requirements and balance sheet

Clean Harbors is not a low-capital service business. It owns and operates
incinerators, treatment facilities, re-refineries, vehicles, containers,
landfills, and response equipment. Property, plant, and equipment was about
$2.541B at December 31, 2025. Capital spending must be divided into sustaining
capacity, compliance and safety, modernization, and growth projects.

The company’s access to specialized capacity can support pricing and
utilization. It also creates fixed-cost and regulatory exposure if volumes or
prices weaken. Debt, interest rates, permits, insurance, surety requirements,
and environmental claims affect liquidity. The next filing should reconcile
net debt, maturities, interest cost, covenant headroom, letters of credit,
asset retirement obligations, and expected capital projects.

## Alden macro and durability lens

Clean Harbors is exposed to North American manufacturing, refining,
transportation, energy, construction, remediation, environmental regulation,
PFAS policy, commodity prices, interest rates, and industrial accidents.

Its durable demand is partly regulatory: customers must handle hazardous
materials, comply with permits, and respond to contamination. That can make
demand less discretionary than ordinary industrial services. The macro risk
is that industrial production falls, emergency work declines, used-oil prices
weaken, or regulation changes the timing and economics of cleanup.

Liquidity must be tested through a period of lower volumes, high maintenance
capex, environmental payments, insurance restrictions, and higher borrowing
costs. The specialized network is valuable only if it can operate safely and
fund its liabilities through that cycle.

## Damodaran narrative-to-numbers lens

The narrative is “Clean Harbors owns scarce environmental infrastructure and
benefits from regulated hazardous-waste demand, industrial activity, and
re-refining.” The model must specify:

1. disposal, incineration, treatment, recycling, and emergency-response
   volume;
2. price per unit, utilization, capacity additions, and customer concentration;
3. Environmental Services versus Safety-Kleen margin and growth;
4. re-refined-oil input and output prices;
5. sustaining capex versus strategic growth capex;
6. environmental payments, closure obligations, insurance, and claims;
7. acquisition returns after assumed liabilities and integration; and
8. normalized cash after debt, interest, and required reinvestment.

Clean Harbors is a mature infrastructure platform with selective growth
projects and a mix shift toward higher-margin Environmental Services. A
higher valuation requires proof that new capacity earns returns above its
full capital and liability burden.

## Valuation screen

The September 11, 2026 market snapshot showed equity value of approximately
$17.097B. The following cases use normalized common owner cash after a full
capital charge, including sustaining capital and a recurring allowance for
growth and environmental obligations. They are screening cases, not price
targets.

| Case | Normalized common owner cash | Equity cash multiple | Implied equity value |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Bear | $400M | 25x | $10.0B |
| Base | $525M | 30x | $15.75B |
| Bull | $675M | 35x | $23.625B |

The current value is above the base screen. It requires sustained
Environmental Services growth, high disposal utilization, successful growth
projects, recovery in Safety-Kleen, stable liabilities, and owner cash above
the conservative post-investment base. The multiple also reflects the value
of permits and network scarcity, but that premium should fall if regulation,
technology, industrial activity, or customer behavior reduces utilization.

## Thesis breakers and next filings

The thesis weakens if:

- adjusted FCF continues to exclude strategic investments that are necessary
  to preserve or expand the platform;
- Environmental Services growth depends on temporary remediation or PFAS
  projects that do not become recurring disposal volume;
- incinerator or treatment utilization falls while fixed costs remain;
- Safety-Kleen’s re-refining spread, collection volume, or product demand
  remains weak;
- environmental liability estimates fall without corresponding reduction in
  expected payments or site risk;
- product-liability, Superfund, insurance, or regulatory costs rise;
- acquisitions add permits and EBITDA but also import underfunded obligations;
  or
- debt and capital spending rise faster than owner cash.

The next filings should disclose capacity utilization, pricing and volume,
segment cash economics, strategic growth investment returns, sustaining versus
growth capex, environmental payment schedules, reserve changes, insurance and
self-insurance, claim counts, debt maturities, acquisition cohorts, goodwill
and permit testing, stock compensation, and capital returns.

## Evidence grade

- Control point: **A-** — permits, specialized assets, and technical execution
  create meaningful customer dependence.
- Operating economics: **B+** — Environmental Services has strong margins,
  while Safety-Kleen and project timing require separate analysis.
- Cash conversion: **B** — operating cash is strong, but adjusted FCF excludes
  strategic growth spending and capex is substantial.
- Balance-sheet resilience: **B** — the platform has scale and liquidity, but
  debt, fixed assets, environmental liabilities, and insurance matter.
- Capital allocation: **B-** — growth investments may create capacity, but
  returns must be measured after liabilities and full capital costs.
- Valuation: **B-** — current value requires durable common cash above the
  conservative post-investment base.
