## What the business earns, and what the accounts require us to separate

CME operates markets where participants trade financial contracts, maintains the systems that process those trades, and sells market data. Its main operating revenue comes from transaction and clearing fees, along with data revenue. Customers use its contracts to manage exposure to prices and interest rates or to take a position on how those prices will change.

A market becomes useful when participants can find other people willing to trade at prices close to those they expect. This is part of what traders mean by **liquidity**. Moving to a competing venue may save a fee but make it harder to complete a large trade at an acceptable price. That gives an established market a practical advantage, although it does not prevent competition or guarantee future trading volume.

The operating economics are unusually clean. 2025 revenue was $6.521 billion,
operating income was $4.230 billion, operating cash flow was $4.277 billion,
and capital expenditures were only $83.5 million. Market-data revenue grew 13%
to $803.1 million, while clearing and transaction fees grew 6% to $5.281
billion. First-half 2026 operating cash flow was $2.207 billion against $45.4
million of property purchases.

But the income statement and balance sheet require special handling. CME’s
2025 balance sheet included $159.7 billion of performance bonds and guaranty
fund contributions, matched by a corresponding liability. Those balances are
not ordinary corporate cash. Investment income includes interest earned on
collateral, while other non-operating expense includes distributions and
related effects. A superficial P/E or cash-flow comparison can therefore
misread both the quality and the cyclicality of earnings.

Our judgment is that the reported fee business produces substantial operating profit while requiring relatively little spending on buildings and equipment. That does not eliminate the cost of technology, market supervision or protecting the clearing system. The conclusion would weaken if trading activity moved elsewhere, fees came under pressure, operating failures damaged trust, or lower interest income exposed how much recent earnings depended on collateral returns. Regulation and distributions to shareholders that exceed sustainable cash generation remain additional risks.

## How trading, clearing and collateral work together

Three terms help explain the business. A **futures contract** sets terms for a purchase or sale at a future date; depending on the contract, settlement can involve cash or delivery. An **option** gives its buyer a right, rather than an obligation, to transact under specified terms. **Clearing** manages the obligations created by trades, including calculating amounts owed and collecting resources to cover potential losses.

CME's clearing operation acts as a central counterparty for cleared trades, standing between the clearing participants. Participants provide collateral, often called margin or performance bonds, to support their obligations. Guaranty fund contributions provide another resource under the clearing system's rules. These resources have a different purpose from cash earned through corporate fees: they support the market's obligations and cannot simply be treated as money for shareholders.

It offers futures, options, cash and OTC products across interest rates,
equity indexes, FX, agricultural commodities, energy, and metals. It also
operates BrokerTec cash/repo fixed-income trading, EBS spot/OTC FX, and a
central counterparty clearing operation.

An institution may use a particular contract because it closely matches the price exposure it wants to manage, because other institutions trade it actively, and because its own systems already process that contract. A competing product must be attractive enough to justify changing those arrangements. The evidence to watch is actual trading activity and fees, not simply the number of products CME offers.

The same role creates obligations. CME must keep trading and settlement systems reliable, calculate collateral requirements, monitor activity and meet regulatory requirements. Low spending on physical assets is therefore not a complete measure of the risk. A serious failure could affect participants across the market and damage the trust on which future fee income depends.

lender, insurer, or asset manager. It is not risk-free: high fixed-cost
economics magnify a volume decline, and regulatory/technology spending cannot
be cut in proportion to a weak quarter.

## Earnings quality and financial-engineering tests

### 1. Client collateral is not corporate liquidity

At December 31, 2025, CME reported $159.656B of performance bonds and guaranty
fund contributions as restricted cash and equivalents, with the same amount in
liabilities. These balances exist to support clearing obligations and cannot
be used to pay ordinary corporate liabilities.

The balance sheet therefore has two layers:

`corporate assets and liabilities -> exchange owner economics`

`client collateral and guaranty funds -> clearing-system obligations and risk controls`

The second layer may generate investment income for CME and can affect net
income, but it is not distributable cash in the same way as trading and data
fees. The proper valuation treatment excludes restricted collateral from
unrestricted cash and separately models the net interest benefit, required
collateral investment, and stress/liquidity obligations.

### 2. Interest on collateral is a real but cyclical earnings component

2025 investment income was $5.737B, while other non-operating expense was
$4.834B. The annual filing explains that interest earned on performance bonds
and guaranty fund contributions accrues to CME, and related distributions are
recognized in other non-operating income/expense. Higher short-term rates and
larger collateral balances can therefore lift earnings, while lower rates,
lower margin balances, or altered distribution economics can reverse the
benefit.

This is not an accounting shenanigan; it is an economic denominator problem.
An analyst who capitalizes all net income as if it were a pure exchange toll
will overstate the durability of earnings if the current interest component is
above a through-cycle rate. An analyst who removes all investment income may
understate the value of the clearing franchise if CME consistently earns a
legitimate spread on collateral under its risk controls.

Required tests:

- Reconcile collateral balances, average rates, investment income, and
  distributions each quarter.
- Estimate earnings at low, normal, and high short-term rates.
- Determine which collateral balances are structurally required and which are
  volatile with client positions and market stress.
- Keep restricted cash out of corporate net-cash valuation.

### 3. Volume and rate per contract are the operating bridge

CME discloses contract volume and average rate per contract, which allows a
cleaner forensic decomposition than headline revenue alone. In 2025, volume
grew 6% and average rate per contract grew 1%. Rate per contract is affected
by product mix, member/non-member mix, trading venue, and volume-based
incentives.

The key tests are:

- whether pricing power is real or purchased through rebates and incentives;
- whether growth is concentrated in lower-rate products;
- whether new contracts deepen liquidity or cannibalize legacy contracts;
- whether market share and open interest remain durable after volatility
  normalizes; and
- whether clearing and data fees rise without impairing client participation.

A higher rate per contract is not automatically pricing power. It may be mix,
temporary volatility, or a change in customer composition.

### 4. Market-data revenue is recurring but not invulnerable

Market-data services are generally recognized monthly as customers consume the
service, while some annual licenses begin as contract liabilities and are
recognized over the license term. This is a comparatively clean revenue model.

The economic risks are customer budget pressure, regulatory limits on data
fees, third-party distributor dependence, unauthorized data use, and the
possibility that artificial intelligence or alternative data products change
how market participants consume benchmark information. The two largest market
data resellers represented approximately 30% of market-data revenue in 2025,
although CME believes most end users could migrate to another reseller if one
failed.

### 5. Intangible amortization and stock compensation remain real claims

2025 included $223.4M of purchased-intangible amortization, primarily tied to
acquired customer relationships and market-data/trading assets. The charge is
non-cash today, but the acquired assets required capital and may require future
replacement or renewed investment.

Stock compensation was $94.8M in 2025 and $46.6M in the first half of 2026.
It is added back in OCF but creates dilution or future repurchase requirements.
The company’s 2025 share count declined modestly after a $266.1M repurchase,
but the economic cost should be measured against the value of the shares
issued and the price paid for repurchases.

## Regulatory, technology, and balance-sheet resilience

CME’s moat is partly a regulatory and market-structure asset. Exchange,
clearing, benchmark, data, and market-access privileges create barriers to
entry, but regulators can alter fees, interoperability, capital requirements,
position limits, clearing mandates, market access, and competition policy.

The technology system is a core production asset. CME must scale capacity,
latency, resilience, cyber defenses, disaster recovery, surveillance, and
connectivity as electronic volume rises. A technology outage or clearing
failure could create direct costs and long-term trust damage even if it does
not immediately appear in depreciation.

At year-end 2025, corporate cash and cash equivalents were $4.417B, marketable
securities were $125M, and long-term debt was $3.422B. The company had a $2.3B
revolving credit facility and no borrowings under it at the reporting date.
This is a conservative corporate liquidity position, but the clearing system
has separate obligations that cannot be judged by corporate net debt alone.

Through Lyn Alden’s durability/liquidity lens:

- CME benefits from volatility, hedging demand, benchmark dependence, and
  institutional risk transfer rather than only from economic expansion.
- It is less exposed to physical inflation and labor scarcity than Quanta or
  WESCO, but more exposed to rates, market structure, liquidity migration, and
  regulatory regime changes.
- High operating margins and low capex create strong self-funding capacity.
- The main macro normalization risk is not insolvency; it is a lower earnings
  and capital-return regime after volatility and collateral interest recede.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers

### Narrative

The bullish narrative is that CME is a regulated global utility for risk
transfer. More institutions need to hedge rates, inflation, FX, energy,
commodities, equities, and digital assets; more products migrate to central
clearing; and market-data and analytics monetize the benchmark ecosystem.

### Operating translation

The narrative must become measurable in:

- contract volume by asset class and venue;
- average rate per contract after incentives and mix;
- market-data users, devices, pricing, and reseller concentration;
- clearing volumes, collateral balances, margin efficiency, and risk controls;
- technology and regulatory expense as a percentage of revenue;
- collateral interest income under normalized rates;
- capital expenditures and system-resilience investment;
- net retention of liquidity and market share; and
- cash returned after stock compensation and required capital.

### Lifecycle and reinvestment

CME is a mature, high-return platform with reinvestment focused on technology,
capacity, clearing resilience, regulatory infrastructure, product innovation,
and selective acquisitions or partnerships. It does not need large physical
working capital, but its moat requires continuous spending that cannot be
treated as optional simply because capex is small relative to revenue.

The lifecycle question is whether new products expand the network or dilute
returns. Treasury clearing, 24/7 crypto trading, prediction markets, and data
products may enlarge the addressable market, but product launch does not prove
liquidity, pricing power, or regulatory durability.

### Valuation expectations

The market should value CME on normalized owner cash with a separate scenario
for collateral interest and volume. A first-pass cash screen uses 2025 OCF less
property and venture investment of $4.183B. At the September 11, 2026 market
snapshot of approximately $99.5B equity value, that is about 23.8x the listed
residual before normalizing stock compensation and collateral interest.

| Case | Normalized owner-cash view | What must be true |
|---|---|---|
| Bear | $3.5B | Volume and collateral rates normalize, data growth slows, and fixed costs remain sticky |
| Base | $4.2B | Market-data growth and durable liquidity offset normal volatility cycles |
| Bull | $4.8B | Clearing migration, product breadth, and data pricing expand the through-cycle earnings base |

These are scenario inputs, not a target price. The main valuation error would
be paying a peak-volatility multiple for a trough-rate-adjusted earnings
stream, or treating the restricted collateral balance as surplus corporate
cash.

## Management incentives and capital allocation

Management is rewarded for revenue, earnings, volume, market-data growth,
capital efficiency, and shareholder returns. Those incentives are broadly
aligned with the platform, but they create three questions:

- Can volume growth be prioritized over fee quality or client economics?
- Can aggressive capital returns reduce resilience investment or clearing
  liquidity buffers?
- Can adjusted earnings exclude recurring technology, amortization, or stock
  compensation costs that matter to long-term owners?

In 2025 CME paid $3.933B in cash dividends, repurchased $266.1M of stock, and
issued $94.8M of stock compensation. The company targets regular dividends as
a percentage of prior-year cash earnings and may also pay variable dividends.
That policy is attractive because cash generation is high, but the dividend
base should be normalized for collateral interest, tax, technology, and
regulatory requirements before being treated as a perpetual annuity.

## Cross-sector position

CME is the cleanest relationship-owner/control-plane example in the current
archive.

- Versus Apollo, CME earns transaction, clearing, and data tolls without
  taking principal credit or insurance balance-sheet risk, although it carries
  systemic clearing obligations.
- Versus Aon, CME’s benchmark and clearing network has higher operating
  margins and less acquisition dependence, while Aon has more direct client
  advisory relationships and less volume sensitivity.
- Versus WESCO and Quanta, CME is far less physical and capital intensive. Its
  burden is software, regulation, trust, and systemic resilience rather than
  inventory, labor, fleet, and project completion.
- Versus asset managers, CME monetizes activity and infrastructure rather than
  relying primarily on AUM levels and investment performance.

The cross-sector pattern is that the cleaner economics often sit at the
accepted-record and coordination layer. CME captures a high share of the
value because benchmark liquidity and clearing are hard to replace, while the
clients carry market risk and the wider system carries the ultimate systemic
obligation.

## Thesis breakers and next-filing tests

### Bullish confirmation

- Volume remains resilient across multiple asset classes after volatility and
  interest rates normalize.
- Average rate per contract rises through genuine product value rather than
  unfavorable mix or lower rebates.
- Market-data revenue compounds with stable reseller and customer retention.
- Clearing migration and new products add liquidity without fragmenting the
  core network.
- Technology, regulatory, and resilience spending remain adequate while
  operating leverage persists.
- Collateral interest economics remain positive but are not required to make
  the core exchange franchise appear profitable.
- Capital returns remain covered after stock compensation and required
  reinvestment.

### Falsifiers

- Volume falls and rate per contract weakens simultaneously across major
  products.
- Liquidity migrates to competing venues or bilateral/alternative platforms.
- Regulatory action forces price compression, interoperability, or materially
  higher clearing capital.
- Market-data growth depends on one reseller, pricing changes, or temporary
  licensing arrangements.
- Collateral interest income falls faster than core fee growth can replace it.
- A technology, cyber, or clearing incident causes sustained client migration.
- Dividends and buybacks exceed normalized cash after resilience investment.
- New products generate headlines but not durable volume, liquidity, or cash.

## Skeptical reader test

What would make this analysis wrong?

It would be too conservative if CME’s liquidity and benchmark centrality allow
it to grow market-data, clearing, and transaction revenue through a wide range
of macro regimes, while collateral interest is a durable ancillary return
rather than a temporary rate windfall. New clearing mandates and Treasury or
other product expansion could extend the runway.

The bullish view would be wrong if market structure fragments, regulators
compress fees, electronic competitors attract liquidity, or current earnings
depend more on temporary volatility and collateral rates than on recurring
transaction and data economics. CME can remain a critical market utility and
still be a poor investment if the entry multiple assumes peak operating
conditions.
