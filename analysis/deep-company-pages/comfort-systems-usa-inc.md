### Q2 2026 call cross-check: technology concentration and customer funding

The Q2 call supports—not replaces—the filing bridge. Management discussion put
industrial customers at roughly three-quarters of first-half revenue and
technology at more than half, while modular capacity was being expanded against
existing customer commitments. The CFO also attributed the unusually large cash
flow change to a collection of customer and operating factors rather than one
single modular customer. That makes the current read more precise: demand and
customer trust are real, but the cash surge still requires reconciliation to
advance billings, contract liabilities, receivables, retainage, and project
execution.

## Bottom line

Comfort Systems is a skilled-trades and project-execution platform, not an
equipment manufacturer with a backlog attached. It designs, installs,
maintains, repairs, and replaces mechanical and electrical systems across
commercial, industrial, and institutional facilities. Its control point is the
ability to coordinate scarce craft labor, local operating units, subcontractors,
equipment, project estimating, bonding, and customer relationships on systems
that customers cannot simply leave unfinished.

The demand evidence is powerful. 2025 revenue reached $9.102B, operating income
$1.315B, net income $1.023B, and operating cash flow $1.186B. Remaining
performance obligations reached $11.94B at year-end, with management expecting
65%–75% to be recognized within the following twelve months. The local packet
then records backlog of $12.45B in Q1 2026 and $14.06B in Q2 2026, alongside
Q2 revenue of $3.27B and operating cash flow above $1.14B.

The forensic question is whether labor scarcity creates durable pricing power or
merely allows the company to accept more fixed-price commitments than it can
execute without margin leakage. The 10-K explicitly identifies incorrect bid
estimates, commitments exceeding labor resources, cost-to-cost revisions,
subcontractor failure, customer-payment delays, surety, insurance, and backlog
cancellations as material risks.

Current judgment: Comfort Systems is one of the clearest physical-economy
control points in the archive, but its earnings quality must be judged through
contract estimates, billings, retainage, deferred revenue, cash collection,
workforce productivity, and acquisition returns—not backlog alone.

## What the company actually controls

The company operates roughly 50 operating units across 190 locations in 142
cities. Its 2025 revenue was 73.3% mechanical and 26.7% electrical; 63.2% came
from installation in newly constructed facilities and 36.8% from renovation,
expansion, maintenance, repair, and replacement. That mix matters: the new-build
piece captures the current data-center and industrial cycle, while the existing-
building piece supplies a more durable replacement and service demand.

The operating chain is:

`customer project -> estimate and contract -> materials / subcontractors -> craft labor -> measured progress -> billing / collection -> service relationship`

The valuable relationship is with owners, general contractors, and facility
operators that need accountable MEP execution. The scarce input is not only
workers; it is the local managerial system that can price work, schedule crews,
manage change orders, satisfy safety and bonding requirements, and deliver the
job without losing the estimate.

The first read is unusually strong:

- 2025 revenue grew 29.5%, gross profit 48.7%, operating income 75.4%, and
  operating cash flow 39.7%.
- Gross margin expanded from 19.0% in 2023 to 21.0% in 2024 and 24.1% in
  2025. That is meaningful operating leverage, but it also raises the cost of
  a future estimate reset if mix, labor, or project execution normalizes.
- Operating cash flow exceeded net income in each year, but that does not
  eliminate working-capital risk. 2025 included a $910.1M increase in billings
  in excess of costs and deferred revenue, primarily tied to technology-sector
  projects.
- Capital expenditures increased 63.9% from 2023 to 2025, while cash
  acquisition payments increased 173.5%. The platform is not capital-heavy like
  a data-center REIT, but it is not free to scale.

## Cash conversion and owner-cash bridge

### 2025 reported bridge

| Item | $ millions |
|---|---:|
| Net cash from operations | 1,186.4 |
| Less: purchases of property and equipment | (154.9) |
| Less: cash acquisitions, net of cash acquired | (279.6) |
| Less: investment purchases | (64.2) |
| Residual after listed uses | **687.7** |

The residual is a useful screening figure, not a terminal owner-cash estimate.
It precedes dividends, share repurchases, debt principal, contingent
consideration, and the capital required to keep expanding the operating
network. It also reflects a large favorable financing-like contribution from
customer billings: billings in excess of costs and deferred revenue increased
$910.1M in 2025, while accounts payable and other current liabilities decreased
$276.1M.

The 2025 cash-flow statement shows the opposing pressures clearly. Receivables
used $594.3M, inventories used $24.4M, prepaid/current assets used $91.4M, and
costs in excess of billings plus unbilled receivables used $19.9M. The company
still generated strong cash because customer billings and other timing items
offset those uses. The next year must test whether the advance billing is
structural, merely project-timing related, or a cash benefit that reverses as
technology projects are executed.

### Acquisition capital is a real economic claim

Comfort Systems paid $279.6M for acquisitions in 2025, $235.5M in 2024, and
$102.3M in 2023. The annual filing also records contingent earn-out obligations
at fair value. The balance fell from $140.2M at the end of 2024 to $34.8M at the
end of 2025 after $142.8M of settlements and a $33.5M fair-value adjustment.

The adjustment is non-cash in the period, but it is not irrelevant. The fair
value is a Level 3 probability-weighted discounted cash-flow estimate based on
future cash flow and operating income assumptions. A falling liability can
reflect actual settlement, forecast changes, or both. Acquisition return
analysis should therefore track cash paid, notes to former owners, earn-outs,
incremental revenue and margin, integration costs, and post-close working
capital rather than treating acquisition spending as a single clean growth
number.

## Earnings quality and accounting judgment

### Cost-to-cost revenue recognition

The company generally recognizes project revenue over time using a cost-to-cost
input method. Revenue and profit depend on estimates of total contract costs,
including labor, materials, subcontractors, schedule, productivity, and final
settlement. The filing states that revisions can arise from job performance,
job conditions, and final contract settlements and are recognized in the period
determined.

This is not an allegation of manipulation. It is the central forensic risk. A
small change in expected cost-to-complete can move both current revenue and
profit. The annual audit procedures specifically include comparing actual
completed-project results with prior estimates and examining changes in total
cost estimates, change orders, supplier contracts, and invoices.

### Contract liabilities are both a strength and a timing risk

At December 31, 2025, contract assets were $88.8M while billings in excess of
costs and deferred revenue were $2.120B, up from $1.149B in 2024. The company
recognized $1.00B of 2025 revenue from contract liabilities that existed at the
start of the year. Technology-sector project billing timing drove approximately
$910.1M of the annual increase.

The clean interpretation is customer-funded working capital on a strong order
book. The skeptical interpretation is that current cash conversion is ahead of
the work and margin realization. The discriminating test is whether the
liability releases into revenue with stable or improving project margins, while
receivables, claims, retainage, and job-loss accruals remain controlled.

### Retainage and collection

Retainage included in accounts receivable rose from $329.2M at year-end 2024 to
$506.5M at year-end 2025. Retainage withheld from subcontractors rose from
$50.9M to $83.1M. The company says most is finalized and collected or paid in
the subsequent year, but this remains a direct cash test. Backlog can be firm
and still produce poor owner economics if completion, customer acceptance, or
claims delay collection.

### SBC and adjusted presentation

Stock-based compensation was $21.8M in 2025, $16.6M in 2024, and $12.9M in
2023. It is non-cash in the current-period cash-flow statement but a real
economic claim if shares are issued or repurchases are required to offset it.
The company also reports same-store SG&A excluding amortization as a comparative
measure; that measure can help isolate acquired operations, but it should not
replace GAAP SG&A in a valuation.

## Balance-sheet resilience and liquidity

At December 31, 2025, Comfort Systems held $981.9M of cash and $34.4M of U.S.
Treasury bills. Total debt was $145.2M, up from $68.3M in 2024; the increase
included $100M outstanding on the revolving facility. The amended facility has
$1.10B of capacity, $79M of letters of credit outstanding, and $921M available.
The revolver is floating-rate, and the weighted average borrowing rate was
approximately 5.0% at year-end.

The balance sheet is resilient relative to the operating cash flow, but the
company also carries $302.6M of lease liabilities, $1.026B of goodwill, and
$485.2M of identifiable intangible assets. The financing risk is less about
near-term solvency than about whether acquisitions, earn-outs, letters of
credit, surety, self-insurance, and working-capital demands grow faster than
recurring project cash.

## Lyn Alden lens: durability, liquidity, and the physical bottleneck

Comfort Systems benefits from several durable forces: an aging building stock,
more complex electrical loads, data-center and technology construction, air
quality and energy-efficiency upgrades, and the scarcity of qualified craft
labor. It is also exposed to the liquidity cycle: customer capital budgets,
commercial real-estate conditions, industrial reshoring, interest rates,
construction financing, and the availability of surety and credit.

The Alden distinction is between necessary physical work and speculative peak
capex. Renovation, replacement, maintenance, and energy-efficiency work may be
more durable than a single data-center expansion cycle. The company’s 36.8%
existing-building revenue is therefore a meaningful durability anchor, while
the 63.2% new-construction exposure provides upside and cyclical risk.

The macro transmission chain is:

`rates / credit / customer capex -> project awards -> backlog -> labor and material execution -> billing -> collected cash`

Liquidity can improve backlog before it improves owner cash. If rates rise or
customer financing tightens, cancellations, slower collections, labor
underutilization, and project claims can appear after the demand signal has
already looked strong.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers and reinvestment

The growth narrative is “AI, electrification, complex buildings, and scarce
trades.” The valuation translation must be narrower:

`addressable work -> signed profitable backlog -> recognized revenue -> after-tax project profit -> reinvestment in people, equipment, acquisitions, and working capital`

The relevant reinvestment rate is not only PP&E. It includes recruiting and
training, local operating-unit expansion, safety and insurance capacity,
working capital, acquired capabilities, and the cost of maintaining the
reputation that wins complex projects. The company may be asset-light relative
to a manufacturer, but it is labor- and coordination-intensive.

The lifecycle question is whether 2025 margin expansion is a durable shift in
mix and execution or a peak-cycle outcome. A normalized model should separately
stress:

- organic revenue and same-store backlog growth;
- gross margin after craft-labor and subcontractor inflation;
- contract-liability release versus new customer advances;
- capex required for fleet, facilities, modular capacity, and technology;
- acquisition cash and earn-out settlement;
- retainage, claims, and customer concentration; and
- dilution from SBC and stock-funded acquisition consideration.

## Management incentives and financial engineering

The operating incentives naturally favor revenue growth, backlog, earnings,
margin, acquisition integration, and share returns. Those goals can be aligned
with owners, but they create predictable pressure points:

- backlog growth can be emphasized before cost-to-complete risk is visible;
- cost-to-cost estimates can make current margin sensitive to forecast changes;
- “same-store” exclusions can make organic execution look cleaner than total
  GAAP economics;
- acquisitions can increase revenue and EBITDA before their full capital and
  integration burden is proven; and
- repurchases can raise per-share results while total share compensation and
  contingent consideration remain owner claims.

The evidence currently supports a set of tests, not a misconduct finding. The
strongest test is a roll-forward of prior-year estimated project margins to
actual completion outcomes, paired with changes in contract assets, contract
liabilities, retainage, job-loss accruals, claims, and collected cash.

## Valuation and reverse-valuation questions

No price target is assigned here. The market must prove that:

1. Backlog converts to revenue without a material project-margin reset.
2. The $2.120B contract-liability balance is a durable customer-financing
   advantage rather than a temporary timing benefit.
3. Gross margin can remain above the pre-2025 range after labor scarcity and
   large-project mix normalize.
4. Acquisition cash produces incremental after-tax cash returns above the
   company's cost of capital.
5. Existing-building service and replacement work dampens the new-construction
   cycle.
6. Repurchases and SBC do not obscure flat or declining total owner value.

The reverse valuation should model the denominator as normalized owner cash
after maintenance capex, normal working capital, acquisition requirements,
contingent consideration, debt, and dilution. A multiple applied to 2025 OCF
without adjusting for the $910M billing contribution would overstate durability.

## Filing-based thesis breakers

The thesis weakens if future filings show:

- backlog or RPO growth accompanied by falling gross margin or rising job-loss
  accruals;
- contract liabilities releasing into revenue without corresponding cash
  collection or stable project profitability;
- receivables, retainage, claims, or unbilled balances growing faster than
  revenue;
- prior cost-to-complete estimates repeatedly revised downward after projects
  finish;
- skilled-labor and subcontractor costs rising faster than contract pricing;
- cash acquisitions and earn-outs rising while same-store returns deteriorate;
- technology-sector concentration increasing without customer or project-level
  disclosure; or
- repurchases and debt usage accelerating while SBC and diluted shares do not
  improve on a total-return basis.

## Final judgment

Comfort Systems is a strong example of how a valuable relationship can sit in
the physical execution layer rather than in a patented product or financial
interface. It may control scarce coordination capacity while customers carry
the larger project and facility burden. But the company also carries the
labor, estimate, safety, surety, working-capital, and acquisition risks that
make backlog economically conditional.

The clean thesis is durable MEP replacement and complex-system execution with
pricing power created by scarce labor and high failure costs. The skeptical
thesis is a peak-cycle contractor whose cost-to-cost accounting and customer
billings make current margins and cash look cleaner before the work is fully
earned. The next filing’s project-estimate roll-forward and cash conversion are
more informative than another backlog headline.
