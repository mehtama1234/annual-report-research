# Core & Main, Inc. — water infrastructure distribution under forensic review

Research date: 2026-09-13
Evidence window: fiscal 2025 ended February 1, 2026 and Q1 2026 update
Ticker: CNM
Evidence grade: A for audited fiscal 2025 filing data; B for the durability of municipal demand, margin initiatives, acquisition returns, and the conversion of consolidated cash into Class A shareholder cash.

## Bottom line

Core & Main controls a specialized water, wastewater, storm-drainage, and fire-
protection distribution channel. Its value comes from local inventory, branch
density, technical product knowledge, project planning, delivery, and a
nationwide supplier/customer bridge. This is a stronger durability story than
ordinary new-construction distribution because much of the product supports
maintenance, repair, replacement, and public-utility reliability.

The central forensic catch is that the business has a complicated capital
structure and a lumpy reinvestment model. The fiscal 2025 audited cash-flow
statement reports $650M of OCF, $46M of capex, and $61M of acquisitions. The
post-capex and post-acquisition residuals are positive in this year, but the
prior fiscal year required $741M of acquisition spending, so a single-year
cash conversion conclusion would be unsafe. In addition, the company had a
$725M Tax Receivable Agreement
payable, non-controlling interests, partnership-interest exchanges, and debt
financing. Core & Main may have a real infrastructure control point, but
consolidated adjusted EBITDA is not the same as cash available to Class A
shareholders.

## Control point and demand stack

Core & Main operates more than 370 branches in 49 states, serving over 5,000
suppliers and more than 60,000 customers. It sells to municipalities, private
water companies, and professional contractors across municipal, non-residential,
and residential markets. Its addressable market is large and fragmented, and
management presents the company as one of only two national distributors at its
scale.

The control point is project and specification execution: the customer needs
the right pipe, valve, meter, fire-protection product, drainage material, and
related support at the right time. The moat is local service plus national
assortment and purchasing power, not proprietary manufacturing.

| Net income | 531 | 434 | 462 |
| Operating cash flow | 1,069 | 621 | 650 |
| Capital expenditures | 39 | 35 | 46 |
| Acquisitions | 231 | 741 | 61 |
| OCF less capex | 1,030 | 586 | 604 |
| OCF less capex and acquisitions | 799 | (155) | 543 |

The year-to-year OCF pattern is highly working-capital-sensitive: $1.069B in
fiscal 2023, $621M in fiscal 2024, and $650M in fiscal 2025. Management
attributed the fiscal 2025 increase to lower interest and income-tax payments
and higher net income, partly offset by greater working-capital investment.
The earlier packet’s approximately $650M figure is consistent with the fiscal
2025 audited statement; the prior memo had incorrectly compared that packet
figure with the fiscal 2024 $621M figure. The period mapping is now corrected,
but the working-capital and acquisition cadence remains a live forensic issue.

## Cash conversion and reinvestment

The fiscal 2025 cash bridge is:

- OCF: $650M
- Capex: $46M
- Acquisitions: $61M
- Other investing: approximately $37M, including tax-advantaged limited
  partnership investments
- Residual before financing: approximately $505M after listed investing cash
  flows, consistent with reported net investing outflow of $145M

The company’s ordinary operating model was cash generative after the fiscal
2025 acquisition program, but the prior year’s $741M acquisition outlay shows
why one clean year cannot establish owner cash. Core & Main says acquisitions
expand underpenetrated products and markets, while greenfields add local
density. The correct Damodaran treatment is to estimate maintenance capex and
the acquisition/greenfield spend required to defend the competitive-advantage
period. Capitalizing only adjusted EBITDA or one year of OCF would overstate
owner cash.

## Q1 2026 checkpoint: adjusted EBITDA was not distributable cash

Q1 2026 net sales were `$1.910B`, gross margin was `27.2%`, adjusted EBITDA
was `$226M`, and operating cash flow was only `$82M`. Management reaffirmed
fiscal 2026 guidance of `$7.8B–$7.9B` of sales, `$950M–$980M` of adjusted
EBITDA, and operating cash flow equal to `60%–70%` of adjusted EBITDA.

The quarter is not evidence that the business is broken. It is evidence that
the conversion ratio must be observed rather than assumed. At the midpoint of
the guidance range, the company is describing annual operating cash flow of
roughly `$579M–$676M`; the Q1 result was about `36%` of adjusted EBITDA. That
ratio can improve with seasonal working-capital release, but inventory,
receivables, acquisitions, greenfield branches, interest, taxes, and the
capital structure still stand between adjusted EBITDA and Class A cash.

Capital allocation makes the test more urgent. Core & Main repurchased `$88M`
of shares during Q1 and another `$37M` after quarter-end while opening five
greenfield locations. Repurchases may be rational if the shares are cheap and
cash conversion normalizes, but the company also had the `$725M` Tax Receivable
Agreement payable, non-controlling interests, debt, and acquisition needs. The
next filing should reconcile repurchases and branch investment with cash after
inventory, taxes, TRA payments, NCI distributions, and debt service.

The correct conclusion from Q1 is therefore narrower than “infrastructure
demand is durable.” The category demand remains credible, but the valuation
requires proof that branch density and higher-value water categories produce
cash available to Class A owners after the ownership and reinvestment claims.

Inventory is carried at lower of cost or net realizable value, using weighted
average cost for substantially all inventory. Inbound freight is capitalized,
and supplier rebates/purchase discounts are accrued into inventory; the rebate
amount included in inventory was $54M at FY2025 year-end versus $43M a year
earlier. Those estimates deserve attention when product prices, freight, or
supplier terms change.

## Earnings quality and financial engineering

Core & Main’s most important forensic issues are structural rather than a
single suspicious line item:

- Adjusted EBITDA adds back equity compensation, acquisition expenses, IPO /
  offering expenses, and debt-modification items. Acquisition expenses are
  economically recurring if the growth strategy depends on a steady deal
  cadence.
- Fiscal 2025 included $14M of equity-based compensation, $11M of acquisition
  expenses, and $142M of interest expense in the adjusted EBITDA bridge.
- The company’s Class A parent consolidates Holdings, but continuing limited
  partners retain non-controlling interests. FY2024 net income attributable to
  Core & Main rose even as consolidated net income declined, partly because
  partnership-interest exchanges changed the allocation.
- Tax Receivable Agreements require payment of 85% of realized or deemed tax
  benefits. A $725M related-party payable was recorded at February 1, 2026;
  the company retained only 15% of the corresponding tax benefit.
- The TRA liability is recorded through equity when established, not earnings,
  but it is a real future cash claim and may accelerate on a change of control.
- Inventory rebates and lower-of-NRV reserves can shift gross margin between
  periods, particularly during cost deflation or a demand slowdown.

These are not proof of shenanigans. They are reasons to distinguish
consolidated earnings, adjusted EBITDA, Holdings cash, parent cash, and cash
available to Class A owners.

## Balance sheet, ownership, and capital allocation

Fiscal 2025 financing included $950M of new senior term debt, $1.110B of ABL
repayments, $223M of long-term debt repayments, $176M of equity-interest
repurchases, $11M of distributions to non-controlling holders, and $11M of TRA
payments. The capital structure can fund growth, but it also means that
acquisitions, debt service, NCI distributions, TRA payments, taxes, and
repurchases compete for the same cash stream.

Management and sales associates participate in performance-based compensation
linked to branch or region profitability and working-capital efficiency. That
is a useful counterweight to pure revenue growth, but the proxy should be
checked for how much of the executive scorecard uses adjusted EBITDA, EPS,
leverage, or share price rather than post-acquisition cash returns.

## Alden lens: unavoidable infrastructure, but municipal timing

Water, wastewater, storm drainage, fire protection, and smart metering are
closer to civic necessity than discretionary building products. Aging systems,
replacement cycles, leak reduction, treatment-plant upgrades, and public
safety provide a durable demand floor. The company can benefit even when new
housing is weak if maintenance and public-works spending remain active.

The macro risk is timing and liquidity: municipal budgets, federal
appropriations, interest rates, contractor backlogs, and supply-chain costs can
delay projects. The Alden question is whether the replacement need is strong
enough to overcome funding cycles, or whether current growth is mainly a
project-timing and acquisition effect.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers

The investment narrative should be translated into:

1. Volume, price, acquisitions, and greenfield growth separately.
2. Gross margin after supplier rebates, freight, private-label mix, and
   inventory reserve changes.
3. Reinvestment including inventory, branches, project-support capacity,
   acquisitions, technology, and working capital.
4. Cash available to Class A owners after NCI distributions, TRA payments,
   interest, taxes, and debt reduction.
5. A competitive-advantage period based on branch density and customer
   specification relationships, discounted for manufacturer direct sales,
   local competitors, and municipal procurement changes.

At the September 11, 2026 market snapshot, CNM was approximately $40.62 per
share with equity value of about $7.854B. Relative to fiscal 2025 OCF less
capex of $604M, the market requires durable growth and a continued high return
on reinvestment. The prior fiscal year’s negative post-acquisition residual
shows why the market should not capitalize the fiscal 2025 $543M residual as a
steady state without testing acquisition cadence and Class A cash claims.
The valuation should therefore be tested against normalized post-acquisition
owner cash, not adjusted EBITDA alone.

## Thesis breakers and next checks

The thesis weakens if:

- municipal or infrastructure demand is repeatedly deferred;
- acquisitions drive reported growth without improving post-deal cash return;
- inventory grows faster than sales or supplier rebates/reserves reverse;
- gross-margin expansion depends on price timing rather than mix and service;
- TRA, NCI, and debt claims absorb an increasing share of Holdings cash;
- repurchases continue while leverage and acquisition spending remain high; or
- smart-utility and treatment-plant growth fails to offset basic-product
  volume weakness.

The next filing review should confirm the corrected fiscal-period mapping,
update the TRA liability and NCI ownership, capture inventory/reserve changes,
acquisition cohorts, greenfield returns, customer concentration, debt
maturities, and cash available to Class A shareholders.
