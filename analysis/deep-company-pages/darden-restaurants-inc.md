# Darden Restaurants, Inc. — full-service dining through traffic, labor, and restaurant capital

Research date: 2026-09-14  
Evidence window: FY2025 and Q1 FY2026  
Ticker: DRI  
Evidence grade: A for restaurant count, ownership structure, sales, same-restaurant sales, segment results, capital returns, and disclosed acquisition facts; B for normalized restaurant-level cash, maintenance capital, lease-adjusted returns, guest traffic quality, labor productivity, and Chuy’s return on invested capital.

## Investment question

Darden is a mature, company-operated full-service restaurant system. It owns
most of its restaurants and captures the guest relationship, the food and
service revenue, and the operating profit. That makes the economics more
transparent than a franchise-heavy model, but it also leaves Darden with the
full burden of food, labor, occupancy, repairs, remodels, new-store investment,
service quality, and corporate execution.

The investment question is whether Darden’s scale and brand portfolio can
produce durable common-owner cash after those burdens. Same-restaurant sales,
guest counts, adjusted EBITDA, and adjusted EPS are useful evidence, but none
is a substitute for incremental cash return on a restaurant or for cash per
diluted share after reinvestment.

## What the customer buys

The guest buys prepared food, drinks, attentive service, and an inviting social
setting. The meal is only one part of the purchase. Convenience, reliability,
menu choice, service execution, atmosphere, location, and the reason for the
visit influence frequency and willingness to pay.

Darden’s portfolio spans Olive Garden, LongHorn Steakhouse, Cheddar’s Scratch
Kitchen, Chuy’s, Yard House, Ruth’s Chris, The Capital Grille, Seasons 52,
Eddie V’s, Bahama Breeze, and The Capital Burger. Different brands expose the
company to different guest occasions, price points, food inputs, labor needs,
and traffic sensitivity. A consolidated same-restaurant number can hide these
differences.

## Control point and operating sequence

The operating sequence is:

`guest occasion -> restaurant visit -> food, beverage, and service execution -> guest satisfaction and repeat traffic -> sales -> restaurant margin -> maintenance and growth investment -> owner cash`

Darden controls brand standards, menu design, pricing, marketing, staffing
systems, purchasing, restaurant operations, digital channels, site selection,
and capital allocation. It does not control wage rates, food and commodity
prices, rent and property taxes, local competition, consumer income, weather,
health regulation, or the quality of every shift-level service interaction.

Because Darden operates most restaurants itself, its reported revenue is closer
to the end customer’s payment than a franchisor’s royalty revenue. That also
means the company bears the cost required to generate that revenue. The direct
operator is the right control case for testing whether a brand relationship is
valuable after the full burden is retained.

## Correct operating denominator

The company should be read through a linked set of denominators:

- same-restaurant sales split into guest traffic, price, menu mix, and channel;
- sales and profit per open restaurant, occupied seat, or operating week;
- food and beverage cost per sales dollar;
- restaurant labor cost, wage rate, hours, turnover, and management coverage;
- restaurant expenses including occupancy, repairs, utilities, insurance, and
  marketing;
- restaurant-level profit after all directly required operating costs;
- maintenance and remodel capital per existing restaurant;
- new-restaurant cost, ramp time, sales, and mature cash return;
- acquisition price, integration cost, acquired sales, and post-acquisition
  restaurant economics;
- leases, debt, interest, taxes, corporate overhead, buybacks, and diluted
  shares.

The main mistake is to treat same-restaurant sales as a margin or cash metric.
Sales can rise because of price while guest traffic falls. Restaurant profit
can rise because of temporary food or labor timing. Adjusted EPS can rise from
buybacks while the company is still spending heavily to maintain and expand
the system.

## Traffic, price, and brand portfolio

The first forensic question is whether the sales increase is healthy demand or
price compensation for lower traffic. A clean result would show positive guest
counts, stable or rising repeat behavior, acceptable check growth, and margins
that do not depend on cutting service or delaying maintenance.

Darden’s portfolio gives management several ways to allocate attention. Olive
Garden and LongHorn can provide scale and traffic. Fine Dining may have higher
average checks but greater exposure to discretionary occasions. Chuy’s adds
growth and a new operating system but makes the consolidated comparisons less
clean until it has been owned long enough for comparable reporting. Bahama
Breeze closures and brand alternatives show that a large system can still
remove concepts whose returns or strategic fit are inadequate.

Portfolio breadth can reduce dependence on one concept, but it can also reduce
clarity. The investor should examine brand-level same-restaurant sales,
restaurant profit, unit openings, closures, and capital allocation rather than
accepting one consolidated growth rate.

## Food, labor, and occupancy burden

Food and beverage cost is directly linked to menu mix, commodity prices,
portion control, waste, supplier terms, and menu design. Pricing can protect
gross margin only if guests accept it and traffic remains healthy. A favorable
commodity quarter is not a structural margin improvement.

Restaurant labor is the largest service constraint. Darden needs enough people
with the right training to prepare food, serve guests, clean the restaurant,
manage shifts, and maintain safety. The key tests are wage inflation, hours per
sales dollar, management coverage, turnover, hiring, training, overtime,
service speed, and guest satisfaction. A labor saving that weakens service can
reduce future traffic and increase turnover costs.

Occupancy includes rent or property cost, taxes, insurance, utilities, repairs,
and the physical condition of the site. A company-operated restaurant retains
more revenue and profit than a royalty model, but it also retains these costs.
The proper comparison with franchisors is therefore not corporate margin alone;
it is the risk-adjusted return after each model’s transferred or retained
burden.

## Digital and first-party ordering

The Q1 FY2026 call discussed continued growth in first-party delivery at Olive
Garden. Digital ordering can improve convenience and customer data, but the
investor must charge delivery fees, commissions, packaging, promotions,
refunds, order errors, labor changes, and technology cost. Sales through a
digital channel are not automatically more profitable than an in-restaurant
visit.

The useful denominator is contribution per order and repeat behavior after all
channel-specific costs. If digital expands reach but reduces restaurant-level
margin or shifts guests away from more profitable occasions, consolidated sales
growth can overstate economic improvement.

## Acquisition and portfolio repair

Chuy’s added `103` restaurants and more than `7,000` employees. An acquisition
can provide new units, a differentiated brand, purchasing scale, and operating
capability. It can also add integration work, systems conversion, retention
cost, remodeling needs, acquired leases, and a higher management burden.

The correct acquisition test is:

`purchase price + integration cost + remodel and capital needs -> acquired unit sales and margin -> cash generation -> return on invested capital`

Bahama Breeze closures and the sale of eight Olive Garden Canada restaurants
show that portfolio decisions change the reported denominator. A divestiture or
conversion may improve the continuing mix without proving that the original
capital allocation was good. Continuing-portfolio results and total capital
spent must both be retained.

## Cash conversion bridge

The filed continuing-operations cash flow gives a more disciplined starting
point than adjusted EBITDA. Operating cash flow was `$1,621.7M` in FY2024,
`$1,707.0M` in FY2025, and `$1,853.1M` in FY2026. Purchases of land, buildings,
and equipment were `$601.2M`, `$644.6M`, and `$734.0M`, respectively. Cash used
for the Chuy’s acquisition was `$613.7M` in FY2025 and there was no comparable
business-acquisition cash use in FY2026.

| Continuing-operations screen | FY2024 | FY2025 | FY2026 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Operating cash flow | `$1,621.7M` | `$1,707.0M` | `$1,853.1M` |
| Land, buildings, and equipment purchases | `$(601.2M)` | `$(644.6M)` | `$(734.0M)` |
| Acquisition cash | `$(701.1M)` | `$(613.7M)` | `$0M` |
| OCF less capital and acquisition cash | `$319.4M` | `$448.7M` | `$1,119.1M` |

This residual is a screening denominator, not final owner cash. It includes
both maintenance and growth capital, and it does not independently resolve
leases, debt repayment, taxes, dilution, or the return required on new units.
The sharp FY2026 increase also shows why a single recent-year residual should
not be capitalized without separating acquisition timing and the mature
maintenance requirement.

## Earnings quality and financial-engineering tests

### 1. Same-restaurant sales must be decomposed

Same-restaurant sales combine traffic, price, mix, and calendar effects. The
investor should compare guest counts, check, frequency, restaurant margin, and
cash rather than treating the headline percentage as demand strength.

### 2. Adjusted EPS must be reconciled to recurring costs

Q1 FY2026 adjusted EPS of `$1.97` excluded Chuy’s transaction and integration
costs, closed-restaurant costs, and the gain on the sale of Olive Garden Canada
restaurants. Some excluded items are genuinely non-recurring; others can recur
whenever Darden acquires, closes, converts, or sells restaurants. The investor
should show the frequency and cash cost of these actions over a multi-year
period.

### 3. Adjusted EBITDA is not restaurant owner cash

Adjusted EBITDA can exclude depreciation and other costs that represent the
consumption or renewal of the restaurant system. A direct operator must spend
on kitchen equipment, dining rooms, roofs, technology, safety, and remodels.
The cash bridge should begin with operating cash flow, subtract maintenance
capital, new-store capital, acquisition cash, and recurring restructuring or
integration costs, then account for debt and diluted shares.

### 4. Buybacks can improve EPS while reducing resilience

Darden returned substantial cash while adding units and integrating Chuy’s.
Buybacks create value when the shares are purchased below conservative value
and the remaining business retains adequate liquidity. They weaken resilience
when funded by debt, when maintenance capital is deferred, or when they mask
flat per-share cash generation after the full investment burden.

### 5. Portfolio change can create artificial comparability

Chuy’s, Ruth’s Chris, Bahama Breeze, the Canadian Olive Garden sale, closures,
and brand conversions all affect the reported base. Management’s comparable
same-restaurant definitions are useful, but the investor still needs total
system sales, continuing units, acquired units, disposed units, and capital
spent by category.

### 6. Franchise economics should not be mixed with operating economics

Darden had `167` franchised restaurants operated by independent third parties,
but the overwhelming majority of the system was company-operated. Franchise
royalties can be high-margin and low-capital, while company-owned sales include
the full food, labor, occupancy, and maintenance cost. The two revenue streams
should be shown separately before judging the quality of consolidated margins.

## Lyn Alden lens: consumer liquidity and operating durability

Full-service dining depends on household income, employment, food inflation,
credit conditions, housing costs, and confidence. Consumers can reduce visit
frequency, trade down, choose smaller checks, or shift occasions when liquidity
is tight. Different Darden brands may respond differently to those changes.

Darden’s scale can improve purchasing, technology, advertising, training, and
site selection. Scale does not remove labor or food inflation, and it does not
guarantee that new units earn acceptable returns. Liquidity resilience depends
on restaurant cash generation, lease and debt obligations, remodel needs,
acquisition commitments, and the ability to preserve service through a weak
traffic period.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers and lifecycle

The bullish narrative is that Darden owns a high-quality portfolio of national
full-service brands, uses scale and data to outperform the category, grows
units, integrates acquisitions, and returns cash while maintaining attractive
restaurant economics.

That narrative requires measurable assumptions:

- sustainable guest traffic and check growth by brand;
- food cost, labor cost, occupancy cost, and restaurant-level margin;
- mature sales and cash return for new restaurants;
- maintenance, remodel, technology, and safety capital per unit;
- Chuy’s integration cost and post-integration return on invested capital;
- the continuing cost and value of portfolio closures, conversions, and sales;
- franchise royalties versus company-operated contribution;
- debt, leases, interest, taxes, corporate overhead, and diluted shares;
- normalized free cash flow after all recurring reinvestment.

Darden is a mature operator with selective unit and portfolio growth. The
valuation should not assume that every sales dollar from new restaurants or
acquisitions earns the mature portfolio margin immediately. A reasonable
scenario set would separately vary traffic, margin, unit growth, maintenance
capital, integration cost, and the multiple applied to normalized owner cash.

## Valuation expectations

The September 14, 2026 market snapshot was approximately `$25.17B` of equity
value, or about `23x` the quoted trailing EPS measure. Against FY2025 adjusted
diluted EPS of `$9.553`, the equity value is roughly `22.8x` adjusted EPS. This
is a market-expectation screen, not a price target, because adjusted EPS still
needs to be reduced for the direct operator’s recurring capital burden.

| Normalized adjusted EPS screen | Applied multiple | Implied equity value |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| `$8.50` bear case | `18x` | `$153` per share |
| `$9.75` base case | `22x` | `$215` per share |
| `$11.50` strong case | `25x` | `$288` per share |

The base case requires traffic stability, acceptable food and labor margins,
successful Chuy’s integration, and maintenance capital that does not consume
the apparent earnings growth. The strong case requires both higher normalized
earnings and continued confidence in unit returns. The correct final valuation
should use diluted common-owner cash after maintenance capital, leases, debt,
and acquisition spending; that bridge remains an open filing test.

## Management incentives and thesis breakers

Management’s long-term framework emphasizes adjusted EPS growth, dividends,
repurchases, new restaurants, same-restaurant sales, and total shareholder
return. Those measures align with shareholder value only when they are supported
by guest traffic, service quality, restaurant-level cash, and returns above the
cost of capital.

The thesis weakens if same-restaurant sales rise only through price while guest
counts fall; food or labor cost absorbs the price increase; service quality or
employee retention deteriorates; new units earn lower returns; Chuy’s requires
repeated integration or remodel spending; closures and impairments recur;
maintenance capital is deferred; lease or debt obligations rise faster than
cash; adjusted EPS grows mainly through buybacks; or the stronger Olive Garden
and LongHorn brands cannot offset weakness in the broader portfolio.

## Next-filing tests

The next filing and earnings call should answer:

- What portion of same-restaurant sales came from traffic, price, and mix?
- Did guest counts remain positive by major brand?
- Did restaurant labor, food, occupancy, and marketing cost per sales dollar
  improve or deteriorate?
- What was operating cash flow after maintenance and remodel capital?
- What were new-restaurant openings, sales ramps, closures, and cash returns?
- How much cash and management time did Chuy’s integration consume, and what
  are the acquired units earning after the integration period?
- Did leases, debt, interest, or share count change materially?
- Are franchise royalties growing without obscuring weaker company-operated
  economics?
- Did portfolio changes improve returns, or only change the reported base?
