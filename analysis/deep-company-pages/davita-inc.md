# DaVita Inc. — chronic treatment density, reimbursement, and owner cash

Date baseline: `2026-09-14`. Primary evidence: [DaVita source ledger](../../extracted/healthcare/specialized-health-services/davita-inc/source-ledger.md), [DaVita company packet](../../extracted/healthcare/specialized-health-services/davita-inc/company-packet.md), the FY2025 Form 10-K, and the Q1–Q2 FY2026 filings and results materials listed in that ledger.

## Investment question

DaVita operates kidney-care services, with dialysis as the central recurring
treatment system. It also supports home dialysis, integrated kidney care, and
risk-based arrangements. The company sits at the intersection of an essential
medical need and a reimbursement-heavy, labor-intensive operating model.

The investment question is whether treatment density and care coordination
produce durable common-owner cash after clinicians, technicians, facilities,
equipment, supplies, payer mix, reimbursement changes, quality requirements,
working capital, debt, acquisitions, value-based medical costs, and capital
needed to keep the network reliable.

The second question is whether the newer integrated and risk-based care models
improve the economics of the chronic-care relationship or add medical-cost and
capital risk that is less visible in the dialysis operating margin.

## What the patient and payer are buying

The patient is buying repeated treatment, access to a nearby or home-supported
modality, clinical monitoring, supplies, and care coordination. The payer is
buying treatment delivery and, increasingly, an opportunity to reduce the total
cost of kidney disease through earlier intervention, home care, and coordinated
management.

DaVita's operating sequence is:

`kidney disease -> treatment eligibility and modality -> scheduled treatment
-> clinical labor, equipment, supplies, and facility -> payer claim and
collection -> repeat treatment -> care coordination or risk arrangement ->
owner cash`

DaVita controls a dense treatment network, clinical workflow, patient
relationship, staff capability, equipment and supply process, and parts of the
care pathway. It does not control reimbursement policy, patient health, labor
availability, or all medical costs. Risk-based arrangements add a separate
claim: the company may receive a fixed or shared payment while carrying more
responsibility for the patient's total medical outcome.

## Operating denominator

The correct denominator begins with treatments and patient care, not revenue
alone. Track:

- treatments per day and patient census;
- revenue per treatment and payer mix;
- clinic utilization, staffing, wage cost, and productivity;
- treatment modality, including home dialysis;
- supply, drug, equipment, and facility cost per treatment;
- quality, hospitalization, adherence, and patient retention;
- revenue and medical cost in risk-based arrangements;
- denial, collection, bad-debt, and supplemental-payment timing;
- maintenance and growth capital for clinics and equipment;
- acquisitions, debt, interest, and buybacks; and
- diluted cash per share after all continuing claims.

Treatment growth is useful only when the treatment produces an adequate return
after clinical labor, equipment, facilities, reimbursement, quality, and
capital. A larger patient or risk-contract count can increase exposure without
increasing owner value.

## Treatment economics and the burden stack

A dialysis treatment requires a facility or home-support system, trained labor,
equipment, water and utilities, drugs and supplies, scheduling, clinical
oversight, infection control, patient transportation, documentation, and payer
collection. The network's density can lower some costs and improve access, but
it also creates fixed operating and quality requirements.

The economic bridge is:

`patients × treatments per patient × revenue per treatment -> treatment revenue
-> labor, supplies, drugs, facility, equipment, transport, and quality cost ->
corporate support -> maintenance and growth capex -> debt, interest, taxes,
acquisitions, and buybacks -> owner cash`

The bridge changes for home dialysis. Home care may shift some facility and
staff burden toward the patient, caregiver, equipment, training, delivery, and
support system. It can improve convenience and cost under the right clinical
conditions, but adoption, adherence, training, supplies, and patient outcomes
must be measured.

## Risk-based and integrated kidney care

Risk-based arrangements can give DaVita an opportunity to earn from preventing
avoidable cost, coordinating specialists, improving adherence, and shifting
care toward appropriate settings. Q2 2026 disclosed about `64,900` patients and
approximately `$5.8B` of annualized medical spend inside these arrangements.

That spend is not revenue or owner cash. The relevant bridge is:

`covered patients × risk payment or shared savings -> clinical and care-
coordination cost -> total medical claims and utilization -> reserves,
settlements, quality measures, and capital -> distributable cash`

Risk-based care introduces claims that do not sit in the same way inside a
simple treatment fee:

- patient disease severity may be worse than expected;
- medical costs may rise faster than the payment rate;
- claims and quality results can arrive after the reporting period;
- shared savings may be delayed, estimated, or reconciled later; and
- regulatory and contract terms can change the economics.

The investment analysis should never add annualized medical spend to revenue or
compare it directly with operating cash. It is an exposure and opportunity
measure that needs a separate medical-cost and settlement bridge.

## Earnings-quality and financial-engineering tests

### 1. Revenue per treatment can hide payer and mix changes

Track commercial, Medicare, Medicaid, and other payer mix, rate updates,
supplemental payments, denials, collections, and patient responsibility. A
higher average payment can reflect mix or temporary policy effects rather than
durable pricing power.

### 2. Treatment growth is not necessarily profit growth

More treatments can require more technicians, nurses, facilities, supplies,
transport, and equipment. Compare treatment growth with cost per treatment,
labor utilization, clinic margin, quality, and maintenance capital.

### 3. Risk-based medical spend is not free float

The `$5.8B` annualized medical spend under risk arrangements is not cash that
belongs to DaVita. Test incurred medical cost, claims development, payment
timing, shared savings, reserves, quality results, and capital requirements.

### 4. Home dialysis can move cost rather than remove it

Home treatment may lower facility intensity but increases training, equipment,
supplies, logistics, caregiver, and adherence requirements. Track total cost
per successful treatment and patient outcome, not only the percentage of
patients using home care.

### 5. Quality and regulatory costs are part of the model

Infection control, staffing, clinical documentation, patient safety, and
regulatory compliance are not optional growth expenses. A margin improvement
that depends on weaker quality or delayed maintenance is not durable owner
cash.

### 6. Acquisitions and capital returns require a common-owner bridge

DaVita can use cash for clinics, care platforms, debt reduction, and buybacks.
The correct comparison is acquired or repurchased value against maintenance
capital, balance-sheet resilience, risk-based obligations, and the return on
existing treatment capacity.

## Lyn Alden lens: essential demand, policy liquidity, and durability

Kidney failure treatment is less discretionary than most healthcare services.
That supports volume durability, but the cash flow is still mediated by
government and commercial reimbursement, staffing, facilities, and policy.

The macro and liquidity risks are:

- reimbursement rates and supplemental-payment policy;
- labor availability, wages, benefits, and clinician retention;
- patient transportation, household support, and adherence;
- inflation in supplies, drugs, utilities, and equipment;
- payer consolidation and collection timing;
- government budget and healthcare-policy changes; and
- capital-market conditions affecting debt, acquisitions, and buybacks.

The durable control point is not merely that patients need dialysis. It is the
combination of local treatment density, trained staff, clinical process,
equipment, patient trust, payer contracting, and the ability to coordinate a
long-duration disease pathway. That control can still produce weak returns if
the reimbursement or labor burden rises faster than treatment contribution.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers and lifecycle

The narrative should become:

`patient base × treatment cadence × reimbursement per treatment -> recurring
contribution -> labor, facilities, supplies, quality, corporate cost, and
working capital -> maintenance and growth capital -> owner cash`

The model should separate:

1. mature in-center dialysis from new-center growth;
2. home dialysis from facility treatment;
3. treatment fees from integrated-care and risk-based economics;
4. payer rate and mix from actual volume growth;
5. core operating margin from value-based medical-cost risk;
6. maintenance equipment and facility capital from acquisitions; and
7. recurring clinical investment from one-time restructuring or integration.

DaVita is a mature treatment network with optionality in care coordination.
Reinvestment creates value if new clinics, home-support capacity, technology,
and integrated-care programs reduce total cost or improve durable treatment
contribution above their full labor, quality, capital, and risk cost.

A valuation should use normalized common-owner cash after maintenance capital,
payer timing, risk-based medical costs, debt, and acquisition normalization. It
should not capitalize annualized medical spend or adjusted earnings without the
claims and capital bridge.

## Management incentives and thesis breakers

Management has reason to emphasize treatments per day, revenue per treatment,
adjusted operating income, adjusted EPS, risk-based patient count, annualized
medical spend, home-dialysis adoption, and care-model expansion. Pair each with
cost per treatment, quality, collections, medical claims, cash, capex, debt,
interest, acquisitions, and diluted shares.

The thesis weakens if:

- treatment volume grows while revenue per treatment or contribution falls;
- reimbursement increases are offset by labor, supply, facility, or quality
  costs;
- payer mix, denials, bad debt, or supplemental-payment timing weakens cash;
- home dialysis grows without better total cost, adherence, or outcomes;
- risk-based medical costs exceed payment or shared-savings assumptions;
- patient quality, staffing, compliance, or clinic reliability deteriorates;
- acquisitions consume cash without higher normalized per-share returns; or
- buybacks or leverage reduce resilience while maintenance and clinical needs
  remain high.
