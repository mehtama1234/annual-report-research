# Digital Realty: hyperscale capacity and lease visibility versus financing burden

## Bottom Line

Digital Realty controls a global data-center and connectivity platform serving
cloud providers, enterprises, networks, and digital ecosystems. The control
point is powered capacity plus the ability to turn that capacity into long-term
leases and interconnection relationships. It is a useful counterpoint to
Equinix: Equinix emphasizes ecosystem density and interconnection; Digital
Realty emphasizes hyperscale capacity, powered land, development, and private
capital.

The FY2025 evidence supports strong demand. Core FFO per diluted share was
$7.39, 4Q25 bookings represented $400M of annualized GAAP rent at 100% share,
year-end backlog was $817M at Digital Realty's share, and the company guided
to $3.25–$3.75B of 2026 development capex with 10%+ stabilized yields. But the
economic burden is substantial: total revenue was approximately $6.1B, the
company carries roughly $18.4B of debt at year-end, and the model requires
large development capex, leasing costs, power, construction, and external
capital.

The current conclusion is **control-with-burden test**. Hyperscale demand and
backlog are valuable leading indicators, but the investment case requires
lease-up, yield, financing, and per-share FFO proof after recurring capex and
capital-market claims.

## How to read the company

`cloud and enterprise demand -> powered land/data-center capacity -> signed lease -> construction and energization -> rent/NOI/FFO -> recurring capex, dividends, debt, and next development`

Digital Realty's PlatformDIGITAL combines data centers, colocation,
interconnection, and connected data communities. It owns, leases, develops,
and invests through joint ventures and funds. The control point is not simply
square footage; it is the availability of strategically located, powered,
connected capacity that customers can occupy at scale.

The business does not control hyperscaler budgets, power availability,
construction costs, interest rates, lease commencement dates, customer
concentration, or the technology that determines how much compute is required.
Private-capital structures can reduce parent funding needs, but they also split
ownership, promote economics, debt, and future cash flows.

The packet's Q4 2025 release reports $1.6B quarterly revenue, $658M FFO,

These are useful visibility measures, but the lag matters. The 4Q25 weighted-

months. Revenue and FFO cannot be treated as if every booked megawatt is
already earning rent.

## Earnings quality and forensic review

### 1. Core FFO is useful, but management itself identifies its limits

Digital Realty defines FFO by excluding real-estate depreciation, impairments,
and gains/losses on property dispositions. Core FFO excludes additional items;
AFFO then adjusts for non-real-estate depreciation, financing amortization,
SBC, straight-line rent, leasing commissions, and recurring capex.

The company explicitly warns that FFO excludes depreciation and does not capture
the level of capital expenditures and capitalized leasing commissions needed to
maintain operating performance. Its 2026 release also states that Core FFO
should supplement—not replace—GAAP net income. This is an important built-in
warning against treating per-share FFO as unconstrained owner cash.

The required bridge is:

`GAAP income -> FFO -> Core FFO -> AFFO -> recurring capex/leasing costs -> growth capex -> debt/equity/JV claims -> per-share cash`

### 2. Backlog converts only after construction and lease commencement

The year-end backlog of $817M at Digital Realty's share is signed but not yet
commenced rent. 4Q25 bookings were $175M at Digital Realty's share, and the
weighted-average commencement lag was eight months. The company also disclosed
2026 development capex of $3.25–$3.75B net of partner contributions and
expected stabilized yields above 10%.

This creates a clean Damodaran reinvestment test: compare the cash cost and time
to build/energize each project with contractual rent, operating cost, financing,
and stabilized yield. Backlog can be valuable, but its return depends on power,
customer acceptance, construction completion, and the absence of cost overruns.

### 3. Private capital can improve returns or hide economic leverage

Digital Realty contributed development projects and operating assets to funds
and joint ventures, receiving capital while retaining an interest and operating
role. These transactions can recycle capital and reduce parent-level funding.
They can also shift development capex, debt, promote economics, and non-
controlling interests away from the simple consolidated view.

For every JV or fund, capture Digital Realty's ownership percentage, partner
capital, debt, guarantees, management fees, promote/earn-out economics,
contributions, distributions, and the share of future rent and capex. The
relevant question is whether private capital lowers the cost of growth or
merely changes where the burden is reported.

### 4. Leasing, power, and maintenance costs are real recurring claims

Digital Realty's 2026 outlook includes $400–$425M of recurring capex plus
capitalized leasing costs, $30–$35M of other nonrecurring capex, and $3.25–$3.75B
of development capex. The release describes power capacity, utilization,
renewal increases, occupancy, same-capital cash NOI, and development yields as
separate measures.

The forensic test is whether rent growth exceeds power, labor, property-tax,
insurance, maintenance, leasing, and interest costs after accounting for
customer incentives and the time value of construction capital.

### 5. FFO growth can coexist with dilution and debt growth

At year-end 2025, Digital Realty had approximately $18.4B of debt and a net-
debt-to-adjusted-EBITDA ratio of 4.9x. It issued stock through its ATM program,
issued senior notes, repaid debt, paid dividends, and used private-capital
transactions to finance growth. Its equity structure includes operating-
partnership units and preferred instruments that belong in the per-share bridge.

The next proxy and annual filing should compare Core FFO growth with diluted
shares/units, SBC, preferred distributions, debt, interest, and the amount of
capital raised to fund development. A rising FFO/share is stronger evidence
than total FFO, but it still does not establish that growth was earned without
increasing financial risk.

## Alden lens: digital demand with a real-estate liquidity cycle

Digital Realty benefits from durable digitalization, cloud migration, AI,
data gravity, and the need for connected infrastructure. These trends can
support long-lived demand, but the business remains exposed to rates, power,
construction, refinancing, and capital-market access.

The Alden stress case is a period in which hyperscalers delay deployments,
power becomes scarce, construction costs rise, and debt refinances at higher
rates. The backlog may remain signed but commence later; development yields may
compress; and equity issuance may be required at unattractive prices. A durable
platform must preserve liquidity without sacrificing the network or using
accounting adjustments to conceal the cost of carrying vacant capacity.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers and valuation

The economic decomposition is:

`powered land -> development capex -> leased capacity -> rent/NOI -> FFO/AFFO -> recurring capex and leasing costs -> debt/JV claims -> per-share value`

At the September 11, 2026 snapshot, DLR traded at $188.58 and the provider
equity-value field was approximately $68.2B. Against FY2025 Core FFO of roughly
$2.56B, the screen is about 26.7x Core FFO. This is not a price target, and
Core FFO is not the same as owner cash.

The market must therefore believe that 2026 development capex earns the stated
10%+ stabilized yields, lease commencement occurs on schedule, same-capital
cash NOI remains healthy, and financing does not absorb the incremental value.
Reverse valuation cases should vary:

- backlog conversion, lease commencement lag, occupancy, and renewal pricing;
- power availability, construction cost, utilization, and stabilized yields;
- recurring capex, leasing commissions, and customer incentives;
- JV/fund ownership, partner contributions, promote economics, and NCI;
- debt maturities, interest, preferred claims, ATM issuance, and dilution; and
- hyperscale concentration, cloud alternatives, and the durability of AI-led
  capacity demand.

## Incentives and possible financial-engineering risks

Management and employees have natural incentives tied to Core FFO/share,
adjusted EBITDA, bookings, backlog, development yield, occupancy, same-capital
NOI, asset recycling, and shareholder distributions. These are useful operating
metrics but can look stronger when growth capex is financed by partners, when
lease commencement is deferred, or when recurring capital claims are treated
as adjustments.

The specific questions are:

- Does Core FFO/AFFO adequately reflect recurring capex, leasing commissions,
  SBC, and JV capital requirements?
- Do bookings and backlog convert to rent and cash at the promised yield and
  timing?
- Do asset contributions and fund transactions create value per share after
  promote, partner capital, and retained obligations?
- Are asset sales or deconsolidations supplying cash that masks weak operating
  conversion?
- Does the company raise debt/equity faster than stabilized NOI and FFO/share?
- Do impairments, transaction costs, restructuring, and non-core adjustments
  recur as part of ordinary expansion?

These are accounting and capital-allocation questions, not allegations of
wrongdoing.

## What would prove this wrong

Downgrade the capacity thesis if future filings show:

1. backlog and bookings rise but commencement is delayed, canceled, or
   repriced;
2. development yields fall below the cost of capital after power, construction,
   leasing, and financing costs;
3. recurring capex and leasing costs consume an increasing share of AFFO;
4. occupancy, renewal pricing, or same-capital cash NOI weaken despite AI
   demand headlines;
5. private-capital/JV structures require unexpected guarantees, partner funding,
   or unfavorable promote economics;
6. debt, preferred claims, and dilution rise faster than FFO/share;
7. hyperscaler concentration or customer alternatives reduce bargaining power;
   or
8. asset sales, deconsolidations, or non-GAAP adjustments are required to fund
   ordinary dividends and growth.

- **Filing fact:** FY2025 occupancy, bookings, Core FFO/AFFO, operating cash,
  capex, power costs, debt, JV/VIE exposure, and dilution are anchored to the
  annual filing.
- **Disclosed/packet-backed:** backlog, powered-shell pipeline, and AI demand
  claims are company-reported visibility evidence rather than realized yield.
- **Inference:** Digital Realty controls powered digital real estate and customer
  connectivity, while utilities, construction, tenants, partners, and capital
  markets carry or transmit much of the physical and financing burden.
- **Unresolved:** the next filing must show lease commencement, stabilized yield,
  recurring capex, JV obligations, power cost, and FFO/share after dilution. A
  larger backlog with slower lease-up or rising external funding would weaken the
  data-center thesis.
