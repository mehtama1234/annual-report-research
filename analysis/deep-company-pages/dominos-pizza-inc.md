# Domino's Pizza — ordering flow, franchise density, and owner cash

Date: `2026-09-14`. Primary evidence: [Domino's company packet](../../extracted/services/restaurants/dominos-pizza-inc/company-packet.md) and [Domino's source ledger](../../extracted/services/restaurants/dominos-pizza-inc/source-ledger.md).

## Investment question

Domino's is a delivery-and-carryout restaurant system with three connected
engines: franchise royalties, supply-chain sales, and digital ordering. It
does not earn the same economics as a company-operated restaurant. Franchisees
fund most local labor, rent, delivery execution, and store-level capital.
Domino's corporate entity controls the brand, technology, ordering flow,
franchise standards, supply centers, and a large global store network.

The question is whether this control point produces durable common cash after
technology, supply-chain investment, franchisee support, debt, stock
compensation, buybacks, and the health of the franchise system.

## Control point and operating sequence

`consumer need for convenient food -> digital order -> store preparation and
delivery/carryout -> franchisee sales -> royalty and supply-chain revenue ->
brand/technology support -> common-owner cash`

More than `85%` of U.S. retail sales came through digital channels in FY2025.
Domino's also disclosed a `$0.375` per-digital-transaction technology fee in
fiscal 2025. Digital penetration is important because it gives the company
more direct ordering data and reduces dependence on third-party ordering, but
it still requires reliable stores, drivers, routing, payment, customer service,
and food quality.

The corporate company controls the consumer interface and the franchise
system. Franchisees supply local labor, real estate, store execution, and much
of the delivery service. Supply centers add another control point: purchasing,
manufacturing, distribution, and product availability can improve consistency
and support franchisee economics.

The latest quarter changes the emphasis. The system is still expanding, but
the next dollar of value depends more on orders, franchisee health, and supply-
chain margin than on simply adding stores.

## Three economic engines

### 1. Franchise royalties and fees

Franchise royalties are linked to franchisee retail sales and store count.
Corporate Domino's can grow fee revenue with limited direct store labor, but
franchisee returns determine whether the network can open, remodel, staff, and
maintain stores.

Track same-store sales, transaction count, average ticket, franchisee margin,
closures, openings, store-level debt, delivery cost, labor, and royalty
collection. A corporate royalty can look resilient for a period while local
operators lose money.

### 2. Supply chain

Domino's sells ingredients, equipment, and other supplies to franchisees. This
creates a physical distribution and manufacturing burden that a pure royalty
company would not carry. It can also improve consistency, purchasing power,
availability, and franchisee dependence.

The supply-chain test is:

`franchisee store volume -> center volume -> gross margin -> purchasing,
manufacturing, inventory, freight, labor, and facility cost -> cash`

Supply-chain revenue is not pure royalty revenue. It carries inventory,
commodity, labor, facility, transportation, and capacity risk. Strong center
margin may support the system but can be pressured if store volume weakens or
food costs and freight move sharply.

### 3. Digital ordering and technology

Digital ordering can improve convenience, customer data, personalization,
order accuracy, and direct customer access. It can also reduce the economic
share paid to aggregators, but technology requires software, cybersecurity,
payment processing, support, and continuous product investment.

The disclosed technology fee makes the economics testable. Ask whether the fee
improves corporate cash after technology spending and whether franchisees see
enough conversion and order efficiency to remain healthy.

## Value, affordability, and routine demand

Pizza delivery and carryout can be a recurring, affordable household choice.
The customer is buying convenience, predictable price, speed, and a familiar
meal. That can be more resilient than higher-ticket restaurant occasions, but
the customer remains sensitive to promotions, delivery fees, employment, and
food inflation.

Same-store sales must be split into transactions and ticket. A higher ticket
can reflect price rather than more demand. Promotions can protect traffic but
reduce franchisee and supply-chain margins. Digital adoption can increase
convenience but also make price comparison and promotional competition easier.

## Cash conversion and earnings quality

Domino's corporate model can produce strong operating leverage because stores
are largely franchised. The analysis must still charge the costs needed to
maintain the control point:

- technology, digital ordering, payment, and cybersecurity;
- supply-center and manufacturing capital;
- food, equipment, freight, and inventory;
- franchisee support, quality, and brand standards;
- acquisitions, investments, and remeasurement effects;
- debt and interest;
- stock compensation and diluted shares; and
- buybacks made while franchisee or corporate liquidity is changing.

The key forensic questions are:

1. Does operating cash flow convert after supply-chain working capital and
   maintenance capex?
2. Are supply-chain margins stable through food-cost and freight cycles?
3. Is digital growth reducing acquisition and aggregator cost, or merely moving
   costs into technology and promotions?
4. Do adjusted results exclude recurring remeasurement, restructuring,
   technology, or franchisee-support costs?
5. Are buybacks funded from normalized cash after debt and system investment?
6. Does the corporate company invest enough in stores, quality, and delivery to
   keep franchisee economics healthy?

Revenue and EPS are incomplete. The owner-cash bridge is:

`royalties + supply-chain cash + technology fees -> corporate labor, technology,
supply, facilities, support, taxes, interest, and working capital -> capex,
acquisitions, SBC, debt, and buybacks -> common cash per diluted share`

## DPC Dash and investment-accounting noise

The Q1 2026 packet notes that revenue and operating income grew while net
income declined because of an unfavorable change in remeasurement gains and
losses tied to DPC Dash. This is a useful quality test. Remeasurement can be a
legitimate accounting consequence of an investment, but it means reported net
income is not a clean measure of the core franchise and supply-chain operation.

Track investment carrying value, remeasurement gains/losses, cash received,
ownership changes, and whether the investment supports the franchise system.
Do not treat a gain as operating royalty cash or a loss as proof that the core
restaurant system deteriorated.

## Macro and liquidity lens

Domino's is exposed to household affordability, food costs, labor availability,
delivery economics, fuel, store financing, and the health of small-business
franchisees. Its value positioning and low-ticket meal can support demand in a
weaker consumer environment, but franchisees may have less liquidity to open
stores or absorb wage and food-cost pressure.

The system is more resilient when digital direct ordering, store density,
supply-chain scale, and transaction frequency reinforce each other. It is more
fragile when same-store sales weaken, promotions increase, delivery labor costs
rise, and franchisee debt or closures limit store growth.

## Damodaran narrative-to-numbers bridge

The narrative is “technology-enabled, asset-light, global pizza network.”
Translate it into:

`transactions × average ticket -> franchisee sales -> royalty rate and store
growth; plus store volume × supply-center margin -> corporate gross profit;
less technology, food, freight, labor, facilities, support, capex, debt, SBC,
and buybacks`

Forecast separately:

- U.S. and international transactions and ticket;
- price, promotion, and value mix;
- franchisee versus corporate stores;
- store openings, closures, and returns;
- supply-chain volume and gross margin;
- digital mix and technology-fee economics;
- aggregator dependence and direct customer acquisition;
- food, labor, freight, and facility investment; and
- cash per diluted share.

The lifecycle question is whether store density and digital convenience still
create incremental returns or whether mature markets require more promotions,
capital, and franchisee support to defend the same sales.

## Valuation framing

Value Domino's through three distinct components:

1. recurring franchise royalty and fee cash after brand support;
2. supply-chain cash after inventory, facilities, manufacturing, freight, and
   maintenance capital; and
3. digital and technology value after software, payments, support, and
   customer-acquisition cost.

The market should not capitalize system-wide retail sales as corporate revenue.
It should not value supply-chain gross profit like a pure royalty stream. A
high valuation requires evidence that same-store transactions, store growth,
franchisee returns, supply-chain margin, and digital direct behavior remain
healthy through a full food, labor, and consumer cycle.

### Initial owner-cash screen — 2026-09-14

The FY2025 Form 10-K reports `$792.1M` of operating cash flow and `$120.6M`
of PP&E purchases. The reported residual is about `$671.5M`; after `$44.6M`
of stock compensation, the initial common-owner measure is about `$626.9M`.
The comparable residuals after stock compensation were approximately `$447.8M`
in FY2023, `$468.8M` in FY2024, and `$626.9M` in FY2025.

FY2025 cash dividends were about `$236.9M` and common-stock repurchases about
`$357.7M`, together nearly equal to the SBC-adjusted residual. That does not
make the buybacks wrong, but it means the franchise platform and supply chain
must fund maintenance and support before the full residual is treated as
distributable. The analysis must also keep the structured debt and the
franchisee's own financing burden visible.

A finance quote retrieved at `2026-09-15 00:15 UTC` showed a share price of
`$323.77` and equity value of approximately `$10.711B`. This is a dated market
input, not filing evidence or a price target.

| Screen | Bear | Base | Bull |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Normalized common-owner cash | `$450M` | `$650M` | `$850M` |
| Equity cash multiple | `14x` | `18x` | `22x` |
| Implied equity value | `$6.300B` | `$11.700B` | `$18.700B` |

The base case assumes transaction and store growth remain healthy, supply-chain
cash continues to convert, and support costs do not rise faster than royalties.
The bear case assumes weaker transactions, franchisee stress, promotions, and
food or labor cost pressure. The bull case requires durable store density,
direct digital behavior, and supply-chain margin without higher leverage or
corporate support. The dated market value is close to the base case; the
market therefore needs evidence that the 2025 cash improvement is repeatable.
This screen is not a price target.

## Management incentives and thesis breakers

Management can emphasize global retail sales, same-store sales, digital mix,
store growth, operating income, EPS, technology, and supply-chain performance.
Pair each with transactions, ticket, franchisee returns, store closures,
inventory, capex, debt, DPC Dash remeasurement, SBC, and diluted shares.

The thesis breaks if:

- same-store sales growth relies on price and promotions while transactions
  weaken;
- franchisee closures, debt stress, or underinvestment rise;
- store growth produces lower returns or requires larger corporate support;
- supply-chain revenue grows while inventory, freight, labor, or facility costs
  consume gross profit;
- digital adoption increases but direct customer acquisition and technology
  costs rise faster than retained value;
- aggregator competition reduces order economics or customer ownership;
- investment remeasurement and other non-operating items obscure core cash; or
- debt-funded buybacks continue while normalized common cash per share fails to
  improve.
