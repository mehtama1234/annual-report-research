# DoorDash — local-commerce coordination, delivery labor, and owner cash

As of 2026-09-14. FY2025 figures are filed figures unless explicitly
identified otherwise; dollar amounts are in millions unless stated otherwise.

## How to read this memo

DoorDash operates several related systems. Its Marketplace brings consumers,
merchants, and Dashers together for orders. Its membership programs encourage
repeat use. Its advertising products sell merchants access to demand. Its
Commerce Platform, including Drive, provides delivery or ordering tools that
can serve demand generated outside the DoorDash consumer app.

Marketplace GOV is the value of orders completed on the marketplaces,
including taxes, tips, applicable consumer fees, and membership fees. It is an
activity denominator, not DoorDash revenue and not DoorDash profit. Revenue is
primarily merchant commissions, consumer fees, membership fees, advertising,
and Commerce Platform fees, after deductions such as Dasher payouts, refunds,
credits, and promotions.

Contribution Profit is a company-defined measure that includes direct order
economics but adds back stock compensation, certain payroll taxes, allocated
overhead, and selected depreciation. Adjusted EBITDA excludes still more
items, including stock compensation, depreciation, interest, taxes, legal and
regulatory items, restructuring, and transaction costs. These measures help
show operating trends, but they do not equal cash available to common owners.

## Investment question

The central question is whether DoorDash can own the local-commerce demand
relationship and earn durable cash after paying for delivery labor, merchant
acquisition, refunds, customer support, payment processing, insurance and
regulatory requirements, software, international integration, stock
compensation, acquisitions, and repurchases.

The company has a potentially valuable control point because it coordinates a
frequent consumer need, a fragmented merchant base, and a geographically
distributed delivery supply. The control point is not secure merely because
orders are growing. Consumers can use merchant-owned channels, restaurants'
own delivery, Uber, Instacart, grocery chains, or local alternatives. Merchants
can resist commissions or use several channels. Dashers can change platforms
if net earnings, flexibility, or order density deteriorate.

## What DoorDash controls

DoorDash controls five connected relationships:

1. **Consumer demand:** search, selection, checkout, tracking, payment, and
   repeat convenience behavior.
2. **Merchant demand access:** a merchant can reach consumers without building
   the same discovery, payment, delivery, and support system itself.
3. **Dasher coordination:** the platform routes orders to available delivery
   workers and manages timing, batching, payout, and service information.
4. **Membership:** DashPass, Wolt+, and Deliveroo Plus can reduce the friction
   of placing another order and increase order frequency.
5. **Merchant software and advertising:** Drive, order tools, sponsored
   placements, data, and operational services can extend the relationship
   beyond orders generated in the DoorDash app.

The strongest control point is local density. More consumers can attract more
merchants; more merchants can improve selection; more orders can improve
Dasher utilization and delivery availability. That relationship must be
tested at the city, category, and cohort level because national GOV can grow
while individual markets become more expensive to serve.

The Q2 release says U.S. restaurant GOV growth accelerated slightly with strong
DashPass membership, while U.S. grocery and retail grew with improved unit
economics. International unit economics also improved, and Deliveroo growth
accelerated. These are useful operating claims, but the filing does not yet
provide a complete cohort-level bridge showing contribution after local
marketing, Dasher incentives, refunds, insurance, and corporate technology.

The acquisition adjustment matters. Excluding Deliveroo, Q2 orders rose 17%,
GOV rose 23%, and revenue rose 24%. Reported growth therefore overstates the
organic growth rate of the pre-existing DoorDash system. The acquisition may
still be strategically correct, but its value has to be measured through
incremental orders, merchant retention, shared technology cost, unit economics,
and cash returns rather than reported consolidation alone.

## Unit economics and denominator control

The first question is who pays for each part of an order. DoorDash's revenue
is after Dasher payout, refunds, credits, and promotions in the company's
revenue presentation. That creates a useful retained-revenue measure, but it
also means the analyst must inspect the amount of consumer and merchant value
used to produce each order.

Track these measures together:

- GOV per order and revenue per order;
- contribution profit per order after direct fulfillment and sales costs;
- Dasher earnings, incentives, wait time, and delivery availability;
- merchant commission, merchant churn, and advertising spend;
- refunds, credits, order defects, support contacts, and insurance cost;
- membership order frequency and the subsidy cost of member benefits;
- city-level order density and delivery time;
- organic growth excluding Deliveroo and other acquisitions.

Q2 GOV rose 36% while orders rose 27%, so the average GOV per order increased.
That is not automatically positive. Higher order value can improve revenue per
delivery, or it can reflect category mix, inflation, or a larger share of
orders where DoorDash carries more service cost. The next filings should
separate price, order frequency, new consumers, existing-consumer retention,
category, geography, and acquisition effects.

## Labor, insurance, and regulatory burden

DoorDash does not employ every Dasher as a traditional employee, but it still
depends on delivery labor being available at an acceptable total cost. The
economic burden can appear through Dasher pay, incentives, insurance, legal
reserves, worker-classification rules, local minimum-pay requirements, safety
programs, and customer refunds.

The critical question is not whether a platform reports a high contribution
margin. It is whether the delivery supply remains available after the worker's
net earnings and the company's total service obligations are accounted for.
If regulation or competition requires higher provider compensation, the cost
can be passed to consumers, charged to merchants, absorbed by DoorDash, or
reduced through lower service quality. Each outcome affects demand or platform
control.

The company also operates across jurisdictions with different rules for
delivery workers, fees, taxes, privacy, competition, and consumer protection.
International scale adds market opportunity but also increases the number of
legal systems in which a marketplace promise must be delivered.

## Membership and advertising

Membership can improve economics if it increases order frequency enough to
cover the value of waived delivery fees, discounts, support, and other
benefits. The correct test is incremental contribution per member after
benefits and service cost, not member count alone.

Advertising can be attractive because DoorDash has transaction context,
merchant inventory, local demand, and purchase feedback. But sponsored
placement can become a fee for visibility that merchants must pay to preserve
traffic. Test advertising revenue against organic conversion, merchant return
on ad spend, merchant retention, consumer relevance, and contribution after
advertising technology and sales expense.

Membership and advertising can reinforce each other, but they can also hide
weakness in the underlying marketplace. A consumer may order because a fee is
waived, and a merchant may advertise because unpaid discovery has weakened.
The company needs to show that the extra activity remains profitable after
the benefit or ad spend is charged.

## Software, capital, and earnings quality

DoorDash capitalized $258M of software and website-development costs in the
first half of 2026 and recognized $564M of depreciation and amortization. The
company's FCF definition deducts capitalized software, which is useful, but
capitalization still shifts some development cost from the income statement to
future amortization. The analyst should compare capitalized development,
amortization, product headcount, and platform reliability over time.

Stock-based compensation was $580M in the first half, and $2.8B of unrecognized
expense remained for unvested restricted stock units at June 30, expected to
be recognized over a weighted-average period of 2.74 years. Adjusted EBITDA
and adjusted profit exclude this cost. Cash flow adds it back, while equity
issuance can transfer value from existing owners to employees. Buybacks reduce
that dilution only if repurchase prices and timing are justified by normalized
cash rather than the current growth narrative.

DoorDash repurchased $1.049B of stock through August 5 under a $5B
authorization. First-half FCF before SBC was $1.162B, so the buyback already
approaches the reported cash generation for the period. The correct owner
question is whether the company is returning excess cash after replacing the
shares issued for compensation and funding Deliveroo integration, software,
and regulatory obligations.

## Lyn Alden, Damodaran, and forensic synthesis

- **Lyn Alden lens:** DoorDash is sensitive to household liquidity, food and
  delivery affordability, fuel and labor costs, local employment, and the
  ability of merchants and couriers to remain economically active. Membership
  and higher frequency can improve utilization, but weak consumer liquidity
  can expose the platform's dependence on convenience spending.
- **Damodaran lens:** translate the local-commerce narrative into orders, GOV
  per order, net revenue margin, contribution per order, member frequency,
  merchant retention, delivery density, reinvestment, dilution, and normalized
  owner cash. Value the base marketplace separately from uncertain returns on
  international integration, autonomous delivery, AI, and new categories.
- **Forensic lens:** do not treat GOV as revenue, Contribution Profit as
  owner earnings, Adjusted EBITDA as cash, FCF as fully distributable, organic
  growth as reported growth after acquisitions, or SBC as free labor. Reconcile
  provider payouts, promotions, processor funds, capitalized software,
  acquisition costs, legal and regulatory items, and share count.

## Valuation framing

DoorDash is a growth-and-normalization valuation case. The current evidence
supports high activity growth and improving reported operating leverage, but it
does not yet establish a stable mature owner-cash margin. A valuation model
should use at least three cases:

1. **Base marketplace:** organic orders and GOV growth normalize, contribution
   margin improves gradually, and SBC remains a material owner-cost.
2. **Platform expansion:** memberships, advertising, Commerce Platform, and
   international integration produce higher contribution without proportional
   delivery or support cost.
3. **Burden case:** competition, regulation, labor cost, merchant resistance,
   or acquisition integration prevents margin expansion and requires continued
   equity compensation and investment.

The model should use cash after capitalized software, PP&E, acquisitions,
SBC, and normalized working capital. It should not capitalize the Q2 adjusted
EBITDA run rate without first explaining why $580M of first-half SBC and the
future cost of delivery and regulatory resilience can be excluded.

### Initial owner-cash screen — 2026-09-14

The FY2025 Form 10-K reports `$2.431B` of operating cash flow and `$257M` of
PP&E purchases. Before acquisitions, that is a residual of about `$2.174B`.
The filing also reports `$4.151B` of net cash paid for acquisitions, principally
the Deliveroo transaction, and `$1.051B` of stock-based compensation. The
residual after charging SBC but before the acquisition was therefore about
`$1.123B`; after the acquisition, the reported cash burden was negative about
`$3.028B` before any separate treatment of working capital or integration cost.

The prior residuals before acquisitions were about `$1.550B` in 2023, `$2.028B`
in 2024, and `$2.174B` in 2025. After stock compensation, they were roughly
`$462M`, `$929M`, and `$1.123B`. This is why the Deliveroo acquisition cannot be
left outside the valuation: the strategic case may be right, but the purchase
price and integration burden are claims on current owners.

A finance quote retrieved at `2026-09-15 00:15 UTC` showed a share price of
`$203.54` and equity value of approximately `$89.424B`. This is a dated market
input, not filing evidence or a price target.

| Screen | Bear | Base | Bull |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Normalized common-owner cash | `$1.000B` | `$3.000B` | `$5.000B` |
| Equity cash multiple | `20x` | `35x` | `50x` |
| Implied equity value | `$20.0B` | `$105.0B` | `$250.0B` |

The base case assumes the core marketplace retains the pre-acquisition cash
conversion, Deliveroo adds contribution rather than only reported activity,
and SBC falls as a percentage of retained cash. The bear case assumes higher
provider, insurance, regulation, or merchant costs and limited acquisition
payback. The bull case requires strong international integration, density,
advertising, membership, and organic growth without a proportional rise in
SBC or required capital. At the dated market value, the quote requires a
base-to-bull outcome; the multiple is carrying a large burden of proof. This
screen is not a price target.

## Evidence grades and thesis breakers

| Question | Current grade | What would upgrade or weaken it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Consumer and merchant control point | B+ | Higher repeat use and merchant retention without rising subsidies |
| Local density and delivery liquidity | B | City-level contribution, availability, and provider economics remain healthy |
| Marketplace monetization | B | Revenue and contribution per order improve without merchant or consumer loss |
| Membership economics | B- | Member frequency and contribution exceed waived-fee and service costs |
| Advertising economics | B- | Incremental merchant sales and retention exceed ad and sales cost |
| Organic growth | B- | Growth remains strong after excluding Deliveroo and other acquisition effects |
| Cash conversion | B- | OCF and FCF remain strong after SBC, software renewal, acquisitions, and working capital |
| Valuation margin of safety | C- | The dated market value requires a base-to-bull cash outcome after Deliveroo, SBC, software, and regulatory burden |
| Capital allocation and dilution | C+ | Repurchases exceed dilution at prices supported by normalized owner cash |

Thesis breakers:

1. Orders and GOV grow while contribution per order falls after Dasher pay,
   refunds, promotions, insurance, or support costs.
2. Merchant commission pressure or direct-order alternatives reduce retention
   and force DoorDash to subsidize demand.
3. Membership increases order frequency but destroys contribution after waived
   fees, discounts, delivery costs, and customer service.
4. Advertising grows because unpaid discovery weakens rather than because ads
   generate incremental merchant demand.
5. Deliveroo integration produces reported growth without organic order,
   merchant, or cash-return improvement.
6. Worker-classification, minimum-pay, insurance, or local fee regulation
   permanently raises fulfillment cost faster than prices or density improve.
7. Capitalized software, stock compensation, restructuring, and acquisition
   spending consume the cash presented as free cash flow.
8. Buybacks fail to offset dilution or are funded while the company still
   needs external capital for growth and platform integration.
