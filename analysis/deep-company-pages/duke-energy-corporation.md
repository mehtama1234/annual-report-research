# Duke Energy — regulated rate base, large-load growth, and financing dependence

## The investment question

Duke Energy owns regulated electric and natural-gas utilities serving millions of customers across several states. Its economic control point is the approved rate base: infrastructure placed in service can earn an allowed return if regulators find the spending prudent and customers can pay the resulting rates.

The investment question is not whether electricity demand exists. It is whether Duke can convert a very large capital program into collected revenue and common-shareholder returns without excessive regulatory delay, customer affordability pressure, debt, dilution, construction overruns, or stranded assets.

Related first-principles explanation: [Rate-base growth versus owner return](../first-principles/rate-base-growth-versus-owner-return.md).

## Filed cash bridge

Duke reported 2025 net cash from operating activities of $12.330B, compared with $12.328B in 2024 and $9.878B in 2023. Capital expenditures were $14.024B in 2025, $12.280B in 2024, and $12.604B in 2023.

| Fiscal year | Operating cash flow ($M) | Capital expenditures ($M) | Post-capex diagnostic ($M) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 2023 | 9,878 | 12,604 | (2,726) |
| 2024 | 12,328 | 12,280 | 48 |
| 2025 | 12,330 | 14,024 | (1,694) |

This negative 2025 diagnostic is not evidence that the regulated model is broken. It shows that Duke is investing ahead of current common-owner cash generation. The investment can become valuable only if the assets enter service, receive regulatory recovery, earn an allowed return, and generate cash after operating costs, interest, taxes, pension contributions, asset-retirement obligations, and future replacement capital.

## The growth claim

Duke serves approximately 8.7 million electric customers and 1.6 million natural-gas customers, with about 55,700 megawatts of energy capacity and approximately 26,400 employees. Management describes a $103B capital plan for 2026 through 2030, about 14 GW of new capacity targeted by 2031, and roughly 4.5 GW of data-center load secured under electric service agreements in the 2025 materials. Later 2026 materials described approximately 7.6 GW of secured data-center ESAs, about 5 GW under construction, and a late-stage pipeline around 15.4 GW.

These figures are commitments and pipeline measures, not collected revenue. The proof chain is: customer contract, credit support, interconnection approval, construction spending, energized load, regulatory treatment, billing, cash collection, and earned return. A signed service agreement can reduce risk, but it does not prove that the customer will arrive on schedule or that every required network and generation cost will be recovered.

The company broke ground on approximately 5 GW of new dispatchable generation and secured gas turbines through a GE Vernova partnership. This improves supply planning, but it creates fuel, construction, emissions, permitting, labor, equipment-delivery, and future-retirement obligations. Rate recovery is the mechanism that can make this capital economic; it is also the source of political and regulatory risk.

## Earnings quality and forensic questions

1. **Regulated earnings versus cash:** Adjusted EPS can grow before the related rate-base cash is collected. Reconcile earnings to operating cash flow, construction work in progress, regulatory assets, deferred costs, and rate-case timing.
2. **Working capital and tax credits:** 2025 operating cash included transferable tax-credit sale proceeds of approximately $723M. These proceeds are useful but should be separated from ordinary customer collections when judging recurring cash.
3. **Capital classification:** The $14.024B capital figure includes the physical build that supports future earnings. The next filings must distinguish maintenance, reliability, environmental, generation, transmission, distribution, and growth capital. Calling all spending “growth” does not make it accretive.
4. **Regulatory recovery:** Track the allowed return, equity layer, recovery mechanism, regulatory lag, disallowances, storm recovery, and customer bill impact in each major jurisdiction.
5. **Large-load economics:** Test minimum demand, take-or-pay provisions, credit support, cancellation rights, construction contributions, and who pays for network upgrades if a data-center project is delayed or reduced.
6. **Debt and dilution:** Duke financed a capital-intensive model with long-term debt, common equity, and asset sales. Higher rates can reduce the value of future cash flows and increase customer bills or equity funding requirements.
7. **Asset-retirement and environmental obligations:** Nuclear, coal, gas, ash, storm, and remediation liabilities can require cash even when the related asset is earning a regulated return.
8. **Management incentives:** Read the proxy for the relation between executive pay, adjusted EPS, rate-base growth, capital deployment, credit metrics, customer affordability, safety, and realized return on invested capital. Incentives tied mainly to EPS growth can favor larger capital plans before returns are proven.

## Management incentives and capital allocation

The proxy statement should establish whether annual and long-term awards reward
adjusted EPS, rate-base growth, operating cash flow, free cash flow, return on
equity, customer growth, reliability, safety, emissions, large-load additions,
credit metrics, share price, or dividend growth. Record definitions, weights,
threshold/target/maximum levels, and exclusions for storms, tax credits,
asset sales, regulatory items, acquisitions, construction, and financing.

The incentive tests are:

- Does a rate-base or capital-spending target count only assets placed in
  service and approved for recovery?
- Does an adjusted EPS target charge interest, equity issuance, regulatory lag,
  tax-credit timing, asset-retirement obligations, and customer affordability?
- Does a large-load target require customer credit support, construction
  contributions, energized load, and durable billing rather than a signed ESA?
- Are projects evaluated by earned return and per-share cash after construction
  cost, fuel, maintenance, emissions, and replacement obligations?
- Are dividends and repurchases evaluated after debt maturities, liquidity,
  credit ratios, and the full capital plan?

A rate-base or load-growth target can reward scale before recovery and customer
payment are visible. An operating-cash target can benefit from tax-credit sales,
working-capital timing, or customer advances. The proxy and future filings must
show whether management is accountable for earned return, credit resilience,
customer affordability, and cash per diluted share.

For each major data-center or generation project, compare management's stated
return with capital spent, customer contribution, in-service date, regulatory
treatment, billing, earned return, debt, and dilution. For each capital return,
compare cash paid with cash remaining after reliability, environmental, and
financing requirements.

## Balance-sheet resilience

Duke's $103B capital plan requires a continuing funding program. Track debt
maturities, interest expense, preferred or hybrid claims, common equity
issuance, credit ratings, liquidity, construction work in progress, regulatory
assets, customer contributions, tax-credit proceeds, and asset-retirement
obligations.

The stress sequence is:

`capital spending ahead of recovery -> debt, equity, or customer funding -> rate and bill pressure -> delayed load or regulatory resistance -> lower common-owner return`

Large-load contracts can reduce demand uncertainty but do not eliminate
construction, interconnection, customer-credit, power-price, or stranded-asset
risk. Tax-credit sales and asset sales can support current cash but should be
separated from ordinary customer collections and recurring earnings.

The utility can remain solvent while common-owner economics weaken through
higher funding cost, equity dilution, lower earned returns, or reduced dividend
capacity. The next filings should reconcile pro forma debt, interest, capital
sources, rate-base additions, large-load energization, regulatory recovery,
and cash per share.

## Lyn Alden lens

Electricity demand is relatively defensive, but the utility's financing structure is sensitive to interest rates, credit spreads, inflation, and the purchasing power of customers. A regulated monopoly can recover prudent costs over time, but bills still face political limits. A large capital plan is therefore both an opportunity and a claim on future customer and capital-market capacity.

Duke's multi-state footprint diversifies weather and regulatory exposure but does not remove it. The data-center load adds industrial growth and potentially better utilization, but the durability depends on customer credit, technology demand, construction schedules, and whether regulators allow the utility to charge the incremental cost to the growth customer rather than existing households.

## Damodaran lens and valuation

The narrative is “regulated infrastructure owner with visible load growth and a large rate-base runway.” The valuation must specify the allowed return, equity financing, rate-base growth, regulatory lag, earned ROE, cost of debt, customer growth, large-load conversion, tax credits, dividend payout, and terminal growth.

At a market value of approximately $93.0B, Duke is not being valued on current post-capex free cash flow. A simple cash screen illustrates the difference:

| Normalized common-owner cash | Multiple | Implied equity value |
|---:|---:|---:|
| $0B bear case | 12x | $0B |
| $1B base case | 16x | $16B |
| $3B strong case | 20x | $60B |

These outputs are deliberately not a price target. They show why a regulated utility requires a rate-base valuation in addition to an owner-cash screen. The market is paying for future regulated earnings, not simply 2025 cash after construction spending. That premium is justified only if capital is placed in service, recovered, financed prudently, and earns an acceptable return.

## Thesis breakers and next evidence

The thesis weakens if regulatory lag grows, allowed returns fall, large-load customers delay or avoid commitments, capital costs exceed estimates, debt and equity issuance dilute common returns, customer affordability limits recovery, construction spending rises faster than rate base, or tax-credit and asset-sale proceeds are repeatedly needed to support cash flow.

The next filings should answer: How much of the capital plan entered service? What portion received timely recovery? Did large-load customers make construction contributions and begin taking power? Did operating cash rise without tax-credit or working-capital support? Did debt metrics remain within target ranges? What is the earned return on recent capital, and who bears the cost if demand arrives late?
