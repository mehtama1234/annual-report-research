# Edison International — regulated growth under wildfire liability

Research date: 2026-09-14  
Evidence window: FY2025 and FY2026 Q2  
Ticker: EIX  
Evidence grade: A for reported results, rate base, capex, wildfire disclosures, and current guidance; B for the sufficiency of the Wildfire Fund, the recovery of future claims, regulatory treatment, and the long-run return on resilience spending.

## Bottom line

Edison International owns Southern California Edison, a regulated electric utility serving approximately 15 million people across Southern, Central, and Coastal California. Its control point is the regulated service territory, grid, reliability obligation, and ability to request recovery of prudent investment. Its burden is unusually severe: wildfire liability, insurance and self-insurance, resilience capital, affordability pressure, regulatory scrutiny, and the need to preserve access to debt and equity markets.

FY2025 rate base was $48.206B, capital expenditures were $6.729B, core earnings were $2.520B, and core EPS was $6.55. Q2 FY2026 GAAP EPS was $1.39 and core EPS was $1.54; management reaffirmed 2026 core EPS guidance of $5.90–$6.20 and 5–7% core EPS growth from 2025 to 2030. These figures are not enough to call the business low risk. The valuation depends on whether wildfire protection, claims, insurance, and regulatory recovery remain compatible with the cost of capital and with affordable customer rates.

## What Edison controls

The operating chain is:

`regulated service territory -> grid operations and reliability -> wildfire mitigation -> regulatory filing -> allowed recovery -> customer rates -> debt and equity funding`

Edison controls grid operations, safety programs, capital execution, regulatory filings, and risk prioritization. It can improve the probability of avoiding ignition and can direct mitigation toward circuits where the safety benefit is greatest.

Edison does not control weather, ignition conditions, third-party claims, the legal standard for utility liability, Wildfire Fund capacity, insurance pricing, CPUC decisions, affordability politics, interest rates, or the speed at which a resilience project becomes recoverable. A safer grid may reduce expected loss without producing a proportional immediate return.

The 2025 earnings increase contains material non-core history. Edison’s annual reconciliation identifies wildfire, tax, settlement, and other discrete items. The investor should not treat the GAAP-to-core difference as an accounting defect, but must test whether the excluded categories recur because wildfire and regulatory events are part of the operating environment.

## Rate base does not remove catastrophe risk

Edison can invest in distribution, transmission, hardening, vegetation management, system automation, and climate adaptation, then seek recovery through rates. But a project can be prudent and still leave shareholders exposed to claims, deductibles, self-insured retention, co-insurance, delays, or disallowance.

The correct sequence is:

`resilience spending -> lower expected ignition or damage -> regulatory review -> recovery -> allowed return`

Each link can fail. Spending may not prevent a fire. The regulator may determine that some costs were imprudent. Recovery may be delayed. Customer affordability may constrain rate increases. The Wildfire Fund may not cover all claims or may be insufficient under a severe event.

## Wildfire liability and financial engineering risks

Edison’s Q2 release lists uninsured wildfire costs, Wildfire Fund availability, clarified prudency standards, credit ratings, and access to capital markets as direct risks. These are financial risks, not only operational risks.

Core earnings exclude significant discrete items that management does not consider representative of ongoing earnings. For Edison, the excluded items include wildfire-related claims and recoveries, Wildfire Fund expense, settlement and insurance effects, taxes, and other items. A claim that a loss is non-core is not the same as proving that it will not recur. The right test is a multi-year bridge of GAAP earnings, core earnings, cash paid for claims, insurance recoveries, regulatory assets, securitization proceeds, and equity or debt funding.

At June 30, 2026, Edison reported $96.171B of assets, $2.855B of current regulatory assets, $12.966B of long-term regulatory assets, $2.479B of current debt, $1.521B of short-term debt, $3.797B of current long-term debt, and $808M of wildfire-related claims. The company also reported $805M of long-term insurance receivables. These balances require recovery and collection analysis; they are not equivalent to cash.

## Lyn Alden lens: catastrophe liquidity and credit durability

Edison’s regulated earnings can be durable in ordinary periods, but catastrophe risk creates a nonlinear liquidity test. A large fire can produce claims, legal expense, insurance gaps, credit pressure, and regulatory delay at the same time. The company must fund operations and grid investment while waiting for recoveries.

The balance-sheet question is whether Edison can withstand a severe wildfire season without issuing equity at a depressed price, losing credit access, cutting investment, or compromising the dividend. A regulated framework can distribute costs over time, but it cannot guarantee that every cost is recovered quickly or fully.

## Management incentives and capital allocation

The proxy statement should establish whether annual and long-term awards reward
core EPS, rate-base growth, operating cash flow, free cash flow, return on
equity, safety, reliability, wildfire mitigation, customer outcomes, credit
metrics, share price, or dividend growth. Record definitions, weights,
threshold/target/maximum levels, and exclusions for wildfire claims, insurance
recoveries, regulatory assets, securitization, storms, settlements, and other
discrete items.

The incentive tests are:

- Does a rate-base or capital-spending target count only investment placed in
  service and approved for recovery?
- Does core EPS exclude wildfire, insurance, regulatory, storm, or settlement
  costs that are part of the recurring operating environment?
- Does an earnings or dividend target charge interest, preferred claims,
  equity issuance, regulatory lag, and customer affordability?
- Are resilience projects evaluated by earned return and risk reduction after
  construction, insurance, and disallowance risk?
- Are capital returns made only after wildfire liquidity, debt maturities,
  credit ratios, and required grid investment are funded?

An incentive plan based mainly on core EPS or rate-base growth can reward scale
before the full cost of wildfire risk and financing is visible. A plan based on
cash per diluted share, earned return, safety, and credit resilience is more
complete only if it charges claims, regulatory lag, and required grid
investment. The proxy and future filings must show which design is actually in
place.

For each major resilience or load-growth project, compare management's stated
return with actual cost, completion, rate recovery, customer bill impact,
insurance and Wildfire Fund treatment, cash collection, and diluted shares.
For each capital return, compare cash paid with cash remaining after claims,
capital expenditure, debt, and preferred obligations.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers

The narrative is: Edison can grow earnings through grid investment, electrification, resilience, and California’s need for a safer clean-energy network.

That narrative must become measurable assumptions:

- rate-base growth placed in service and allowed return;
- resilience capex, maintenance capex, and cost per avoided risk;
- wildfire claims, insurance limits, self-insured retention, and Wildfire Fund recovery;
- regulatory asset balances, securitization, recovery timing, and disallowance;
- customer affordability, load growth, electrification, and political constraints;
- debt, preferred stock, interest expense, credit ratings, and equity access;
- core-to-GAAP adjustments and whether excluded categories recur;
- dividend payout, parent-company cash, SCE cash, and noncontrolling claims;
- terminal earnings after the current grid-hardening cycle.

Edison’s market value was approximately $21.672B on September 14, 2026. A simple core-earnings screen is:

| Normalized annual core-earnings screen | Multiple | Implied equity value |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $1.9B bear case | 8x | $15.2B |
| $2.5B base case | 9x | $22.5B |
| $3.0B strong case | 10x | $30.0B |

These are scenario outputs, not price targets. The base case requires wildfire protection and recovery mechanisms to remain credible, the $38–$41B capital program to earn an adequate return, and the company to preserve financing access and per-share earnings after claims, interest, preferred dividends, and dilution.

## Thesis breakers and next evidence

The thesis weakens if wildfire claims exceed insurance and Wildfire Fund recovery; if regulators disallow resilience or historical capital; if regulatory assets grow faster than collections; if credit ratings fall; if interest costs rise faster than core earnings; if equity issuance dilutes per-share growth; if affordability limits rate increases; if core earnings repeatedly exclude operating wildfire and regulatory costs; or if a severe event forces Edison to reduce necessary grid investment.

The next filings should answer: Did wildfire claims and insurance receivables move toward cash recovery? Did regulatory assets decline or grow? Did the capital program enter rate base at the expected return? Did debt and preferred obligations rise? Did core EPS growth survive after non-core items, claims, interest, and share count? Did the Wildfire Recovery Compensation Program change the company’s expected exposure?
