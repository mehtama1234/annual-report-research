# Essential Utilities — regulated water and gas networks, rate-base funding, and acquisition discipline

Date baseline: 2026-09-13  
Primary annual filing: [Essential Utilities FY2025 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/78128/000007812826000050/wtrg-20251231x10k.htm)  
FY2025 results: [Essential Utilities FY2025 results](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/78128/000155278126000071/e26072_ex99-1.htm)

## Bottom line

Essential Utilities owns regulated water, wastewater, and natural-gas systems.
Its customers receive essential services, but the company cannot simply set
any price it wants. State regulators decide which investments are prudent,
which costs can be recovered, what rates customers pay, and what return the
company may earn.

The control point is the regulated customer franchise and the physical network
that serves it. The economic engine is rate base: infrastructure that has been
placed in service and accepted for recovery can earn an allowed return over
time. Rate base is not cash and it is not automatically profitable. It must be
financed, built, approved, operated, and recovered from customers without
creating unaffordable bills or political resistance.

FY2025 net income was $616.4M, operating cash flow was $1.010B, capital
expenditures were $1.430B, and utility-system acquisitions were $57M. The
company invested a record $1.4B in infrastructure and received $46.2M of PFAS
class-action settlement proceeds in operating cash. Cash generation therefore
did not fully fund capital investment, and the settlement is not recurring
utility operating cash.

Essential is also pursuing a merger with American Water. Shareholder approval
was secured, but approval is not the same as completed integration or proven
value creation. The transaction creates potential scale in procurement,
operations, financing, and regulatory expertise, while also adding execution,
approval, customer-affordability, and stranded-cost risks.

Current judgment: Essential owns a durable essential-service franchise and a
large infrastructure reinvestment runway. The common-owner return depends on
timely rate recovery, financing access, earned returns, acquisition prices,
PFAS and pipe-replacement costs, gas exposure, merger execution, and the gap
between accounting earnings and cash available after required investment.

## What Essential controls

`regulated service territory -> water, wastewater, or gas network -> approved rates -> customer payments -> reinvestment and allowed return`

Essential controls service territories, utility operations, pipes, treatment
plants, meters, gas distribution, customer billing, operating expertise, and
the ability to propose capital programs and rate cases. Regulators control
allowed rates and returns. Customers control affordability and political
acceptance. Municipal sellers control acquisition opportunities and terms.

The control point is stronger where the company has a protected service
franchise, operating scale, compliance expertise, and local infrastructure
knowledge. It is weaker where regulators disallow spending, delay recovery,
reduce returns, or require customer protections that limit cash flow.

The screen is deliberately simple. It shows that operating cash did not cover

for a growing regulated utility only if the company can finance the gap,
place prudent assets into rate base, earn an adequate return, and collect the
result through future customer bills.

The financing need is therefore part of the business model. Debt and equity
issuance are not automatically signs of weakness, but they can reduce common
returns if rates rise, equity is issued below intrinsic value, or regulatory
recovery lags construction spending.

## Rate base is a future claim, not current cash

Rate base is the regulated value of utility infrastructure used to serve
customers. A regulator may allow the company to recover depreciation, taxes,
operating costs, and a return on the approved investment.

The investment sequence is:

1. the company spends cash on a pipe, plant, meter, treatment system, or gas asset;
2. the asset is placed in service and its cost is reviewed;
3. regulators approve recovery through a rate case or surcharge;
4. customers pay higher bills over time; and
5. the company earns a return after operating, financing, and compliance costs.

Failure at any step reduces the value of the capital plan. Construction in
progress, announced rate-base growth, and requested surcharges are not the same
as earned common cash.

## Water, wastewater, and gas have different burdens

### Water and wastewater

Water and wastewater systems require pipe replacement, treatment, storage,
meters, pumping, labor, testing, and compliance. PFAS treatment and detection
can add capital, operating cost, litigation, and regulatory uncertainty.

The demand is essential, but affordability constrains rate increases. Track
customer usage, arrears, service quality, leakage, compliance, pipe age,
capital completion, allowed returns, and recovery timing.

### Regulated natural gas

The gas segment has commodity-purchase and weather exposure in addition to
regulated network economics. Purchased-gas costs may be passed through, but
timing, customer bills, inventory, weather, safety, and methane-reduction
programs affect cash and customer acceptance.

Track weather-normalized volumes, purchased-gas recovery, gas inventory,
pipeline and distribution capex, safety incidents, methane programs, customer
arrears, and rate-case lag. Gas can diversify water but also adds a different
transition and commodity risk.

## Earnings quality and forensic tests

### 1. PFAS settlement cash should not be treated as recurring

Operating cash increased in 2025 partly because Essential received $46.2M of
PFAS class-action settlement proceeds. The settlement may help fund a real
obligation, but it is not the same as customer-paid utility revenue.

Separate settlement proceeds, insurance recoveries, regulatory assets, and
customer-funded recovery from recurring operating cash. Then estimate PFAS
treatment, testing, legal, remediation, and future operating costs that the
company or ratepayers must fund.

### 2. Capital investment is valuable only after recovery

Essential invested $1.430B in 2025. The company reported $92.6M of incremental
annual water revenue from rate awards or surcharges and $8.9M from gas rate
awards or surcharges during the year.

The next filing should reconcile capital placed in service, capital included
in rate base, approved return, actual earned return, regulatory lag, customer
contributions, and unrecovered construction. A large capital plan can grow
reported assets while producing weak common returns if financing and recovery
costs rise faster than allowed revenue.

### 3. Acquisition growth can expand rate base without adequate returns

Essential acquired approximately $550M of rate base and added more than
135,000 customers or equivalent dwelling units since 2015. It also had signed
agreements for systems serving more than 200,000 customer equivalents at an
approximate $300M purchase price.

Track acquisition price, rate base acquired, condition of the system, required
repair, regulatory approval, customer affordability, incremental opex, capex,
and actual earned return. Municipal consolidation can create value when scale
reduces cost and improves compliance; it can destroy value when the buyer pays
for assets that require more remediation than the tariff can recover.

### 4. Working capital can distort gas cash flow

Natural-gas commodity purchases can cause large inventory and payable swings.
Operating cash may rise because gas inventory is reduced or fall because the
company buys ahead of winter demand. The change does not necessarily show a
change in long-term utility profitability.

Reconcile cash flow with weather-normalized volumes, gas prices, inventory,
customer collections, purchased-gas adjustments, and settlement terms. Treat
working-capital releases as timing until they persist through a full seasonal
cycle.

### 5. Regulatory accounting can defer economic pain

Utilities may record regulatory assets when regulators permit future recovery
of costs. That accounting can be appropriate, but it creates a claim on future
customer bills. If recovery is denied, delayed, or limited by affordability,
the asset may be impaired or recovered over a longer period.

Track regulatory assets and liabilities, amortization, carrying costs, rate-case
decisions, disallowances, customer affordability programs, and whether the
allowed return covers the financing cost of deferred recovery.

### 6. Debt and equity fund the gap between cash and capital spending

The company’s capital spending exceeded operating cash flow in 2023, 2024,
and 2025 before considering dividends. Essential states that future capital
programs and acquisitions require internally generated funds plus debt and
equity financing.

Review debt maturities, interest, credit ratings, commercial paper, equity
issuance, dividend coverage, construction commitments, and rate-base growth.
The right question is not whether a utility has debt. It is whether the allowed
return and recovery timing produce enough cash to service that debt without
transferring too much value from common owners to lenders or new shareholders.

### 7. Merger economics need a clean baseline

The American Water merger may create operating and financing synergies, but
the combined company will also inherit regulatory proceedings, integration
costs, debt, customer-affordability constraints, and different state
commission relationships.

Before crediting synergies, identify one-time transaction costs, stranded
systems, employee retention, financing fees, required divestitures, rate-case
conditions, and the timing of realized savings. An announced merger is not a
cash return until the combined utility earns and retains the expected benefit.

## Alden macro and durability lens

Essential benefits from the essential nature of water, wastewater, and gas
service, but its liquidity is tied to interest rates, debt markets, customer
affordability, state politics, construction inflation, labor, weather, and
regulatory timing. A high-rate environment raises the cost of funding assets
before they enter rate base.

Water-quality and resilience spending can remain necessary during a downturn,
but customers and regulators may resist rapid bill increases. The company must
balance compliance, reliability, and affordability while maintaining access to
debt and equity capital.

## Damodaran narrative-to-numbers lens

The narrative is “Essential is a regulated essential-network compounder with a
water-plus-gas platform and consolidation opportunity.” The model must specify:

1. rate base growth, capital placed in service, and approved recovery;
2. allowed return, earned return, regulatory lag, and financing cost;
3. water, wastewater, and gas customer growth, usage, and affordability;
4. PFAS, pipe replacement, safety, environmental, and compliance spending;
5. municipal acquisition price, repair needs, integration, and earned return;
6. customer advances, contributions in aid of construction, and grants;
7. gas inventory, purchased-gas recovery, weather, and working capital;
8. debt, equity issuance, interest, dividend coverage, and credit ratings;
9. merger costs, synergies, conditions, and stranded obligations; and
10. normalized common cash after required infrastructure investment.

Essential is in a capital-intensive regulated-growth phase with merger exposure.
The valuation should reward reliable earned returns and recovery, not simply
the size of the announced capital plan or customer count.

## Valuation screen

The September 11, 2026 market snapshot showed equity value of approximately
$11.67B. The cases below use normalized common owner cash after maintenance and
growth capital, financing costs, PFAS and compliance reserves, and an allowance
for acquisition and merger obligations.

| Case | Normalized common owner cash | Equity cash multiple | Implied common equity value |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Bear | $350M | 18x | $6.3B |
| Base | $500M | 22x | $11.0B |
| Bull | $650M | 25x | $16.25B |

The current value is close to the base case. The base case requires timely rate
recovery, earned returns near allowed returns, manageable financing costs,
controlled PFAS spending, and merger benefits that do not require excessive
dilution. The bear case reflects regulatory lag, affordability limits, higher
interest costs, acquisition repair needs, or merger costs. The bull case
requires durable rate-base growth with efficient financing and measurable
operating synergies.

## Thesis breakers and next filings

The thesis weakens if:

- capital spending grows faster than assets placed in rate base;
- allowed recovery or earned returns fall below the cost of debt and equity;
- customer arrears and affordability resistance limit rate increases;
- PFAS, pipe, gas-safety, or remediation costs exceed recoverable amounts;
- acquisitions add customers but require disproportionate repair and capex;
- gas working-capital releases reverse into inventory and supplier funding;
- debt and equity issuance grow faster than common earnings and rate base;
- the American Water merger creates stranded costs, regulatory conditions, or weak synergies; or
- settlements, tax benefits, customer contributions, or regulatory deferrals are treated as recurring owner cash.

The next filings should show capital placed in service, rate-base additions,
allowed and earned returns, regulatory assets, rate-case lag, customer arrears,
PFAS spending and recovery, acquisition cohorts, gas working capital, debt,
equity issuance, merger costs, and cash per common share.

## Evidence grade

- Control point: **B+** — protected essential-service franchises and physical networks create durable demand, subject to regulators.
- Operating economics: **B** — allowed returns provide visibility, but execution, affordability, and financing determine realized returns.
- Cash conversion: **C+** — FY2025 operating cash was $1.010B against $1.430B of capital spending, with settlement and working-capital effects.
- Balance-sheet resilience: **B-** — the model depends on continuing debt and equity access while large investment programs continue.
- Capital allocation: **B-** — infrastructure spending is strategically necessary, but acquisition and merger discipline require proof.
- Valuation: **B-** — current value is near the base case and depends on timely regulatory recovery and merger execution.
