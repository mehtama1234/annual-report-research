# Exelon: Regulated Wires, Recovery Coverage, and the Cost of Rate-Base Growth

Date baseline: `2026-09-14`

Evidence base: Exelon's fiscal `2025` Form 10-K, fiscal `2025` Q4, fiscal
`2026` Q1 and Q2 results materials, and the company packet. The annual filing
is the controlling source for the cash-flow bridge. The dated scenario register
preserves a September 14, 2026 equity-value input; it is an expectations
screen, not a live quote or price target.

## Bottom line

Exelon owns six regulated transmission and distribution utilities serving almost
`11 million` customers. It is primarily a wires utility: its value comes from
delivering electricity reliably through approved networks, not from owning a
large merchant-generation portfolio.

The business has a clear control point. Customers need the local grid, and
regulators determine which investments may be recovered through rates. That
creates more earnings visibility than an uncontracted power plant, but it does
not create free cash. Exelon must finance large construction programs, wait for
regulatory recovery, maintain reliability, and keep bills affordable.

The fiscal 2025 cash bridge shows the burden clearly. Operating cash flow was
about `$6.25B` and capital expenditures were about `$8.53B`, leaving a mechanical
post-capex deficit of approximately `$2.28B` before dividends, debt service, and
other financing. The deficit is not automatically a value destruction signal for
a regulated utility; it means the investment thesis depends on future allowed
returns, financing access, regulatory recovery, and per-share discipline.

## 1. What Exelon controls

Exelon controls regulated transmission and distribution networks, local
customer relationships, system reliability, outage restoration, and the
execution of approved grid investment.

A rate base is the regulated value of utility assets on which the regulator may
allow the company to earn a return. Rate-base growth is not current profit. The
company first spends cash, places assets in service, obtains regulatory
approval, and collects the return over future customer bills.

The real control point is therefore a combination of:

- an essential local network that customers cannot easily replace;
- regulatory permission to recover prudent investment;
- reliability and operating performance;
- the ability to finance construction; and
- customer and political acceptance of the resulting bill increase.

## 2. Three-year cash and capital record

| Year | Operating cash flow | Capital expenditures | Mechanical cash after capex | Common dividends |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2023 | `$4.70B` | `$7.41B` | `($2.71B)` | `$1.43B` |
| 2024 | `$5.57B` | `$7.10B` | `($1.53B)` | `$1.52B` |
| 2025 | `$6.25B` | `$8.53B` | `($2.28B)` | `$1.62B` |

The mechanical residual is not Exelon's reported free-cash-flow definition. It
is a simple owner-cash screen that asks whether operating cash funded the asset
investment required in the period. For this company, it is more informative
than a standard software-style free-cash-flow multiple because capital spending
is the main mechanism through which future regulated earnings are created.

The next bridge is:

`operating cash -> construction spending -> assets placed in service -> rate recovery -> allowed return -> common cash per share`

The weak point can occur at any stage. A project can be delayed, disallowed,
financed at a high cost, or recovered from customers only after a long lag.

## 3. Current operating sequence

The company packet reports:

- Q4 2025 adjusted operating earnings of `$0.59` per share;
- Q1 2026 adjusted operating earnings of `$0.91` per share;
- Q2 2026 adjusted operating earnings of `$0.43` per share;
- 2026 adjusted operating earnings guidance of `$2.81` to `$2.91` per share;
- a revised four-year capital plan of `$41.7B`; and
- expected rate-base growth of `7.9%`.

Exelon also identified an additional `$12B` to `$17B` transmission opportunity
beyond the base capital plan and said close to `90%` of rate base was covered by
established recovery mechanisms through 2026–2027.

These are useful visibility measures, not cash receipts. The investment test is
whether the recovery mechanisms produce earned returns after interest, common
equity issuance, operating costs, and customer affordability constraints.

## 4. Earnings quality and accounting risks

### Regulated accounting does not remove judgment

Utilities record regulatory assets and liabilities when regulators allow future
recovery or require future customer benefit. These balances can be economically
valid, but they depend on continued regulatory support and the timing of future
collections.

The forensic review should track:

- regulatory assets and the period over which they are recovered;
- regulatory liabilities and customer refunds;
- storm, environmental, pension, and decommissioning balances;
- deferred taxes and tax-recovery mechanisms;
- transmission and distribution projects not yet in service; and
- disallowances, prudence reviews, and rate-case settlements.

A reported earnings increase supported by a growing regulatory asset is not the
same as cash already collected from customers.

### Adjusted operating earnings

Exelon reports adjusted operating earnings to remove selected items from GAAP
earnings. The reconciliation is useful, but repeated exclusions for storm
costs, regulatory matters, plant or asset impairments, financing effects, or
restructuring should be treated as part of the economic cost of operating a
large utility unless the evidence shows they are genuinely unusual.

The correct per-share measure is common cash after capital spending, interest,
dividends, equity issuance, and required financing—not adjusted EPS alone.

## 5. Alden lens: liquidity and durability

Electric distribution has durable demand because customers require continuous
service. Exelon's regulated territories and recovery mechanisms provide more
stability than merchant generation.

Liquidity risk remains central because the company spends before it earns the
full regulated return. The main stress points are:

- interest-rate increases raising financing cost;
- construction inflation and labor shortages;
- regulatory lag or disallowance;
- customer affordability and political resistance;
- storm and reliability obligations;
- transmission-project timing; and
- the need to issue debt or equity while maintaining credit quality.

The durable case is strongest when capital enters rate base promptly, allowed
returns are earned, customer bills remain manageable, and financing does not
transfer too much value from existing shareholders to lenders or new equity.

## 6. Damodaran lens: translate the regulated-growth narrative

The narrative is that Exelon can compound earnings through rate-base and
transmission growth. The required numbers are:

1. capital spending by utility and project type;
2. percentage of spending approved, in service, and earning a return;
3. rate-base growth versus actual collected revenue;
4. allowed return on equity versus earned return on equity;
5. regulatory lag and the recovery period for major projects;
6. operating cash after interest and working-capital changes;
7. debt, equity issuance, and diluted share count;
8. reliability, storm, environmental, pension, and safety costs; and
9. customer bill impact and affordability evidence.

The lifecycle question is whether the company is generating value from a
repeatable regulated investment program or simply increasing the asset base and
financing needs. A larger rate base is valuable only when the allowed return is
earned after financing and operating burden.

## 7. Control point versus burden carrier

| Layer | Exelon captures | Exelon carries |
|---|---|---|
| Local grid monopoly | Customer relationship and essential network access | Reliability, outage restoration, safety, and public scrutiny |
| Rate base | Allowed return on approved investment | Construction cash, regulatory lag, disallowance, and financing |
| Transmission expansion | Long-lived infrastructure and recovery opportunity | Siting, permits, interconnection, project execution, and customer affordability |
| Urban service territory | Dense customer base and system scale | Political oversight, reliability expectations, and aging infrastructure |
| Capital plan | Future earnings visibility | Debt, equity dilution, interest cost, and execution risk |

Exelon captures a protected relationship, but the regulator and customer carry
part of the economic decision. Shareholders do not own a free pricing right;
they own a regulated claim whose value depends on allowed and collected returns.

## 8. Valuation readiness

The registered scenario screen uses a preserved current equity-value snapshot
of approximately `$44.3B`. A utility valuation should not use the same
owner-cash multiple applied to software companies. The bridge should use:

`equity value -> rate-base growth -> allowed and earned return -> financing and dilution -> sustainable common earnings and cash`

The registered bear, base, and bull common-earnings cases are `$1.8B`, `$2.6B`,
and `$3.4B`, valued at `12x`, `15x`, and `18x`, for implied values of `$21.6B`,
`$39.0B`, and `$61.2B`. This is a risk-adjusted rate-base screen, not a
post-capex owner-cash calculation. It requires approved wires investment to
enter service, recovery to remain high, earned returns to cover financing, and
dilution to remain contained.

Bear, base, and bull cases should vary capital spending, in-service timing,
allowed ROE, regulatory lag, financing cost, equity issuance, customer bill
growth, and terminal rate-base growth. The mechanical post-capex deficit is a
stress indicator, not a standalone fair-value estimate.

## 9. Next-filing watchlist and thesis breakers

1. Does the `$41.7B` capital plan remain approved, on schedule, and within cost?
2. Does rate-base growth enter service and earn returns rather than remain work
   in progress?
3. Does recovery coverage remain near the stated `90%` through 2026–2027?
4. Does operating cash grow with allowed earnings after working-capital timing?
5. Does debt grow faster than rate base or operating earnings?
6. Is equity issued at prices that dilute existing shareholders materially?
7. Do storm, environmental, pension, and reliability costs remain manageable?
8. Do customer bills rise faster than local income, increasing political risk?
9. Does the additional `$12B` to `$17B` transmission opportunity become an
   approved, financed, in-service program?
10. Does common cash per diluted share support the dividend after capex and
    interest, without relying on repeated external financing?

The thesis breaks if capital spending is delayed or disallowed, recovery fails
to keep pace with financing cost, debt and equity issuance absorb the allowed
return, or affordability pressure prevents the company from earning the return
assumed in the capital plan.

## Conclusion

Exelon is a useful regulated-utility control case because it is less dependent
on merchant power or a single data-center load story. Its valuable relationship
is the regulated local grid and the recovery architecture around it.

The investment case is durable only when approved capital becomes in-service
assets, those assets earn an allowed return, customers can pay the resulting
bills, and the financing burden does not consume the common-shareholder return.
The annual cash record makes the key point clear: strong operating cash does not
fund the full capital program. The future filings must therefore be read for
recovery, financing, and per-share economics—not rate-base growth alone.
