# Ferguson Enterprises Inc. — broad-line infrastructure distribution under forensic review

Research date: 2026-09-13
Evidence window: FY2025, five-month transition period ended December 31, 2025, and Q1/Q2 2026
Ticker: FERG
Evidence grade: A for reported filing data and business mix; B for acquisition returns, price/volume quality, and the durability of non-residential share gains.

## Bottom line

Ferguson controls a high-value channel between a fragmented supplier base and
fragmented contractors. Its moat is local availability, branch density,
inventory, fleet, technical support, and the ability to simplify complex
water, plumbing, HVAC, mechanical, and infrastructure jobs. Roughly two-thirds
of sales are tied to repair, maintenance, and improvement rather than new
construction, and residential and non-residential exposure are approximately
balanced.

The contradiction is that this is a broad and resilient control point, but not
a light-capital or acquisition-free one. In FY2025 Ferguson generated $1.908B
of operating cash flow, invested $305M in capex and $301M in acquisitions, and
carried $4.175B of debt against $674M of cash. The operating cash story is
solid; the owner-cash story depends on treating acquisitions as recurring
reinvestment rather than an optional adjustment. The current thesis is
therefore “strong channel control with a meaningful capital and integration
burden,” not simply “asset-light distributor.”

## Control point and demand stack

Ferguson served a roughly $340B North American construction market with about
37,000 suppliers, 1,746 branches at July 31, 2025, approximately 6,200 fleet
vehicles, and about 35,000 associates. The transition filing later reported
1,729 branches at December 31, 2025. The company spans waterworks, Ferguson
Home, commercial/mechanical, residential trade plumbing, HVAC, industrial,
facilities supply, and fire/fabrication.

The control point is not a proprietary product. It is the reduction of job
friction: the contractor can source complex baskets quickly, obtain technical
help, and rely on Ferguson’s local stock and delivery. This creates a useful
workflow relationship, but a competitor can attack it through price, local
service, niche expertise, or a better digital procurement interface.

## Reported financial baseline

The fiscal year-end changed from July 31 to December 31, so the five-month
transition period must not be treated as a normal annual observation. The last
clean comparable series in the FY2025 10-K is:

| Net sales | — | 29,635 | 30,762 |
| Operating profit | — | 2,652 | 2,606 |

| Operating cash flow | 2,723 | 1,873 | 1,908 |
| Capital expenditures | 441 | 372 | 305 |

FY2025 sales grew 3.8% and operating cash flow grew 1.9%, while operating
profit declined modestly. The subsequent calendar-2025 results release
reported $31.3B of sales, $3.011B of adjusted operating profit, $2.2B of
operating cash flow, and eight acquisitions with more than $300M of annualized
revenue. These two views are not contradictory: they use different reporting
periods and one is adjusted; they should be kept separate in the model.

## Cash conversion and capital burden

The FY2025 cash bridge is straightforward but economically important:

- OCF: $1.908B
- Less capex: $305M
- Less acquired businesses: $301M
- Residual before dividends, buybacks, debt service, and other investing: about
  $1.302B

That residual is not pure maintenance free cash flow. Management describes
capex as supporting new distribution centers, branches, and technology, while
acquisitions expand density and capabilities. If acquisitions are required to
defend the control point, excluding them would overstate owner economics. If
they are genuinely optional, the company has more flexibility than the screen
implies. The next filings need to show acquired revenue, post-acquisition
margin, working-capital investment, and returns by cohort.

Working capital is a central forensic field. At July 31, 2025, receivables were
$3.964B, inventories $4.492B, and receivables were subject to customer credit
and collectability risk. Management attributed the 2025 OCF increase partly to
the timing of vendor and tax payments, while receivables and inventory used
cash. A favorable payable or tax timing effect should not be capitalized as
durable operating leverage.

## Earnings quality and accounting risks

Ferguson’s reported earnings appear relatively conventional, but the main
quality questions are embedded in normal operating accounting rather than
obvious “one-time” exclusions:

- Supplier price timing affects gross margin comparisons. Price inflation can
  lift sales while the cost catch-up arrives later or compresses margin.
- Acquisitions create customer-relationship intangibles and goodwill. FY2025
  included nine acquired businesses, with preliminary purchase accounting
  showing $136M of customer-relationship intangibles, $16M of other
  intangibles, and $116M of goodwill.
- Receivables are not factored, but the company uses a receivables facility and
  its covenants include performance requirements for timely collections,
  write-offs, and receivables availability. That makes collection quality and
  financing structure worth tracking together.
- Share-based compensation was $28M in FY2025, down from $49M in FY2024 and
  $51M in FY2023. It is not large enough to explain the cash/earnings profile,
  but dilution and incentive design still matter.
- Adjusted operating profit excludes selected items and is the basis of much
  of management’s margin narrative. Every adjustment should be checked against
  recurring branch, integration, restructuring, and acquisition costs.

There is no evidence here establishing fraud or misconduct. The appropriate
classification is a real distributor with ordinary but material normalization
risks: price timing, working-capital timing, acquisition accounting, and
adjusted-profit presentation.

## Balance-sheet resilience and capital allocation

At July 31, 2025, Ferguson had $674M of cash, $4.2B of total debt, and about
$2.0B of available undrawn liquidity. Debt maturities included $400M in 2026,
$450M in 2027, $525M in 2028, $750M in 2029, and $600M in 2030. The balance
sheet is manageable against the cash-generation base, but it is not immune to
an inventory/receivables downturn or an acquisition-heavy period.

Capital allocation is active: FY2025 included $948M of treasury-share
purchases and $489M of dividends. The company extended its repurchase
authorization to $5B, and Q1 2026 included $236M of repurchases plus a new
$2B program. Buybacks can be sensible when the channel is undervalued, but
they raise the burden on the valuation case when debt-funded or when cash is
also needed for acquisitions and working capital.

## Alden lens: durable demand versus cyclical project exposure

The durable portion of Ferguson’s demand is tied to failure-driven repair,
water systems, HVAC replacement, compliance, and operating continuity. This
is stronger than a pure new-home or discretionary remodeling thesis. Aging and
underbuilt infrastructure, climate comfort, and water investment create
multi-year demand floors.

The cyclical portion is still material. Residential starts, permits, RMI, large
capital projects, interest rates, and contractor confidence affect volumes and
mix. Q1 and Q2 2026 showed the constructive version of the thesis: residential
remained difficult while non-residential grew about 8%, and Q2 guidance rose to
mid-single-digit sales growth. The Alden question is whether that outperformance
comes from durable share gains in unavoidable channels or from a temporary
non-residential/project cycle.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers

Ferguson’s narrative should be translated into:

1. Organic growth after separating supplier price, volume, market growth, and
   share gains.
2. Gross and adjusted operating margin after supplier price timing and
   acquisition integration normalize.
3. Reinvestment including inventory, branches, fleet, distribution centers,
   technology, and bolt-on acquisitions.
4. A competitive-advantage period based on local density and contractor
   workflow, discounted for competitors, supplier disintermediation, and
   customer consolidation.
5. Leverage and share count after debt service, acquisitions, and buybacks.

At the September 11, 2026 market snapshot, FERG was about $222.54 per share
with equity value of approximately $43.17B. A simple FY2025 OCF-minus-capex
screen is about $1.603B, but subtracting FY2025 acquisition cash gives about
$1.302B. The market is therefore pricing a durable, growing channel and/or
continued capital returns; it is not pricing merely the current reported
cash harvest.

## Incentives and thesis breakers

Ferguson’s executive awards include adjusted operating profit, adjusted EPS
growth, return on capital employed, and relative total shareholder return.
That is directionally better than a pure sales target, but “adjusted” metrics
and ROCE can still benefit from acquisition timing, buybacks, or exclusions.
The proxy should be reconciled to actual cash returns and acquired-business
performance.

The thesis weakens if:

- non-residential growth fades while residential remains weak;
- price inflation explains more growth than volume and share gains;
- gross margin remains dependent on supplier timing or adjusted exclusions;
- inventory and receivables grow faster than sales;
- acquisition revenue rises without post-acquisition margin or cash returns;
- net debt rises while repurchases continue; or
- branch/site density grows but incremental ROCE falls.

The next filing review should capture organic/price/volume bridges, acquisition
cohort economics, inventory turns, receivable days, supplier concentration,
branch closures/openings, capex by category, buyback funding, and exact
incentive-scorecard outcomes.
