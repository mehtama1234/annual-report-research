# Fortis Inc. — regulated rate-base compounding with financing and approval risk

Research date: 2026-09-14  
Evidence window: FY2025 and FY2026 Q2  
Ticker: FTS  
Evidence grade: A for reported results, capital plan, and regulatory disclosures; B for the return on future projects, regulatory recovery, customer affordability, and the durability of the dividend-growth path.

## Bottom line

Fortis owns a portfolio of regulated electric and gas utilities across Canada, the United States, and the Caribbean. Its control point is not a consumer brand or a software interface. It is the right to provide essential service in defined territories and to seek recovery of prudent investment through regulated rates.

The economic model is durable but capital intensive. Fortis must build and maintain generation, transmission, distribution, gas, storage, and information systems before the full revenue recovery arrives. Regulators determine what investment enters rate base, the allowed return, the timing of recovery, and the portion borne by customers or shareholders. The company also carries debt, interest-rate, foreign-exchange, weather, wildfire, storm, environmental, labor, and affordability risks.

Fortis reported 2025 revenue of approximately C$12B, annual net earnings of C$1.7B, adjusted earnings per share of C$3.53, and C$5.6B of capital expenditures. Its 2026–2030 plan is C$28.8B and is expected to grow midyear rate base from C$42.4B in 2025 to C$57.9B in 2030, a 7% compound annual growth rate. Q2 2026 net earnings were C$396M, and first-half capital expenditures were C$2.7B against the C$5.6B annual plan.

The central investment question is whether Fortis can earn an acceptable return on approved capital while preserving credit quality and dividend capacity, not whether it can grow the rate base in dollars.

Related first-principles explanation: [Rate-base growth versus owner return](../first-principles/rate-base-growth-versus-owner-return.md).

## What Fortis controls

The operating chain is:

`service territory and infrastructure -> regulatory filing -> approved rate base and allowed return -> customer rates -> cash collection -> debt and equity funding -> next investment cycle`

Fortis controls the operating execution of its utilities, project development, regulatory filings, capital allocation across subsidiaries, and the reliability of the systems it operates. Geographic diversification across five Canadian provinces, ten U.S. states, and the Caribbean reduces dependence on one regulator or weather system.

Fortis does not control approval outcomes, allowed returns, customer affordability, interest rates, construction costs, storms, wildfire, environmental remediation, labor availability, currency translation, or whether a new project is placed in service on time. Diversification reduces single-jurisdiction risk but does not remove the common need for debt and regulatory recovery.

The plan is a forecast, not a cash return. Rate base growth becomes valuable only if the regulator permits recovery at an adequate allowed return, the project is completed within budget, and the resulting earnings exceed the financing and operating burden.

## Rate base is an accounting and regulatory claim, not unrestricted cash

Fortis invests cash today and expects to recover the investment through future customer rates. That creates a timing gap. Construction work in progress, regulatory assets, deferred costs, and approved but not yet recovered spending can support future earnings while consuming current cash.

The investor must separate capital placed in service and earning a return; construction work in progress awaiting approval or completion; regulatory assets that depend on future recovery; customer contributions and other offsets; maintenance capital versus growth capital; allowed return versus actual project return; and cash available after interest, dividends, and equity funding.

Fortis’ Q2 release illustrates the distinction. The Tilbury Phase 1B expansion received approval for an allowance of up to C$2.2B, but remains subject to further approvals and permitting, with construction potentially beginning in 2027 and service as early as 2031. Approval of a cost allowance is evidence of regulatory progress, not proof of final cost, completion, or shareholder return.

## Earnings quality and financial engineering risks

Utility earnings are not fraudulent simply because they include regulatory accounting. The forensic question is whether reported earnings are supported by cash collection and durable recovery rights.

Fortis reported adjusted EPS of C$3.53 for 2025 versus C$3.28 in 2024. The annual release also identified C$63M of losses associated with dispositions of FortisTCI, Fortis Belize, and Belize Electricity, roughly half related to income taxes. Adjustments can improve comparability, but the investor should identify which items are truly nonrecurring and whether the company repeatedly excludes project, restructuring, storm, impairment, or regulatory costs.

Holding-company finance costs and subsidiary debt matter. Regulated subsidiaries may have their own capital structures and recovery mechanisms, while the parent must fund dividends, equity contributions, interest, and investment. A dividend-growth record is not proof that the dividend is funded from residual cash rather than new equity, debt, or a favorable regulatory timing difference.

The company’s dividend reinvestment plan is part of the funding model. If shares are issued to fund dividends or capital needs, per-share growth must be measured after dilution. The relevant test is earnings, rate base, and cash flow per share—not only consolidated totals.

## Lyn Alden lens: liquidity, credit, and regulated durability

Fortis is more defensive than a merchant generator because regulated revenue and approved recovery can stabilize cash flows. It is not immune to tighter liquidity. Higher interest rates increase the cost of funding the rate base, while inflation and construction delays can create a gap between spending and recovery.

The balance-sheet test is whether Fortis can fund the C$28.8B plan while maintaining targeted subsidiary capital structures, credit ratings, covenant compliance, and dividend capacity. The 2025 filing identifies access to long-term capital, interest costs, debt maturities, and common-equity funding as central assumptions. Those are not secondary risks; they determine how much of the rate-base growth accrues to existing shareholders.

Regulatory diversification reduces dependence on one regulator but adds currency translation, different allowed returns, different environmental rules, and multiple approval calendars. A bad outcome in one territory may be manageable; simultaneous inflation, rate, storm, and affordability pressure can still affect the group.

## Management incentives and capital allocation

The proxy statement should establish whether annual and long-term awards reward
rate-base growth, adjusted EPS, operating cash flow, return on equity, safety,
reliability, customer outcomes, debt metrics, credit ratings, share price, or
dividend growth. Record metric definitions, weights, threshold/target/maximum
levels, and exclusions for storms, dispositions, regulatory items,
acquisitions, restructuring, and financing costs.

The incentive tests are:

- Does a rate-base target count only capital placed in service and approved for
  recovery, or also projects that remain in construction and financing risk?
- Does adjusted EPS exclude recurring holding-company, storm, environmental,
  project, or regulatory costs?
- Does a growth target charge interest, equity issuance, dividend-reinvestment
  dilution, and the gap before customer recovery?
- Are acquisitions measured by post-integration earnings and cash per share
  rather than customer count or consolidated rate base?
- Are capital returns evaluated after maintenance, compliance, liquidity, debt
  maturity, and credit-rating requirements?

A plan that rewards rate-base growth can encourage more investment without
proving that the investment earns an adequate return after financing and
affordability constraints. A dividend-growth target can encourage equity or
debt funding when internally generated cash is insufficient. The proxy and
regulatory filings must show whether management is accountable for earned
return and per-share economics.

For each major project, compare the executive narrative with actual cost,
placed-in-service date, regulatory approval, customer bill effect, financing,
earned return, and diluted shares. For each capital return, compare the cash
paid with owner cash after the full capital plan and obligations.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers

The narrative is: Fortis is a diversified regulated utility that can compound rate base and dividends through essential infrastructure investment.

That narrative must become measurable assumptions: rate-base growth placed in service rather than merely planned; allowed returns, regulatory lag, and recovery of construction work in progress; actual project cost versus approved allowance and schedule; maintenance capital, storm and environmental spending, and customer contributions; debt, interest, refinancing, equity issuance, and dividend reinvestment dilution; jurisdictional earnings, currency translation, and regulatory outcomes; customer affordability, load growth, weather, and commodity exposure; holding-company costs, subsidiary dividends, and cash available for common shareholders; and terminal growth after the current capital plan is complete.

Using 507.34 million shares outstanding at year-end and an approximate U.S. share price of $54.37 on September 14, 2026, the equity value was approximately $27.6B. A simple risk-adjusted common-earnings screen is:

| Normalized annual common earnings / owner-earnings screen | Multiple | Implied equity value |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| C$1.50B bear case | 15x | C$22.5B |
| C$1.80B base case | 17x | C$30.6B |
| C$2.10B strong case | 19x | C$39.9B |

These are scenario outputs, not price targets. They are in Canadian dollars for the earnings screen and should not be compared mechanically with the U.S.-dollar market value. The main valuation risk is that the market pays for 7% rate-base growth while actual per-share returns are reduced by financing costs, equity issuance, regulatory lag, or projects that earn below the allowed return.

## Thesis breakers and next evidence

The thesis weakens if rate-base growth remains planned rather than placed in service; if regulatory lag or disallowances increase; if project costs exceed approved allowances; if debt and interest rise faster than rate-base earnings; if credit ratings weaken; if dividend reinvestment or equity issuance dilutes per-share growth; if storm, wildfire, environmental, or remediation costs are not recovered; if customer affordability limits rate increases; if foreign exchange or Caribbean exposure creates recurring losses; or if the company uses adjusted earnings to hide recurring holding-company and project costs.

The next filings should answer: How much of the C$5.6B plan entered rate base? What was the actual cost and regulatory treatment of major projects? Did financing costs and common shares outstanding rise? Did the 7% rate-base path produce comparable per-share earnings growth? Did regulators approve recovery of new LNG, transmission, battery, and load-growth investments? Did credit metrics remain consistent with dividend growth?
