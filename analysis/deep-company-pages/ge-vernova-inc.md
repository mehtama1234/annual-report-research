# GE Vernova Inc.: generation and grid equipment, services, and the cost of being the capacity owner

## Research question

GE Vernova sells gas turbines, nuclear and hydro equipment and services, wind
equipment, transformers, switchgear, grid systems, power conversion, storage,
and grid software. The company participates in the demand created by
electrification, industrial expansion, and data-center load, but it also owns
manufacturing capacity, product warranties, project execution, inventory,
supplier commitments, and long-term service obligations.

The central question is not whether electricity demand is strong. The evidence
already supports that. The question is how much of the demand becomes durable
common-owner cash after equipment expansion, customer prepayments, working
capital, warranty exposure, acquisitions, pensions, debt, tax effects, and
share repurchases are separated.

## Plain-language operating model

The operating sequence is:

`power or grid requirement -> customer specification and contract -> equipment design and manufacturing -> delivery, installation, and acceptance -> long-term service -> cash collection`

GE Vernova controls product technology, manufacturing capacity, installed-base
service relationships, project execution, and parts availability. Customers,
utilities, governments, developers, and large industrial users control project
approval, financing, site readiness, regulation, and payment timing. GE Vernova
therefore captures a valuable technical and installed-base relationship while
carrying the physical and contractual burden required to deliver it.

## Definitions

- **Power** includes gas power, nuclear power, hydro power, and related equipment
  and services. It supplies dispatchable generation and maintains installed
  equipment.
- **Electrification** includes transmission, grid integration, power conversion
  and storage, grid automation, software, switchgear, and transformers.
- **Wind** includes onshore and offshore wind turbines and blades.
- **Remaining performance obligation (RPO)** is contracted revenue assigned to
  work that has not yet been completed. It is a visibility measure, not current
  profit or collected cash.
- **Contract liabilities** are customer cash or billings received before the
  company has recognized the related revenue. They help current cash flow but
  create future delivery obligations.
- **Free cash flow** here means operating cash flow less additions to property,
  plant, equipment, and internal-use software. It does not deduct acquisitions,
  debt repayment, pension funding, warranties, or shareholder distributions.

## Evidence chain

| Period | Demand and revenue | Cash, capital, and balance sheet |

| FY2023 | Revenue about `$32.9B`; operating loss and net loss were reported | Operating cash about `$1.186B`; capital additions about `$744M`; residual about `$442M` before acquisitions and other owner claims |
| FY2024 | Revenue `$34.935B`; RPO `$119.023B` | Operating cash `$2.583B`; capital additions `$883M`; free cash flow `$1.701B` |
| FY2025 | Orders `$59.315B`; revenue `$38.068B`; RPO `$150.238B`; adjusted EBITDA `$3.196B` | Operating cash `$4.987B`; capital additions `$1.277B`; free cash flow `$3.710B`; customer contract-liability inflow was a major working-capital source |
| Q2 FY2026 year-to-date | Revenue `$20.442B`, up `19%`; adjusted EBITDA `$2.146B`; RPO `$176.284B` | Operating cash `$10.680B`; capital additions `$783M`; free cash flow `$9.897B`; cash and restricted cash `$13.120B`; long-term borrowings `$2.794B` |

The current half-year cash result is unusually strong, but it requires a bridge.
The six-month operating cash inflow included `$13.695B` from contract
liabilities and current deferred income. It also included a `$1.258B` cash tax
payment, a `$1.744B` inventory use, a `$843M` receivable use, and a `$358M`
contract-asset use. The customer-funding source is economically useful, but it
is not the same as margin earned on completed equipment and services.

## What GE Vernova controls

### Power: installed-base service and scarce production slots

Power benefits from a large gas-turbine, nuclear, and hydro installed base.
Service contracts can last for five to twenty-five years and include
preventive maintenance, outage services, and performance or availability
commitments. That creates a recurring relationship after the original equipment
sale.

The control point is not only the turbine. It is the combination of installed
equipment, specialized parts, service technicians, operating data, and customer
confidence in availability. The burden is the warranty-type obligation,
manufacturing capacity, project timing, and the cost of a product or component
failure.

In Q2 FY2026, Power produced six-month revenue of `$10.449B` and segment EBITDA
of `$1.842B`, a `17.6%` margin. Power RPO included equipment and service demand,
with gas-power equipment and contractual service agreements driving much of the
increase. Slot reservations can improve capacity planning and provide customer
funding, but the investment case still requires proof that reserved capacity
becomes profitable deliveries and service cash.

### Electrification: the grid bottleneck and the Prolec GE acquisition

Electrification supplies equipment and systems between generation and
consumption. Transformer and switchgear demand is directly connected to grid
expansion, data-center interconnection, industrial load, and transmission
modernization.

The company acquired the remaining `50%` of Prolec GE on February 2, 2026 for
approximately `$5.254B` in cash. Prolec GE has about `10,000` employees and
seven manufacturing sites. The acquisition added transformer capacity and
customer access, but it also added integration risk, goodwill, intangible
amortization, inventory step-up, debt, and capital requirements.

Prolec GE generated `$1.344B` of revenue and a pre-tax loss of `$166M` from the
acquisition date through June 30, 2026. The result includes inventory step-up
amortization, intangible amortization, and integration costs. That is an early
warning against treating the acquisition as immediately accretive simply
because it expanded RPO.

Electrification six-month revenue was `$6.597B` and segment EBITDA was
`$1.200B`, an `18.2%` margin. Its RPO was `$44.563B`, up from `$34.242B` at
December 31, 2025. The increase came from Prolec GE and demand for switchgear,
transformers, alternating-current substations, high-voltage direct current,
and synchronous condensers. The next proof is earnings and cash from the added
manufacturing base, not the acquisition's contribution to backlog alone.

### Wind: the contradiction inside the energy-transition thesis

Wind gives GE Vernova exposure to long-term decarbonization and renewable
generation, but it has carried the weakest execution and margin profile. In Q2
FY2026, management reported lower onshore equipment deliveries, higher offshore
project costs, and higher offshore contract losses during the six-month period.
The 2026 guidance still expects approximately `$400M` of Wind segment EBITDA
losses.

This matters for valuation. A high multiple applied to consolidated backlog can
silently capitalize profitable Power and Electrification demand while ignoring
the cash and capital required to complete loss-making Wind contracts. The
company must be analyzed by segment rather than as one uniform energy-growth
asset.

## RPO quality: contracted visibility with different cash timing

At June 30, 2026, total RPO was `$176.284B`: equipment RPO was `$87.821B` and
services RPO was `$88.463B`. Equipment RPO was expected to be recognized `36%`
within one year, `65%` within two years, and `97%` within five years. Services
RPO was expected to be recognized `16%` within one year, `54%` within five
years, `79%` within ten years, and `92%` within fifteen years.

This distinction is essential:

- equipment RPO requires factories, materials, labor, installation, and
  acceptance before revenue and margin are complete;
- service RPO has a longer recognition period and can provide better continuity,
  but it carries uptime, availability, parts, and warranty-type obligations;
- contract modifications can change timing and amount;
- contract liabilities can create a large cash inflow before the associated
  revenue is earned.

RPO is therefore evidence of customer commitments, not a substitute for a
segment-level cash bridge.

## Cash conversion and forensic financial-engineering tests

### Customer prepayments are helpful but not free cash

FY2025 operating cash was `$4.987B`, but `$8.019B` came from contract
liabilities and current deferred income. Management attributed the increase to
higher down payments on Power orders and slot reservation agreements, plus
Electrification down payments and collections. These sources reduce the need
for external financing, but they also represent work GE Vernova must deliver
later.

The first test is whether cash remains strong after contract-liability growth
normalizes. A second test is whether the company can keep receiving deposits
without increasing cancellations, customer concessions, delayed deliveries, or
contract losses.

### Inventory and contract assets can absorb the demand cycle

At June 30, 2026, inventory was `$12.692B`, up from `$10.429B` at December 31,
2025. Current contract assets were `$9.522B`. In the first half, inventory used
`$1.744B` of cash and contract assets used `$358M`. Equipment demand can require
materials and production before billing catches up.

The relevant question is not whether inventory supports future sales. It is
whether turns, customer deposits, delivery timing, and final acceptance protect
cash returns when projects are delayed or prices and input costs move.

### Acquisition accounting created a large non-operating gain

The Prolec GE transaction produced a pre-tax gain of approximately `$3.992B`
from remeasuring GE Vernova's existing 50% interest to fair value. That gain
helped produce six-month net income attributable to GE Vernova of `$5.413B`,
but it did not represent operating profit generated by the acquired business.

The half-year effective tax rate was `10.5%`, below the U.S. statutory rate,
primarily because the Prolec gain was nontaxable and stock compensation
created a tax benefit. Reported EPS and net income therefore overstate the
quality of recurring operating earnings for the period unless the gain and tax
effect are removed.

### Capital spending is part of the business model

GE Vernova spent `$1.277B` on PP&E and internal-use software in FY2025, up from
`$883M` in FY2024. In the first half of 2026 it spent `$783M`, including
manufacturing expansion. Management says material PP&E spending is required to
support new products, manufacturing capacity, and ongoing maintenance.

The owner-cash denominator must therefore include more than depreciation. A
period with low replacement spending would make free cash flow look better even
if the company had underinvested in factories, tooling, or service capacity.

### Warranty, quality, and project losses are real owner claims

The product-warranty liability was `$1.595B` at June 30, 2026. Warranty
estimates are based largely on historical claims experience, but actual claims
can differ. Contracts can also impose performance, delivery, availability,
liquidated-damage, repair, and replacement obligations.

The Wind segment demonstrates why these claims matter: offshore project costs,
delivery timing, and contract losses can absorb the margin from stronger
segments. A backlog valuation that does not reserve for quality and execution
risk is incomplete.

## Balance-sheet resilience and hidden claims

At June 30, 2026, GE Vernova had `$13.120B` of cash, cash equivalents, and
restricted cash, against `$2.794B` of long-term borrowings. The balance sheet
is strong in liquidity terms, but the cash is not entirely unrestricted or
discretionary. It includes restricted cash and customer-funded working capital.

The Prolec acquisition was financed partly with approximately `$2.6B` of new
senior notes. The company also had `$40.037B` of total contract liabilities and
deferred income, `$10.104B` of accounts payable and equipment-project payables,
and `$843M` of operating lease liabilities at the end of 2025.

GE Vernova also disclosed a `$390M` liability connected with cash transferred
from General Electric for legacy legal matters and `$169M` of indemnifications
connected with separation agreements. An IRS audit of GE's 2016-2020 returns
could create additional tax obligations allocated under the tax matters
agreement; the amount was not reasonably estimable and no liability had been
accrued at June 30, 2026.

These are not reasons to reject the business. They are reasons to avoid treating
the `$13.1B` cash balance as equivalent to distributable excess cash.

## Management incentives and capital allocation

Management presents a strategy of organic investment, targeted acquisitions,
and returning roughly one-third of cash generation to shareholders. In the first
half of 2026, the company repurchased `$3.671B` of stock and paid `$273M` of
dividends. It repurchased about `4.3M` shares through June 30 at an average
price of approximately `$854` per share.

Repurchases can increase per-share value when the shares are bought below
intrinsic value. They can destroy value if management treats temporary cash
from customer deposits, tax recovery, or acquisition accounting as recurring
owner cash. The test is whether buybacks continue after the company funds
factories, warranty reserves, pension requirements, Prolec integration, and
loss-making Wind obligations.

## Lyn Alden lens: liquidity, durability, and monetary-cycle exposure

GE Vernova is exposed to a long-duration physical-investment cycle. Its
customers must finance generation, grid, and industrial capacity, and projects
can be delayed by interest rates, permitting, tariffs, commodity prices, and
political decisions.

The strongest durability comes from installed-base services, grid equipment
with long qualification cycles, and customer deposits attached to scarce
production capacity. The weakest durability comes from projects where the
customer can delay spending, the contract is fixed-price, and cost inflation or
technical failure remains with GE Vernova.

Liquidity analysis should therefore track:

- customer deposits and slot reservations as a source of funding and future
  delivery obligations;
- inventory, supplier advances, contract assets, and receivables through the
  cycle;
- net cash after Prolec debt, pension, warranties, leases, and restricted cash;
- the sensitivity of customer projects to rates, tariffs, public policy, and
  utility affordability;
- whether services and grid equipment remain cash-positive if equipment orders
  normalize.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers and reinvestment

The narrative is that electricity demand, AI data centers, electrification, and
grid modernization create a multi-year investment cycle. To make that narrative
testable, the model must specify:

1. revenue by Power, Electrification, and Wind;
2. equipment versus services mix;
3. segment margin after warranty, project losses, and acquisition costs;
4. working-capital investment required per dollar of incremental revenue;
5. sustaining and growth PP&E spending;
6. Prolec GE's post-integration return on invested capital;
7. tax-normalized operating earnings after removing the remeasurement gain;
8. share count after repurchases, equity compensation, and dilution.

The company can grow revenue and RPO while destroying value if it accepts
low-return contracts, expands factories ahead of demand, or funds customer
projects with insufficient margin. The correct reinvestment question is not how
much GE Vernova spends, but whether each dollar of factory, R&D, service
capacity, and acquisition investment earns a return above its cost of capital.

## Valuation cases

The cases below use normalized owner cash, not six-month reported free cash
flow. The base case is deliberately below the 2026 guidance midpoint because
the current period includes unusually large customer-deposit inflows, tax
recovery, and cash effects around Prolec.

| Case | Normalized owner cash | Multiple | Implied equity value | What must be true |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Bear | `$5.0B` | `18x` | `$90B` | Power and Electrification remain profitable, but deposits normalize, Wind losses persist, Prolec earns slowly, and capex remains elevated |
| Base | `$8.0B` | `25x` | `$200B` | Services and grid equipment sustain high demand, Prolec reaches acceptable returns, Wind losses shrink, and working capital remains controlled |
| Bull | `$11.0B` | `30x` | `$330B` | Current cash conversion becomes durable, equipment pricing remains strong, services compound, Wind reaches break-even, and reinvestment earns high returns |

The September 14, 2026 market snapshot was approximately `$237.6B`. That is
above the base case and below the bull case. The price therefore requires more
than electricity-demand growth. It requires confidence that customer-funded
working capital, Prolec integration, equipment pricing, Wind repair, and
services cash will remain strong after the current surge.

## Thesis breakers and next-filing tests

| Question | Evidence that supports the thesis | Evidence that weakens or breaks it |
|---|---|---|
| Does Power convert scarce capacity into durable cash? | Power equipment and service margins remain strong; deposits fund production without rising cancellations | Slot reservations convert into delayed or low-margin deliveries; service claims or warranty costs rise |
| Does Electrification earn on the Prolec investment? | Prolec revenue grows, losses reverse, transformer capacity expands, and cash return exceeds integration capital | Prolec remains loss-making after step-up and integration effects, or new capacity requires repeated capital without margin |
| Does RPO become revenue and cash? | RPO converts on schedule with stable margins, billings, collections, and manageable inventory | RPO ages, contracts are modified, contract assets rise, or customers delay acceptance and payment |
| Is Wind no longer a structural burden? | Offshore losses decline, onshore deliveries normalize, and segment cash turns positive | Contract losses, delays, warranty claims, or policy changes keep consuming cash |
| Is current free cash flow repeatable? | Operating cash remains strong after contract-liability growth normalizes and tax effects fade | Cash falls toward reported earnings because deposits stop, inventory rises, or receivables and contract assets expand |
| Are buybacks value-creating? | Repurchases occur after growth capex, pension, warranty, debt, and integration needs are funded | Repurchases rely on temporary deposits, new debt, or cash that should fund factory expansion and claims |

## Bottom line

GE Vernova owns a valuable set of technical standards, manufacturing assets,
installed-base relationships, and grid bottlenecks. The 2025 and first-half 2026
results show real operating improvement in Power and Electrification. They also
show why the headline numbers need separation.

RPO is not collected cash. Free cash flow is not fully discretionary cash.
Six-month net income includes a large Prolec remeasurement gain. Contract
liabilities funded much of the cash increase. Wind remains a material
contradiction case. Prolec expands the grid-control opportunity but currently
adds integration and loss risk.

The investable conclusion remains conditional: GE Vernova can become a durable
owner of power and grid capacity if services, equipment pricing, Prolec returns,
and Wind repair survive normalization. The next filings must prove that the
cash belongs to the common owner after customer obligations and reinvestment,
not merely that customers have placed large orders.
