# Granite Construction Incorporated — civil execution, materials integration, and CAP-to-cash quality

Date baseline: 2026-09-14  
Primary evidence: [Granite company packet](../../extracted/industrial-goods/general-contractors/granite-construction-incorporated/company-packet.md), [Granite FY2025 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/861459/000086145926000004/gva-20251231.htm), [Granite Q2 FY2026 Form 10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/861459/000086145926000029/gva-20260630.htm), and [Granite source ledger](../../extracted/industrial-goods/general-contractors/granite-construction-incorporated/source-ledger.md)

## Investment question

Granite is a civil-infrastructure contractor and construction-materials
producer. It builds roads, bridges, airports, rail projects, border and
federal facilities, and site infrastructure while also producing aggregates,
asphalt, and related materials.

The investment question is whether Granite's vertical integration and project
selection create durable owner cash after equipment and plant capital,
contract estimates, weather, claims, working capital, acquisitions,
unconsolidated joint ventures, convertible debt, noncontrolling interests, and
share dilution.

## What Granite controls

The operating sequence is:

`public or private infrastructure funding -> bid and project selection -> awarded project / CAP -> materials, labor, equipment, and subcontractor execution -> measured progress -> billing and collection -> follow-on work and materials pull-through -> cash`

Granite controls local materials access, civil construction capability,
project-selection discipline, field labor, equipment, and relationships with
public agencies, developers, and general contractors. Customers control
funding, scope, change orders, payment, and project timing. Granite carries
weather, labor, equipment, cost-overrun, claim, and completion risk.

## Definitions

- **CAP** means combined assigned and awarded projects. It is a demand and
  visibility measure, not the same as revenue or cash.
- **Assigned work** is work awarded or otherwise identified for execution;
  **awarded work** is not automatically recognized revenue because the project
  still requires starts, progress, billing, and collection.
- **Percentage-of-completion accounting** recognizes revenue and profit as a
  project progresses using estimated total revenue and cost. Estimate changes
  can change current earnings.
- **Contract assets** are amounts recognized before the right to bill is
  unconditional. **Retainage** is billed or earned cash held back until
  contractual conditions are met.
- **Recurring-capital residual** here means operating cash flow less property
  and equipment purchases. Acquisitions, marketable securities, joint-venture
  contributions, debt, dividends, and buybacks remain separate claims.

CAP is useful because it shows the project pool available to a civil
contractor. It is weaker than signed backlog when it includes assigned work or
projects that have not reached full execution certainty. The next filing must
show how CAP turns into starts, revenue, margin, contract assets, receivables,
and collected cash.

## Three-year cash bridge

| Fiscal year | Operating cash flow | Property and equipment purchases | Recurring-capital residual | Acquisition cash |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| FY2023 | `$183.7M` | `$140.4M` | `$43.3M` | `$294.0M` |
| FY2024 | `$456.3M` | `$136.4M` | `$319.9M` | `$134.4M` |
| FY2025 | `$468.9M` | `$138.3M` | `$330.6M` | `$777.5M` |

FY2025 operating cash was supported by a `$122.0M` source from contract assets,
while receivables used `$38.7M`, accounts payable used `$20.4M`, and
contributions to unconsolidated construction joint ventures used `$9.2M`.
Operating cash is therefore not independent of project progression and
contract timing.

The major owner-cash issue is acquisition intensity. Granite spent more than
twice its FY2025 recurring-capital residual on acquisitions, including the
large acquisition program that expanded materials and civil capability. The
acquired businesses may earn attractive returns, but the valuation must show
incremental revenue, margin, working capital, integration cost, and cash rather
than treating acquired CAP as organic evidence.

## Earnings quality and financial-engineering risks

### CAP is not contracted cash

Granite's CAP rose to `$7.0B` at FY2025 year-end and `$7.4B` in Q2 FY2026.
That is strong evidence of demand, but it may include projects at different
stages of award, funding, and execution. Separate assigned work, awarded work,
executed contracts, starts, and revenue. A project can be delayed, resized,
cancelled, or become less profitable after it enters CAP.

### Percentage-of-completion estimates can move profit forward

Granite estimates total cost, revenue, change orders, claims, incentives,
delay penalties, and completion timing. If labor, materials, equipment,
weather, or subcontractor costs rise, previously recognized margin can reverse.
Review unfavorable estimate changes, loss provisions, unapproved change orders,
claims, retainage, contract assets, and final margins by project cohort.

### Contract assets and receivables determine cash quality

The FY2025 cash-flow statement shows the contract-asset release that supported
operating cash. That release may be a real conversion of previously recognized
work, but it cannot repeat indefinitely. Compare contract assets, retainage,
receivables, billing, collections, and cash over the full project cycle.

### Vertical integration helps, but adds capital and operating complexity

Granite's materials operations can provide aggregate and asphalt supply,
pricing support, and internal pull-through. They also require quarry, plant,
equipment, environmental, maintenance, and inventory capital. The correct test
is whether vertical integration improves project gross margin and cash return
after maintenance capital, not merely whether it increases consolidated
revenue.

### Acquisition accounting and noncontrolling interests matter

Granite spent `$777.5M` on acquisitions in FY2025 and recorded `$39.2M` of
stock compensation. It also paid `$53.2M` of distributions to noncontrolling
partners. Acquired businesses can increase adjusted EBITDA while cash is
shared with partners, integration cost is incurred, goodwill rises, and debt
claims become larger. Track cash per diluted share and cash available to
Granite shareholders after partner distributions.

### Convertible-debt accounting can obscure operating performance

Q2 FY2026 reported a large GAAP loss from convertible-debt transactions, while
adjusted net income and adjusted EBITDA increased. The accounting loss is not
the same as an operating loss, but it is also not irrelevant: capped calls,
warrants, conversion terms, refinancing, and potential share issuance affect
future equity claims. Reconcile adjusted results to cash interest, principal,
conversion dilution, and settlement terms.

## Balance-sheet resilience and capital requirements

At December 31, 2025, Granite had about `$529M` of cash and marketable
securities and current maturities of long-term debt of about `$376M`. The
company also had construction equipment, materials plants, joint-venture
commitments, and project working-capital needs. Q2 FY2026 added new senior debt
and continued convertible-note complexity.

Granite's balance sheet is not evaluated by cash alone. Required capital
includes equipment replacement, plant and quarry investment, contract-asset
funding, joint-venture contributions, insurance, environmental obligations,
debt service, partner distributions, and acquisitions. The company can show
strong operating cash in a project-heavy year while still requiring external
financing for growth.

## Lyn Alden lens

Granite benefits from long-term public infrastructure needs, federal funding,
regional population growth, logistics, transportation repair, and mission-
critical site development. These are durable physical requirements, but demand
still passes through public budgets, project approvals, interest rates,
materials prices, and customer financing.

The company is more liquidity-sensitive than a materials royalty or a pure
design adviser because it must fund labor, equipment, inventory, and contract
assets before final collection. Higher rates can delay private projects and
raise the cost of acquisition debt. Weather and project timing can create
quarterly cash volatility even when the long-run need remains intact.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers

The narrative becomes:

`public and private infrastructure funding × Granite bid and project-selection share × materials and field-execution capability -> revenue and project margin -> labor, equipment, materials, subcontractors, working capital, claims, taxes, debt, and reinvestment -> owner cash`

The valuation should separately model:

1. CAP conversion into executed starts, revenue, and collected cash;
2. construction margin by project cohort and materials margin;
3. contract assets, receivables, retainage, claims, and weather timing;
4. vertical-integration return after plant and equipment maintenance;
5. acquisition revenue, margin, integration, goodwill, and partner claims;
6. convertible debt, cash interest, capped calls, warrants, and dilution; and
7. dividends, buybacks, SBC, NCI, and diluted cash per share.

At the September 14, 2026 market snapshot, Granite equity value was about
`$5.0B`. The valuation register uses FY2025 recurring-capital residual of about
`$330.6M`, but applies lower normalized cases because FY2025 acquisition cash
was `$777.5M`, contract assets supported operating cash, and debt and dilution
remain material. The market must prove that CAP converts through the full cash
cycle rather than only through adjusted earnings.

## Management incentives and thesis breakers

Management is incentivized to emphasize CAP, revenue, adjusted net income,
adjusted EBITDA, operating cash flow, materials contribution, acquisitions,
and shareholder return. Pair every measure with project estimates, contract
assets, receivables, capex, acquisition cash, debt, NCI, SBC, and diluted shares.

The thesis breaks if:

- CAP grows while starts, revenue, margins, and collections do not;
- contract-asset releases or favorable payables explain most of operating cash;
- project estimate changes, claims, weather, or labor costs repeatedly reduce
  final margins;
- acquisitions add CAP and adjusted EBITDA without incremental owner cash;
- materials integration requires rising maintenance capital without better
  project returns;
- convertible-debt settlements create persistent interest, dilution, or cash
  claims;
- federal and private infrastructure funding slows enough to reduce starts; or
- adjusted EPS rises while cash per diluted share falls after acquisitions,
  partner distributions, SBC, and debt.

## Next-filing evidence ladder

| Question | Confirming evidence | Falsifying evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Is CAP high quality? | Assigned and awarded work becomes starts, revenue, margin, billing, and cash | CAP rises through pipeline or unsigned work while starts and cash lag |
| Are project estimates reliable? | Final margins remain close to original estimates and claims decline | Recurring unfavorable estimate changes, loss provisions, or late margin reversals |
| Is cash repeatable? | Contract assets, receivables, payables, and retainage normalize through the project cycle | Operating cash depends on contract-asset releases or temporary working-capital timing |
| Did acquisitions earn their cost? | Acquired businesses add organic growth, margin, and cash after integration and partner claims | Acquisition cash, goodwill, debt, and dilution grow faster than owner cash |
| Does vertical integration help? | Materials pull-through improves project margin and return after plant and equipment capital | Materials revenue grows but maintenance capital and cycle risk absorb the benefit |
| Is per-share value improving? | Cash per diluted share rises after debt, conversion claims, SBC, NCI, dividends, and buybacks | Adjusted EPS rises while dilution, debt, or partner claims increase |

## Bottom line

Granite is a physical-conversion company: it turns public and private funding
into roads, bridges, sites, facilities, and material throughput. Its vertical
integration can produce better control than a pure general contractor, and its
CAP growth indicates strong demand.

The forensic conclusion is more restrained. FY2025 operating cash was strong,
but contract assets supported the cash bridge and acquisitions consumed more
than twice the recurring-capital residual. Convertible-debt transactions made
GAAP earnings difficult to read, while materials, equipment, joint ventures,
partner claims, and project estimates create real capital burdens. The durable
thesis requires CAP to become collected cash through stable project margins,
acquisitions to earn their funding cost, vertical integration to improve return
after maintenance capital, and cash per diluted share to rise.
