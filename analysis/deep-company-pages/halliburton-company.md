# Halliburton — oilfield service execution, customer capex, and owner cash

As of 2026-09-13. FY2025 figures are filed GAAP figures unless explicitly
identified otherwise; dollar amounts are in millions.

## How to read this memo

Halliburton provides services and products used to drill, complete, stimulate,
evaluate, and maintain oil and gas wells. A service company converts a
customer's field-development plan into physical work using crews, pumps,
tools, equipment, software, and engineering. GAAP is the accounting rule set
used in the filed statements. Adjusted earnings remove selected items; they
can describe operating performance, but they do not replace cash received.
Productive-asset spending is cash invested in equipment and other assets used
to deliver the services.

## Investment question

Halliburton controls a valuable execution layer between upstream reserves and
producing wells. The control point is strongest where a customer needs
qualified equipment, field crews, engineering, software, and integrated
completion or drilling capability that cannot be substituted quickly. The
investment question is whether this position produces durable common cash
after equipment renewal, working capital, skilled labor, customer pricing,
geopolitical disruption, and the industry's commodity-driven capital cycle.

The company does not own the underlying reserves. It therefore does not earn
the full value of a successful well, but it also avoids much of the geological
depletion risk. Its earnings depend on whether customers keep spending and
whether Halliburton earns adequate returns on the assets and people required
to deliver that spending.

company, but it is a direct test of whether capital returns are being funded by
current cash generation or by balance-sheet flexibility.

## What Halliburton controls

Halliburton reports two principal operating segments:

- **Completions and Production** provides well stimulation, pressure pumping,
  cementing, completion tools, artificial lift, and related services. The
  customer pays for making a drilled well capable of producing at the desired
  rate and for maintaining that production.
- **Drilling and Evaluation** provides drilling services, directional systems,
  logging, formation evaluation, drilling fluids, bits, and project support.
  The customer pays for accurate well placement, geological information, and
  lower execution risk during construction of the well.

The control point is not simply ownership of a pump or tool. It is the
combination of equipment availability, trained crews, engineering knowledge,
customer qualification, field data, and the ability to coordinate multiple
steps under time pressure. A service line is valuable when failure would delay
production, damage a well, create a safety event, or force the customer to
replace a qualified vendor during an active program.

That control point has limits. Large customers can negotiate aggressively,
move work between service providers, defer activity, or vertically integrate
selected tasks. Halliburton's exposure is therefore tied to service intensity,
pricing, utilization, contract terms, and customer concentration rather than
to reserves alone.

## Macro and durability analysis

### Commodity and liquidity exposure

Halliburton's demand is a second-order exposure to oil and gas prices. A
producer must first believe that expected project returns justify drilling and
completion spending. That decision is affected by realized commodity prices,
hedges, decline rates, service prices, access to financing, shareholder-return
targets, and government policy.

When financing is tight or customers prioritize free cash flow, service
companies can see lower activity before the market sees a large change in
production. When supply security or higher prices support new projects,
international contracts can create a longer work cycle, but awards still take
time to become revenue and cash.

The balance sheet provides resilience, not immunity. At December 31, 2025,
cash was $2.206B against long-term debt of $7.158B. The company also had
receivables of $4.942B and inventory of $2.976B. In a downturn, customers may
delay payment while Halliburton still carries crews, equipment, inventory, and
facility costs.

### International breadth versus North American volatility

Q2 2026 revenue was approximately $3.4B internationally and $2.3B in North
America. Completions and Production revenue was $3.2B and Drilling and
Evaluation revenue was $2.5B. This mix matters because international work can
be more contract-based and project-oriented, while North American pressure
pumping is often more exposed to rapid changes in customer activity and
equipment utilization.

International breadth can improve durability, but it adds political risk,
currency risk, local-partner dependence, restrictions on moving equipment, and
collection risk. The correct question is not whether international revenue is
growing; it is whether the added revenue earns an adequate return after these
burdens.

## Earnings quality and forensic questions

### 1. Does adjusted profit describe the recurring business?

Reconcile adjusted operating income and adjusted EPS to GAAP operating
income, net income, and operating cash. Identify impairments, litigation,
restructuring, foreign-exchange effects, tax items, integration costs, and
software-system spending. A charge may be non-cash in the current period while
still representing capital previously funded by shareholders or a future cash
requirement.

### 2. Are customer receivables converting to cash?

Track receivables against revenue by segment and geography, the allowance for
credit losses, overdue balances, contract assets, customer concentration, and
cash collected after quarter end. A service job can be economically complete
while cash remains exposed to a customer dispute, government approval, or
weaker producer balance sheet.

### 3. Is capex sufficient and properly classified?

Separate fleet renewal, maintenance, technology, capacity additions, and
strategic projects. Lower capex can improve near-term free cash flow while
reducing future utilization, reliability, or pricing power. Higher capex is not
automatically growth; it may replace worn pressure-pumping equipment or
preserve the ability to execute contracted work.

### 4. Are buybacks being made across the cycle?

Halliburton repurchased about $1.007B of stock in 2025 while the simple
residual was $1.487B and debt remained $7.158B. Test repurchase prices against
mid-cycle cash, service-line returns, debt maturities, and the probability of a
downturn. A buyback is economically weaker when it is followed by debt-funded
fleet renewal or lower service capacity.

### 5. Do digital and integrated contracts change the economics?

Measure job-level productivity, equipment uptime, pricing, repeat awards,
software revenue, and service-line margin. The claim is credible only if the
company captures part of the benefit through higher price, lower labor per job,
lower equipment downtime, or better retention after software and support costs.

### 6. Who bears environmental, safety, and legal costs?

Read reserves, insurance recoveries, self-insurance, indemnities, regulatory
proceedings, and contract allocation. Field work carries exposure to spills,
well-control events, silica and chemical handling, worker injuries, and
customer property damage. The absence of a large current charge does not mean
the burden is absent; it may be insured, reserved, disputed, or borne through
future compliance spending.

## Lyn Alden, Damodaran, and forensic synthesis

- **Lyn Alden lens:** Halliburton is a physical service business with a liquid
  balance sheet but meaningful operating leverage to customer capex. Inflation
  can raise service prices, but it also raises wages, equipment replacement,
  energy, and financing costs. Durability depends on surviving the downturn
  without destroying service capacity or taking excessive debt.
- **Damodaran lens:** the narrative must be translated into international
  growth, North American utilization, service pricing, reinvestment, and
  incremental return on equipment and technology. Value a normalized service
  cycle, not a peak completion margin or one quarter of customer activity.
- **Forensic lens:** the main presentation risks are treating adjusted EPS as
  recurring cash, treating lower capex as efficiency without testing fleet
  health, treating backlog or awards as collected revenue, treating
  international growth as risk-free, and treating buybacks as value creation
  without a through-cycle cash denominator.

## Valuation framing

The September 11, 2026 market snapshot was approximately $30.034B of equity
value at $35.84 per share. This is about 20 times the FY2025 owner-cash screen
of $1.487B. A simple normalized range of $0.9B / $1.5B / $2.1B at 14x / 17x /
20x implies approximately $12.6B / $25.5B / $42.0B of equity value.

These cases are not price targets. They show the expectation embedded in the
current price: the market requires a recovery toward the middle or upper part
of the service cycle, adequate equipment renewal, continued international
execution, and no major debt or legal shock. The base case assumes that 2025
was below normalized cash but that the business does not return to a prior
peak without paying for the capacity that produces it.

Thesis breakers:

1. International growth requires lower pricing, higher working capital, or
   repeated parent support and fails to earn an acceptable return.
2. North American recovery depends on adding equipment faster than customer
   demand, causing utilization and service pricing to weaken.
3. Receivables, contract disputes, or customer failures repeatedly prevent
   reported operating income from becoming cash.
4. Productive-asset spending falls below the level needed to preserve fleet
   reliability, safety, service quality, and competitive position.
5. Adjusted earnings continue to exclude recurring impairments, legal costs,
   integration costs, or system spending.
6. Dividends and buybacks exceed cash after renewal capex and acquisitions,
   forcing additional debt or reducing operating resilience.
7. Digital, automation, or integrated-contract claims do not produce measurable
   pricing, utilization, labor productivity, or retention gains.
