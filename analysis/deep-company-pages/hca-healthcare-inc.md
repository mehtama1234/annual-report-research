# HCA Healthcare — hospital throughput, reimbursement, labor, and capital returns

Date baseline: 2026-09-13

## The investment question

HCA Healthcare owns and operates hospitals, ambulatory surgery centers,
endoscopy centers, emergency facilities, physician practices, and other care
sites. Its control point is not simply “healthcare demand.” It is the ability to
route patients, clinicians, procedures, and negotiated reimbursement through a
large local care-delivery network.

The investment question is whether that network can turn higher admissions,
better service mix, and outpatient growth into durable common-owner cash after
labor, supplies, uncompensated care, technology, facility renewal, acquisitions,
debt service, and noncontrolling-interest claims.

This is a provider business. It should not be valued like a fee platform or a
medical-device company merely because demand for care is persistent.

## What HCA actually controls

The operating chain is:

`patient need -> facility access and physician referral -> staffed capacity -> care delivered -> payer adjudication -> collection -> reinvestment and common returns`

HCA controls a large portion of facility access, operating workflow, staffing
systems, service-line development, and capital deployment. It does not control
the final reimbursement rate, patient insurance coverage, physician behavior,
labor availability, government policy, or the patient's ability to pay.

At December 31, 2025, HCA operated 190 hospitals, 121 freestanding ambulatory
surgery centers, and 31 freestanding endoscopy centers across 19 states and
England. Scale can improve procurement, revenue-cycle operations, clinical
program development, and use of capital. It also creates a large fixed cost
base that must be staffed and maintained even when a particular service line or
payer cohort weakens.

## Three-year reported bridge

Amounts are $ billions. The post-capex column is a diagnostic, not management's
definition of free cash flow. It excludes acquisitions and therefore should not
be treated as unrestricted common-owner cash.

| Fiscal year | Revenue | Net income attributable to HCA | Operating cash flow | PP&E capex | Post-capex diagnostic |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2023 | 64.968 | 5.242 | 9.431 | 4.744 | 4.687 |
| 2024 | 70.603 | 5.760 | 10.514 | 4.875 | 5.639 |
| 2025 | 75.600 | 6.784 | 12.636 | 4.944 | 7.692 |

The increase in operating cash is real, but the denominator needs care. The
cash-flow statement is consolidated and includes cash attributable to
noncontrolling interests. In 2025 HCA also spent $397M acquiring hospitals and
healthcare entities, distributed $827M to noncontrolling interests, paid $2.207B
of interest, and paid $1.740B of income taxes. A more conservative common-owner
screen starts with the post-capex diagnostic, subtracts acquisitions and
noncontrolling-interest distributions, and then asks whether the remaining cash
can support debt reduction, dividends, and repurchases without relying on new
borrowings.

The 2025 increase in operating cash also benefited from timing. Approximately
$1.3B of estimated tax payments that had been deferred after severe weather in
Tennessee were paid in the fourth quarter. That is a cash timing effect, not a
permanent improvement in operating margin.

## Where the earnings come from

Revenue depends on admissions, emergency visits, outpatient procedures,
ancillary services, physician orders, service mix, negotiated rates, and payer
coverage. Gross charges are not the economic price. The relevant price is the
amount ultimately collected from Medicare, Medicaid, managed-care plans,
commercial insurers, and patients.

In 2025, Medicare represented 14.9% of revenue and managed Medicare 17.8%.
The filing also reports meaningful commercial, Medicaid, and patient-responsibility
exposure. A volume increase can therefore produce weaker cash if it is
concentrated in lower-paying or less-collectible categories.

The most important operating measures are:

- admissions and equivalent admissions;
- emergency visits and inpatient and outpatient surgeries;
- revenue per equivalent admission;
- labor cost per adjusted admission and use of contract labor;
- payer mix and patient-responsibility collections;
- facility occupancy and staffed-bed capacity;
- same-facility margin and cash conversion; and
- capital spending required to maintain and expand service capacity.

In Q2 2026, same-facility admissions rose 2.5% and equivalent admissions rose
2.7%, but management estimated approximately $400M of pretax pressure from
exchange-related coverage loss and approximately $400M of incremental Medicaid
Supplemental Payment Program benefit, primarily from Florida. This is a useful
contradiction to a simple “volume equals durable growth” thesis: policy and
payment mix can offset or reverse the economics of the same physical activity.

## Earnings quality and forensic questions

### 1. Collection estimates are a major earnings control point

At December 31, 2025, HCA recorded approximately $7.674B of implicit price
concessions to reduce revenue and accounts receivable to estimated collectible
amounts. Estimated total uncompensated-care cost was $4.605B, including an
estimated charity-care cost of $1.584B. These are disclosed estimates, not
evidence of misconduct. They are, however, central to the quality of reported
revenue and operating cash.

The test is whether collection experience, write-offs, patient responsibility,
uninsured volume, and payer adjudication support the assumptions. A stable
admission count with deteriorating accounts receivable or rising concessions
would indicate weaker economic demand than the volume headline suggests.

### 2. Supplemental payments must be separated from ordinary pricing power

Medicaid directed and supplemental payment programs can materially affect a
quarter or year. The investor should isolate recurring approved payments from
retroactive approvals, timing benefits, and policy-dependent amounts. HCA's Q2
2026 benefit illustrates why a reported margin improvement is not automatically
a durable improvement in the underlying care-delivery economics.

### 3. Labor is the largest operating burden

Hospitals require nurses, physicians, technicians, therapists, and support
staff at the time care is delivered. Wage inflation, shortages, overtime,
contract labor, workforce disruption, and training affect both capacity and
margin. AI and electronic medical-record investments may improve scheduling,
documentation, revenue-cycle work, or clinical coordination, but they do not
remove the need for staffed beds and qualified clinicians.

Track salary and benefit growth, contract labor, productivity, vacancy rates,
staffed beds, agency expense, and whether reported efficiency comes from a
repeatable operating change or temporary staffing normalization.

### 4. Capex is partly maintenance and partly growth

The $4.944B of 2025 PP&E spending includes maintaining existing hospitals,
modernizing facilities, expanding outpatient capacity, and adding service-line
capability. Treating all of it as growth would overstate owner cash. Treating
all of it as maintenance would understate the return on genuinely incremental
capacity.

The next filings should separate replacement, regulatory, information-system,
and growth projects, then compare each project's added staffed capacity,
procedures, reimbursement, margin, and cash return.

### 5. Buybacks can increase EPS while leverage remains high

HCA ended 2025 with $46.492B of total debt and $1.040B of cash. It repurchased
5.432 million shares for $2.558B in the fourth quarter and had $750M remaining
under the existing authorization at year-end. The board then authorized a new
$10B repurchase program.

Repurchases are economically attractive only if recurring cash after
maintenance capex, acquisitions, noncontrolling-interest distributions,
interest, and working-capital needs exceeds the cash returned. A lower share
count can make EPS rise even while debt increases or the company underinvests in
capacity and workforce resilience.

### 6. Noncontrolling interests are a real claim

HCA's consolidated facilities include partnerships and joint ventures. The
$827M distributed to noncontrolling interests in 2025 is cash that does not
belong to HCA common shareholders. Consolidated EBITDA and operating cash must
therefore be bridged to common-owner cash before applying an equity multiple.

## Lyn Alden lens

Healthcare demand is structurally persistent, but the cash outcome is exposed
to labor inflation, government budgets, insurance coverage, household balance
sheets, interest rates, and capital-market access. HCA's debt makes the
financing cycle important: a strong volume year can still create weak equity
economics if interest, labor, and renewal capital rise faster than collected
reimbursement.

The durability test is not whether people need care. It is whether HCA can
maintain staffed capacity, collect a sufficient price, and fund the facilities
and technology required to keep that capacity productive through a policy and
credit cycle.

## Damodaran lens and valuation

The narrative is “large, locally dense hospital network with outpatient growth,
operating leverage, and disciplined capital returns.” Translate it into:

`admissions and procedures -> payer-adjusted collected revenue -> labor and supply cost -> facility margin -> maintenance/growth capex -> interest, NCI, and taxes -> common cash per share`

At the September 11, 2026 market snapshot, HCA's equity value was approximately
$95.1B. A simple common-owner cash screen is:

| Normalized annual common-owner cash | Multiple | Implied equity value |
|---:|---:|---:|
| $5.5B bear case | 12x | $66B |
| $7.5B base case | 15x | $112.5B |
| $9.0B strong case | 18x | $162B |

These are scenario outputs, not price targets. The base case requires volume
growth to remain collectable, labor cost to remain controlled, supplemental
payments not to provide an inflated denominator, maintenance capex to stay
near current levels, debt not to rise faster than cash, and buybacks to be
funded from residual cash rather than refinancing.

## Incentives and thesis breakers

Management and facility leaders are naturally evaluated on adjusted EBITDA,
adjusted EPS, admissions, revenue per equivalent admission, cost control,
quality, workforce measures, cash flow, and capital returns. Each can be useful,
but the combination can create pressure to emphasize volume, adjusted measures,
temporary payment approvals, working-capital timing, or repurchases before the
underlying cash and service capacity are secure.

The thesis weakens if:

- admissions grow but payer-adjusted revenue per equivalent admission falls;
- implicit price concessions, patient balances, write-offs, or uncompensated
  care rise faster than revenue;
- Medicaid or exchange policy benefits reverse and ordinary operations do not
  absorb the loss;
- labor expense, contract labor, or staffing shortages prevent capacity from
  converting into procedures;
- capex remains high while new capacity produces insufficient incremental cash;
- debt and interest rise while repurchases continue; or
- quality, legal, cybersecurity, or regulatory events create recurring costs
  that management continues to label as unusual.

## Evidence grades and next filing questions

- **Filed fact:** 2025 revenue, attributable net income, operating cash flow,
  capex, debt, interest, tax payments, acquisitions, noncontrolling-interest
  distributions, payer mix, implicit price concessions, and uncompensated-care
  estimates come from the 2025 Form 10-K.
- **Current operating evidence:** Q1 and Q2 2026 volume, policy, and payment
  effects come from the packet and HCA's quarterly materials; they require
  follow-through in cash flow and receivables.
- **Inference:** HCA controls a valuable care-delivery network, but the cleaner
  relationship economics remain conditional on labor availability, payer
  collection, and capital discipline.
- **Unresolved:** recurring maintenance capex, incremental facility returns,
  patient-responsibility collection, contract labor, debt-funded buybacks, and
  the persistence of supplemental Medicaid benefits.

The next filings should answer: Did same-facility volume convert into collected
cash? Did revenue per equivalent admission hold after payer changes? Did
implicit concessions and uncompensated care improve or worsen? What happened to
contract labor and wage cost? Which capex was maintenance versus growth? Did
new capacity earn an acceptable return? Did debt fall after buybacks and
dividends, or did common returns depend on new borrowing?
