# Henry Schein, Inc. — office-based healthcare procurement under forensic review

Research date: 2026-09-13
Evidence window: FY2025 and Q1/Q2 2026
Ticker: HSIC
Evidence grade: A for reported financials and disclosed operating metrics; B for the durability of the specialty/technology mix shift, restructuring benefits, acquisition returns, and cyber normalization.

## Bottom line

Henry Schein controls a recurring procurement relationship with office-based
dental and medical practitioners, alternate-care sites, laboratories, and
clinics. More than one million customers, over 300,000 products, 55
distribution/manufacturing centers, and approximately 150,000 cartons shipped
daily make this a genuine healthcare infrastructure channel. The company is
trying to widen its economics through specialty products, corporate brands,
technology, value-added services, and practice workflow.

The forensic catch is the distance between the strategic narrative and owner
cash. FY2025 OCF was $712M; property capex, capitalized software, and
acquisition/equity-investment cash totaled roughly $390M, leaving about $322M
before shareholder returns and debt. GAAP diluted EPS was $3.27 versus
non-GAAP EPS of $4.97, with a restructuring plan expected to continue through
2027. The business is cleaner than a pure box distributor, but the mix upgrade
must prove itself after recurring restructuring, acquisition, technology, and
working-capital costs.

## Control point and operating model

Henry Schein is paid for being the trusted, centralized procurement and
workflow partner to fragmented healthcare practices. The relationship combines
medical and dental merchandise, equipment, specialty products, corporate
brands, technology, consulting, and value-added services. This creates
switching costs through ordering habits, practice systems, product familiarity,
service relationships, and supply continuity.

The control point is less powerful than a proprietary procedure platform: a
practice can change suppliers, and manufacturers can sell more directly. The
moat therefore depends on fulfillment reliability, breadth, sales support,
pricing, private-label value, and the ability to reduce practice operating
friction.

FY2025 sales grew 4.0%, consisting of 2.6% internal growth, 0.9% acquisition
growth, and 0.5% foreign exchange. OCF fell from $848M to $712M despite higher
sales and slightly higher GAAP net income. The cash-flow change is a useful
counterweight to the adjusted EBITDA increase from $1.061B to $1.101B.

The historical series is distorted by working capital and the 2023 acquisition
outlay. It nevertheless supports a clear conclusion: adjusted EBITDA growth
has not yet demonstrated a proportionate, stable increase in owner cash.

## Cash conversion and reinvestment

The FY2025 cash bridge is approximately:

- OCF: $712M
- Property and equipment: $139M
- Capitalized software: $52M
- Equity investments and acquisitions: $199M
- Other investing: approximately $10M net outflow after affiliate-loan
  proceeds and other items
- Residual before financing: approximately $312M-$322M

The company also repurchased approximately $850M of common stock in 2025,
well above the residual after capex and acquisitions. That capital return may
be funded by debt, cash balances, asset sales, or balance-sheet flexibility; it
should not be read as proof that the operating model generated $850M of
sustainable excess cash.

Working capital is a central field. Q2 2026 showed accounts receivable of
$1.763B and inventory of $2.059B, with cash of only $157M. The business is
exposed to provider credit, product availability, inventory obsolescence, and
the timing of supplier payments. The next filings should reconcile inventory
turns, receivable days, acquisition-related working capital, and cash
conversion against the claimed mix shift.

## Earnings quality and financial engineering

The $2.70 gap between 2025 GAAP and non-GAAP diluted EPS is material. The
company’s non-GAAP framework excludes or adjusts for restructuring, acquired
intangible amortization, contingent consideration, cyber-incident items,
shareholder-advisory matters, value-creation consulting, litigation, and other
selected costs. Some are genuinely unusual; the 2024 Plan is expected to run
through the end of 2027, so integration and efficiency costs cannot yet be
assumed to disappear.

Specific forensic checks:

- FY2025 restructuring and related charges were $105M, versus $110M in 2024;
  the continuing plan is to integrate acquisitions, right-size operations,
  and increase efficiency.
- Acquisition/equity-investment cash was $199M in 2025, while acquisition
  growth contributed only 0.9% of sales growth. The cohort return must be
  measured after integration costs and working capital.
- The company has a consolidated VIE and noncontrolling interests. Q2 2026
  reported $1.763B of receivables and $2.059B of inventory; consolidated
  balances should not be treated as entirely parent-owned operating cash.
- Stock-based compensation, contingent consideration, cyber recoveries, and
  special consulting or shareholder-advisory costs should be included in a
  recurring owner-cost view when they recur alongside the BOLD+1 plan.
- A $250M strategic equity investment from KKR in 2025 and subsequent ownership
  arrangements change the capital structure and deserve dilution/control
  analysis.

No fraud conclusion is established. The central risk is “adjusted margin
transformation” becoming a permanent bridge of recurring integration and
strategic costs.

## Balance sheet and capital allocation

At December 27, 2025, cash was $156M, total assets were $11.215B, HSIC
stockholders’ equity was $3.245B, and noncontrolling interests were $654M.
Reported total debt was approximately $3.1B by year-end. The company amended
its term-loan facility in 2025 for working capital, capex, repurchases,
refinancing, and acquisitions.

The combination of debt, high repurchases, acquisitions, and low cash balances
creates a real liquidity sensitivity even though the recurring distribution
model is resilient. The capital-allocation question is whether buybacks are
being made after the business has funded inventory, technology, restructuring,
and acquisition integration, or whether EPS optics are being prioritized.

## Alden lens: decentralized healthcare and operating necessity

Henry Schein benefits from care moving into dental practices, physician offices,
labs, alternate-care sites, and home-adjacent settings. Routine replenishment,
infection control, practice uptime, and provider efficiency create a durable
demand floor. This is less dependent on a single hospital capital budget than
Stryker and less exposed to manufacturing quality events than Baxter.

The macro risks are practitioner traffic, elective procedures, reimbursement,
labor scarcity, equipment financing, provider liquidity, and tariff/supply-chain
volatility. A fragmented customer base supports channel power, but small
providers can be price-sensitive during a liquidity contraction. The Alden
question is whether Henry Schein is an unavoidable operating partner or a
replaceable distributor whose premium mix is cyclical.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers

The BOLD+1 narrative should be translated into:

1. Internal growth, acquisition growth, FX, price, and volume separately.
2. Gross-margin and operating-margin expansion by distribution, specialty,
   technology, and value-added services.
3. Reinvestment in inventory, distribution centers, software, equipment,
   practice support, acquisitions, and cyber resilience.
4. Cash after recurring restructuring, acquisition integration, stock
   compensation, debt service, and required working capital.
5. A competitive-advantage period based on practitioner workflow attachment,
   corporate-brand adoption, and fulfillment/service density.

At the September 11, 2026 market snapshot, HSIC was approximately $88.36 per
share with equity value of about $10.108B. Against the roughly $322M FY2025
OCF-after-capex/software/acquisitions screen, the market requires sustained
cash conversion improvement. A 25x cash multiple would imply only about $8.1B
of equity value at that denominator; the current valuation requires either
normalized cash materially above FY2025 or a long duration of mix-led growth.

## Incentives and thesis breakers

The proxy should be tested for whether management rewards emphasize adjusted
EPS, adjusted EBITDA, internal sales growth, cash flow, ROIC, acquisition
returns, and integration milestones. The best scorecard would penalize growth
that consumes inventory and debt while rewarding post-deal cash returns and
provider retention.

The thesis weakens if:

- specialty and technology growth does not lift gross margin or cash conversion;
- restructuring charges continue through the end of the BOLD+1 period;
- acquisitions produce sales but not incremental ROIC;
- inventory and receivables absorb the claimed operating improvement;
- buybacks continue while debt rises or cash remains thin;
- cyber incidents or VIE/consolidation effects obscure parent cash; or
- provider liquidity turns routine replenishment into price-led volume.

The next review should capture segment-level margin, inventory turns,
receivable days, acquisition cohorts, restructuring cash, cyber recovery/cost,
KKR ownership/dilution, debt maturities, and exact incentive outcomes.
