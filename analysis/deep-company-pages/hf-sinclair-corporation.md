# HF Sinclair — refining spreads, specialty products, and the cost of maintaining physical capacity

Date baseline: 2026-09-13  
Primary annual filing: [HF Sinclair FY2025 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1915657/000191565726000016/dino-20251231.htm)  
FY2025 results: [HF Sinclair investor relations filings](https://investor.hfsinclair.com/investor-relations/financial-information/sec-filings/sec-filings-details/default.aspx?FilingId=19194360)  
Companion packet: [HF Sinclair company packet](/home/mehtama1/git-repo/annual-report-research/extracted/energy/oil-gas-refining-marketing/hf-sinclair-corporation/company-packet.md)

## Bottom line

HF Sinclair refines crude oil into fuels, produces renewable diesel, sells
branded fuel, operates midstream assets, and owns a lubricants-and-specialties
business. The company operates refineries in Kansas, Oklahoma, New Mexico,
Wyoming, Washington, and Utah. Its specialty portfolio includes base oils,
white oils, waxes, food-grade lubricants, and industrial lubricants.

The control point is physical capacity in useful locations, access to crude and
product logistics, refinery operating knowledge, branded distribution, and
specialty-product relationships. The main earnings driver is still the spread
between the value of refined products and the cost of crude and other inputs.
That spread changes with global supply, regional transportation, outages,
seasonality, regulation, and demand. It is not a stable service margin.

FY2025 net income attributable to HF Sinclair stockholders was $579M, operating
cash flow was $1.315B, property and equipment spending was $449M, precious
metals purchases were $72M, and total debt was $2.769B. The company also spent
$354M on treasury stock and paid $376M of dividends. The cash result was
productive, but it followed a weak 2024 and a much stronger 2023. A single
year should not be used as normal refining cash.

The deepest forensic issue is the difference between reported earnings and
maintenance-adjusted owner cash. Refinery turnarounds are large, periodic
cash costs. Inventory valuation adjustments, renewable-fuel credits, derivative
marks, and working-capital changes can move reported earnings and cash across
periods. HF Sinclair’s planned separation of Lubricants & Specialties also
creates a live test of whether the specialty business deserves a different
capital and valuation framework from commodity refining.

Current judgment: HF Sinclair has a diversified downstream asset base and
valuable specialty and midstream elements, but the common equity should be
underwritten as a cyclical, capital-intensive system. The durable thesis
requires normalized margins, adequate turnaround funding, disciplined
inventory and credit management, manageable environmental and regulatory
costs, and proof that specialty cash is not being used to mask weak refinery
returns.

## What HF Sinclair controls

`crude access and logistics -> refinery conversion -> fuels, renewable products, and specialties -> terminals, brands, and customers -> cash flow`

HF Sinclair controls refinery equipment, processing configurations, crude and
product logistics, storage, some midstream infrastructure, renewable-diesel
units, branded fuel relationships, and specialty-product formulations and
distribution. It does not control crude prices, global product prices,
regional crack spreads, government credit rules, or customer demand.

The control point is stronger where a refinery has a favorable location,
feedstock flexibility, logistics access, reliable operation, or a product
specification that customers cannot replace quickly. It is weaker when margins
are set by global commodity prices or when an outage removes capacity while
fixed costs and environmental obligations remain.

## Segment economics

### Refining

Refining converts crude and other feedstocks into gasoline, diesel, jet fuel,
asphalt, and other products. The relevant margin is the value of products
minus crude and operating inputs, measured against throughput and reliability.
Reported segment income can change sharply with crack spreads, inventory
valuation, planned turnarounds, unplanned outages, and regional basis effects.

Track gross margin per produced barrel, crude charge, utilization, product
yield, feedstock discount, energy cost, turnaround days, unplanned downtime,
maintenance capital, and cash margin after all refinery costs. Do not use a
high-margin quarter as a permanent earnings rate.

### Renewables

Renewable diesel results depend on feedstock costs, product prices, plant
utilization, transportation, renewable identification numbers and other policy
credits, tax credits, and compliance obligations. Credit benefits can be real
cash economics, but they depend on policy, market prices, timing, and
eligibility.

Separate operating margin before credits, credit realization, feedstock margin,
volume, plant reliability, policy exposure, and capital required to comply with
future fuel standards. A tax or credit benefit should not be valued like an
independent recurring service contract.

### Lubricants & Specialties

The specialty business sells products with more formulation, quality, and
customer-relationship content than ordinary fuel. It may have better pricing
stability and customer retention than commodity refining, but it still depends
on base-oil costs, product mix, plant reliability, working capital, and
industrial demand.

The planned separation and retirement of the Mississauga base-oil refining
assets create two tests. First, can the specialty platform operate with a
clearer capital base and strategic focus? Second, are the assets being
separated because they have higher value outside HF Sinclair, or because the
remaining refining system needs to remove a weak or capital-consuming unit?

### Marketing and Midstream

Marketing earns from branded and wholesale fuel volumes, station relationships,
product mix, and logistics. Midstream earns from transportation, storage,
gathering, and related infrastructure. These businesses can reduce exposure to
pure refining margins, but they do not eliminate commodity and volume risk.

Track branded versus unbranded volume, retail margin, wholesale margin,
terminal access, pipeline and storage utilization, contract terms, customer
concentration, and capital required to maintain the network.

## Earnings quality and forensic tests

### 1. Inventory valuation can create a large earnings swing

The lower-of-cost-or-market rule requires inventory to be written down when its
recorded cost exceeds the amount expected to be recovered. The reversal of a
previous valuation adjustment can increase reported earnings without being a
new operating improvement.

In Q1 2026, the packet reports a $604M inventory valuation benefit excluded from
adjusted refinery EBITDA. The 2025 cash-flow statement also included a $417M
lower-of-cost-or-market inventory adjustment. Reconcile the timing and cash
effect of these adjustments before using reported or adjusted earnings as a
normal margin.

### 2. Renewable credits and tax benefits can be economically temporary

Renewable fuels can receive policy credits and tax benefits that support
profitability. The benefit may be valid and recurring under current law, but
its amount depends on feedstock, volume, credit prices, eligibility, and policy
interpretation.

Show renewable margin before and after credits, cash received versus accrued,
credit inventory, compliance liabilities, plant utilization, and the effect of
proposed or changed Treasury and IRS rules. A favorable ruling or deferred
credit should not be placed in a base case without a policy and cash-timing
assessment.

### 3. Turnarounds are maintenance cash, not unusual noise

A turnaround temporarily shuts a refinery unit for inspection, cleaning,
repair, and replacement. The expense can vary by year, but the work is part of
keeping the refinery operating safely and reliably.

HF Sinclair spent $437M on turnaround expenditures in 2025. Analysts should
normalize this cost over a full maintenance cycle and also test whether delayed
work increases future outage, safety, or reliability risk. Excluding recurring
turnaround cash overstates owner economics.

### 4. Working capital can reverse quickly

The 2025 cash-flow statement showed a $171M inventory use of cash and a $345M
accounts-payable use of cash. In 2024, inventory provided $138M and accounts
payable provided $25M. These swings reflect commodity prices, inventory levels,
purchase timing, and settlement terms.

Track inventory barrels and dollars, days of supply, payable days, crude and
product price changes, collateral requirements, margin calls, customer credit,
and cash after year-end. Working-capital release is not permanent cash if the
business must rebuild inventory to operate.

### 5. Derivative marks are not the same as settled margin

Refiners use derivatives to manage exposure to crude, products, foreign
currency, and other prices. Changes in fair value can move earnings before the
underlying physical transaction settles.

Reconcile derivative gains and losses to physical volumes, hedge designation,
settlement cash, margin deposits, and future exposure. A favorable mark can
offset a weak physical margin temporarily; an unfavorable mark can reverse
without proving the long-term business is impaired.

### 6. The balance sheet carries physical and regulatory obligations

HF Sinclair had $2.769B of total debt at December 31, 2025, against $978M of
cash. Refinery assets also carry environmental, safety, closure, remediation,
renewable-fuel compliance, pension, insurance, and litigation exposure.

Review debt maturities, interest, covenant capacity, environmental reserves,
asset-retirement obligations, insurance coverage, letters of credit, required
working capital, and planned capital spending under weak-margin conditions.
Debt should be tested against mid-cycle cash, not a high-spread year.

### 7. Capital returns may compete with asset maintenance

HF Sinclair repurchased $354M of stock and paid $376M of dividends in 2025.
The company also issued and repaid debt during the year. Capital returns are
not evidence that all operating cash is surplus when refineries need periodic
turnarounds and the company is considering a separation and asset retirement.

Compare returns with mid-cycle cash, maintenance capital, turnaround reserve,
debt reduction, specialty investment, environmental obligations, and share
count. A high dividend in a strong spread environment can increase future
financial pressure if management treats cyclical cash as permanent.

### 8. Related-party and control structure require explicit review

HF Sinclair’s history includes the Sinclair transaction, former Holly Energy
Partners assets, branded relationships, and material share repurchases from
REH. Review related-party agreements, purchase prices, governance, voting
power, executive incentives, repurchase terms, and whether capital allocation
serves all common owners equally.

The question is not whether a related transaction is automatically improper.
The question is whether the terms, disclosures, and incentives make normalized
owner cash harder to assess.

## Alden macro and durability lens

HF Sinclair is exposed to global crude supply, refined-product demand, regional
transportation constraints, industrial activity, driving, aviation, weather,
geopolitical disruption, inflation, interest rates, renewable-fuel policy, and
environmental regulation. Refining margins can improve sharply when capacity
is constrained and fall sharply when supply expands or demand weakens.

Liquidity must survive low margins, inventory funding, unplanned outages,
turnarounds, environmental spending, debt maturities, and dividend policy. The
specialty and midstream businesses may make cash less volatile, but they do not
remove the need to fund the refinery system.

## Damodaran narrative-to-numbers lens

The narrative is “HF Sinclair is a diversified downstream platform with
refining, specialty, renewables, marketing, and midstream assets.” The model
must specify:

1. mid-cycle crack spreads and regional basis differences;
2. crude charge, utilization, yield, energy cost, and unplanned downtime;
3. turnaround frequency, cost, and sustaining capital;
4. renewable volumes, feedstock economics, credits, and policy risk;
5. specialty-product price, mix, volume, customer retention, and separation costs;
6. branded and wholesale marketing margins and midstream throughput;
7. inventory, receivables, payables, derivatives, and cash timing;
8. environmental, safety, closure, insurance, and regulatory obligations;
9. debt, interest, dividends, repurchases, and related-party incentives; and
10. normalized common owner cash across a full commodity cycle.

HF Sinclair is in a portfolio-reconfiguration phase. The valuation should not
assume that every segment deserves the same multiple or that specialty cash
automatically offsets refining cyclicality. The separation plan is a testable
capital-allocation event, not proof of value creation by itself.

The September 11, 2026 market snapshot showed equity value of approximately
$19.35B. The cases below use normalized common owner cash after routine
property spending, periodic turnaround funding, working-capital normalization,
and environmental and regulatory reserves.

| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Bear | $600M | 8x | $4.8B |
| Base | $1.000B | 10x | $10.0B |
| Bull | $1.400B | 12x | $16.8B |

The current value is above this conservative screen. That does not prove
overvaluation because the market may be valuing specialty, midstream,
renewables, and a stronger future refining cycle separately. It does mean the
investment case requires either higher normalized cash, higher segment values,
or a favorable separation outcome. The bear case reflects a weak refining
cycle, full maintenance cash, debt, and limited specialty separation value.

## Thesis breakers and next filings

The thesis weakens if:

- reported earnings depend on inventory reversals, derivative marks, or policy credits;
- gross refinery margins fall below the level needed to fund turnarounds and environmental work;
- utilization and crude charge weaken because of outages, feedstock access, or regional oversupply;
- renewable operations require repeated capital while credits or tax benefits decline;
- specialty margins weaken or the separation carries large stranded costs and debt;
- working-capital releases reverse into inventory and receivable funding;
- debt, dividends, and repurchases consume cash needed for maintenance;
- environmental, safety, insurance, or closure liabilities exceed reserves; or
- related-party transactions and capital returns reduce the value available to minority common owners.

The next filings should show segment cash and margin before special items,
turnaround schedule and cost, sustaining versus growth capex, refinery
reliability, inventory valuation, derivative settlement, renewable credits,
specialty separation costs, stranded costs, debt, environmental obligations,
related-party transactions, and cash per common share across a full cycle.

## Evidence grade

- Control point: **B+** — useful physical locations, logistics, brands, and specialty relationships create value, but commodity prices remain outside control.
- Operating economics: **B** — diversified downstream earnings are real, but refinery spreads and turnarounds create wide variation.
- Cash conversion: **B-** — FY2025 operating cash was strong, while working capital, inventory valuation, and periodic maintenance require normalization.
- Balance-sheet resilience: **B-** — debt is manageable in a strong cycle but less comfortable against weak margins and required maintenance.
- Capital allocation: **C+** — dividends and buybacks continued, but maintenance needs and the specialty separation raise the standard for proof.
- Valuation: **C** — current value requires stronger normalized cash or meaningful value realization from specialty and midstream assets.
