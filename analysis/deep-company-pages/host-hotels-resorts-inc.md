## Bottom line

Host Hotels & Resorts owns the physical lodging asset beneath the branded
travel system. Its control point is a portfolio of luxury and upper-upscale
hotels in constrained urban and resort locations; its burden is the building,
labor, renovation, insurance, interest, taxes, and recurring renewal capital.
Hotel managers and brands operate the properties, but Host owns the residual
real-estate economics.

The 2025 picture was constructive: comparable hotel Total RevPAR increased
4.2%, comparable RevPAR increased 3.8%, net income was $776M, Adjusted EBITDAre
was $1.757B, and Adjusted FFO was $1.436B. Yet GAAP net income included $148M
of gains on asset sales and $17M of condominium-sale income, while $644M of
capital expenditures and $235M of interest expense remained real cash claims.
Host's own filing warns that Adjusted FFO, EBITDAre, and related measures omit
cash expenditures for renewal and replacement assets and should not be treated
as liquidity.

Current judgment: Host is the essential contrast to Marriott. It can benefit
from scarce properties, affluent travel, and pricing power, but the property
owner captures a less clean economic stream because it must continuously
reinvest to preserve the asset. A strong RevPAR quarter is not enough; the
investment case depends on property-level cash after maintenance capex,
interest, labor, insurance, and asset recycling.

## What Host actually controls

`scarce lodging real estate -> room inventory and destination access -> hotel operating cash -> asset value and refinancing capacity`

Host Inc. owns approximately 99% of Host L.P. and conducts the business through
that partnership. The combined 10-K explicitly distinguishes the public REIT
from the operating partnership and the roughly 1% outside OP-unit interest.
That structure matters for per-share economics, dilution, distributions, and
the correct equity denominator.

Host's portfolio is more concentrated and physical than Marriott's. The value
comes from location, hotel quality, barriers to new supply, group and leisure
demand, and the ability to renovate or reposition properties. Its competitive
advantage is scarce real estate and asset-management expertise, not a global
loyalty database.

## Earnings quality and forensic tests

### 1. Adjusted FFO is necessary but incomplete

REIT investors need a depreciation-adjusted measure because GAAP depreciation
does not represent the full economic pattern of hotel real estate. Adjusted
FFO is therefore more useful than EPS for recurring property operations. But
Host explicitly says its non-GAAP measures exclude cash expenditures for
long-term assets, interest, and other items, and should not be treated as
liquidity or cash available for distributions.

The correct bridge is:

`Adjusted FFO -> recurring renewal/replacement capex -> interest and debt service -> distributions -> residual owner cash`

The 2025 $2.07 Adjusted FFO per share must be tested against $644M of capex,
not accepted as the shareholder cash yield.

### 2. Asset-sale gains flatter GAAP earnings

Host recorded $148M of gains on property sales and $17M of net income from
condominium sales in 2025. These transactions can be good capital allocation,
but they are not recurring hotel operating earnings. Exclude them from a
through-cycle income model and separately evaluate whether the sales price
demonstrates NAV, whether replacement assets have better returns, and whether
asset recycling is being used to fund distributions.

### 3. RevPAR must be converted into property cash

Room rate and occupancy improvements can be offset by wages, benefits,
insurance, utilities, food-and-beverage costs, management fees, and renovation
disruption. Host's 2025 comparable Total RevPAR rose 4.2%, but comparable hotel
food-and-beverage profit margin was 32.4%, down from 33.5% in 2024.

Required tests:

- RevPAR by market, property, occupancy, and ADR;
- Total RevPAR including food and beverage;
- hotel EBITDA margin after labor and insurance;
- maintenance and renewal capex per room;
- renovation downtime and return on renovated capital; and
- cash flow after interest and required distributions.

### 4. Property carrying value is not automatically NAV

The portfolio's balance-sheet property value does not equal realizable value.
Hotel values depend on normalized NOI, cap rates, property condition, brand
contracts, location, debt, and transaction liquidity. A higher market value
can increase NAV, but a higher cap rate or capital need can reverse it.

The next NAV bridge should use property-level NOI and market cap-rate ranges,
subtract debt and preferred/senior claims, and charge known renovation and
environmental obligations. Do not value a hotel portfolio solely on book value
or current EBITDAre.

### 5. The REIT/OP structure creates a denominator issue

Host Inc. is the general partner and owns approximately 99% of Host L.P.; the
remaining OP units participate economically. Host Inc. common shares and OP
units therefore both matter in diluted FFO and NAV. A per-share model that
uses only public shares can overstate the claim if it ignores partnership
interests and conversion economics.

## Alden macro and durability lens

Host is levered to affluent leisure, group travel, corporate meetings, supply
constraints, wages, energy, insurance, rates, and credit availability. Luxury
and upper-upscale locations may have stronger demand floors, but they also
carry larger renovation and service expectations.

The balance-sheet resilience test is whether Host can fund renewal capital and
debt maturities without selling good assets at weak prices. The liquidity test
is not merely cash on hand; it is unencumbered asset value, revolver capacity,
debt ladder, fixed-rate coverage, and the ability to recycle capital across a
hotel cycle.

Stress cases should include a 15%–25% RevPAR decline, wage and insurance
inflation, a two-year renovation cycle, higher refinancing costs, and a
capital-market shutdown. The durable asset case survives if location scarcity
and normalized NOI support refinancing and NAV; it fails if the portfolio
requires continual sales or debt issuance to fund ordinary maintenance.

## Damodaran narrative-to-numbers lens

The narrative is “high-quality lodging real estate plus active asset management
can generate superior risk-adjusted returns.” The model must specify:

1. normalized property NOI and RevPAR;
2. recurring maintenance and renewal capex;
3. hotel-level and corporate labor, insurance, and management costs;
4. interest expense and debt maturity/refinancing assumptions;
5. acquisition and disposition returns versus the cost of capital;
6. NAV cap-rate ranges; and
7. dividends and buybacks after all required property claims.

Host is a mature real-estate owner in a reinvestment and recycling lifecycle.
Growth through acquisitions is valuable only when the spread between property
returns and funding cost remains positive after renovation capital.

## Management incentives and capital allocation

Management can emphasize Adjusted EBITDAre, Adjusted FFO, and asset sales
because these measures are standard and useful, but the incentives can also
favor visible distribution growth or NAV narratives over long-term property
quality. The scorecard should include:

- comparable property cash flow after labor and insurance;
- capex per room and renovation return;
- asset-sale gains versus replacement cost;
- debt reduction and refinancing spreads;
- acquisitions relative to NAV and cost of capital;
- FFO after recurring renewal capex; and
- share repurchases versus NAV and liquidity.

## Thesis breakers and next-filing questions

The thesis weakens if:

- RevPAR grows but hotel EBITDA margins and cash conversion fall;
- maintenance capex remains below the long-run renewal requirement;
- asset-sale proceeds or condominium income support recurring distributions;
- debt maturities require asset sales or expensive refinancing;
- insurance, labor, or property damage costs become structurally higher;
- acquisitions earn below the cost of capital;
- Adjusted FFO grows while diluted shares and OP-unit claims expand; or
- luxury demand proves more cyclical than location scarcity can offset.

The next filing should capture property-level RevPAR, occupancy, ADR, Total
RevPAR, hotel EBITDA margin, capex by maintenance versus renovation, insurance
claims, debt maturities and rates, asset sales, NAV indicators, FFO bridge,
distributions, repurchases, and OP-unit dilution.

## Skeptical-reader test

The strongest counterargument is that Host is simply a capital-heavy hotel
operator with a low-growth, rate-sensitive cash stream. The strongest response
is the scarcity and quality of its locations, the ability to recycle capital,
and the continuing demand for high-end travel. That response fails if the
portfolio's required renewal capital and debt service consume the apparent
RevPAR and FFO gains.
