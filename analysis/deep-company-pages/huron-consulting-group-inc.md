# Huron Consulting Group — institutional adaptation, labor utilization, and cash realization

As of 2026-09-14. FY2025 figures are filed figures unless explicitly
identified otherwise; dollar amounts are in millions unless stated otherwise.

## How to read this memo

Huron sells consulting, digital services, and managed-services execution to
healthcare, education, and commercial institutions. It reports revenues before
reimbursable expenses (RBR) because reimbursable travel and other costs are
billed to clients at cost and do not represent the economic value of Huron's
services.

RBR is a better activity denominator than total revenue, but it is not profit.
The company must still pay professional labor, contractors, technology,
support staff, acquisitions, financing costs, software investment, and working
capital. Adjusted EBITDA excludes some of those burdens and must be reconciled
to operating cash flow and cash after acquisitions.

## Investment question

The question is whether Huron controls a durable institutional workflow or
whether it is primarily selling highly paid labor into temporary client
projects.

The company is strongest when it becomes embedded in a client's operating
system: revenue-cycle management, clinical or administrative workflow,
technology implementation, research administration, compliance, financial
advisory, or recurring managed services. The relationship weakens when the
client can bring the work in-house, defer the project, replace Huron with a
larger integrator, or reduce scope after the initial transformation.

Read this page with the [institutional adaptation and capability outsourcing
comparison](../cross-sector/institutional-adaptation-and-capability-outsourcing-comparison-2026-08-11.md).

## Control point

Huron controls several parts of the institutional adaptation workflow:

1. **Diagnosis:** industry-specific knowledge of financial, operating, and
   technology problems.
2. **Design:** process redesign, performance improvement, strategy, and
   implementation plans.
3. **Execution:** consultants, digital teams, and managed-services workers
   carry out work that the client cannot staff or scale internally.
4. **Systems:** technology, analytics, software, and data workflows can make
   the relationship more embedded than a one-time advisory report.
5. **Institutional memory:** repeated work can create client-specific process
   knowledge and switching cost.

The control point is not equivalent to a software license. Much of Huron's
value remains dependent on skilled people, utilization, client budgets, and
the ability to deliver measurable improvement.

The Q2 filing says RBR growth came from all three segments and both principal
capabilities. It also says $19.5M of Q2 RBR came from acquisitions, with $10.1M
from RelateCare, Eclipse Insights, and AXIOM. The organic result is therefore
the right starting point, while acquisition contribution must be tested for
retention, margin, integration cost, and cash collection.

Managed-services professionals in healthcare rose to 3,794 from 1,807 a year
earlier, including approximately 1,100 from RelateCare. This makes the growth
more recurring in one sense, but also more labor-dependent. A managed-services
contract can be durable revenue while still producing weak returns if wage
inflation, staffing requirements, or service-level obligations consume the
margin.

## Segment economics

### Healthcare

Healthcare was $232.3M of Q2 RBR and $457.5M for the first half. It includes
managed services, performance improvement, strategy, financial advisory,
technology, analytics, and software. The opportunity is large because health
systems face labor shortages, reimbursement pressure, technology complexity,
and the need to improve operating performance.

The risk is that healthcare clients are financially stressed. A hospital can
need Huron's help and still delay payments, reduce scope, renegotiate fees, or
cancel lower-priority work. Track client credit quality, unbilled services,
receivables, contract terms, staffing levels, and segment margin.

### Education

Education was $139.4M of Q2 RBR and $266.8M for the first half. Digital RBR
grew while education consulting declined in the quarter. This mix change can
improve scalability, but digital products also require ongoing software,
hosting, data, implementation, and support investment.

The test is whether digital growth represents repeatable product revenue or
project work reported through a digital capability label. Examine contract
duration, renewal, implementation labor, deferred revenue, software
capitalization, and customer concentration.

### Commercial

Commercial was $94.0M of Q2 RBR and $185.0M for the first half. Q2 growth was
24.6%, with acquisitions of Treliant and WP&C contributing professionals and
revenue. Commercial margins improved as contractor expense declined, but
acquisition-driven growth must be separated from organic client demand.

The commercial segment is exposed to financial-services, industrial, energy,
and other corporate budgets. These clients may have more flexibility than
health systems or public institutions, but consulting budgets can be cut
quickly when returns are hard to measure.

## Labor, utilization, and delivery capacity

Huron's main production asset is skilled labor. The economic bridge is:

`professionals × utilization × billing rate - compensation - contractors - support and delivery costs`

Revenue growth can be weak if hiring runs ahead of demand. Margin growth can
be temporary if utilization rises because staff are under-deployed later. A
managed-services acquisition can add recurring volume while increasing
supervision, wage, recruiting, quality-control, and service-level obligations.

Track revenue-generating professionals, managed-services headcount, utilization,
billing rates, salary increases, contractor expense, revenue per professional,
project overruns, employee retention, and client delivery outcomes.

The Q2 operating margin evidence was mixed. Segment margins improved in
Education and Commercial, while Healthcare's first-half margin remained 29.3%.
Corporate expenses increased because of support compensation, software and data
hosting, deferred compensation, and share-based compensation. The company can
show segment leverage while corporate investment and financing costs consume
the improvement.

## Cash conversion and accounting quality

The six-month cash flow statement is the most important forensic evidence. Net
income was $54.5M and share-based compensation was $28.7M, but operating cash
flow was negative $41.7M. Cash was absorbed by a $38.9M increase in unbilled
services, a $11.1M increase in receivables, a $6.8M tax movement, and an $88.7M
decline in accrued payroll and related benefits.

The payroll movement may be seasonal or reflect payment timing. It still means
the company paid out cash faster than it collected from clients in the period.
The next filing must show whether unbilled services and receivables convert to
cash and whether accrued payroll rebuilds naturally or was unusually high at
the prior year-end.

Adjusted EBITDA also adds back $7.7M of fair-value changes in contingent
consideration, $2.7M of transaction-related expenses, $1.2M of unrealized
investment losses, $1.1M of restructuring, and $7.8M of amortization in the
first half. Some items are non-cash or acquisition-related, but recurring
acquisition and compensation burdens should not be treated as permanently
absent from the owner model.

## Acquisitions, debt, and capital allocation

Huron paid $27.8M for businesses in the first half and accrued $8.7M of
deferred acquisition payments plus $3.4M of contingent consideration. It also
capitalized $10.1M of internally developed software and spent $10.9M on
property and equipment.

The company repurchased $206.7M of shares while operating cash flow was
negative $41.7M. Financing activities included $579M of bank borrowings and
$256M of repayments. This is not automatically irresponsible, but it changes
the capital-allocation question: the company returned cash to shareholders while
client collections and working capital consumed cash and acquisition
obligations remained.

The next filings should reconcile repurchases with diluted shares, debt,
interest expense, acquisition payments, and normalized cash conversion. A
buyback can create value only when the shares are purchased below intrinsic
value and the company is not borrowing to mask weak operating cash generation.

## Lyn Alden, Damodaran, and forensic synthesis

- **Lyn Alden lens:** Huron's demand is linked to institutional budgets, labor
  shortages, healthcare and education affordability, technology spending, and
  the ability of clients to fund transformation. Its balance sheet is less
  rate-sensitive than a lender, but delayed client collections and wage
  inflation can pressure liquidity during a weak cycle.
- **Damodaran lens:** translate the narrative into RBR, organic growth,
  revenue per professional, utilization, managed-services renewal, segment
  margin, reinvestment, acquisition returns, working capital, debt, and cash
  per diluted share. Value recurring managed services separately from project
  consulting and acquisition-dependent growth.
- **Forensic lens:** treat RBR as a cleaner revenue denominator, not as cash;
  reconcile adjusted EBITDA to GAAP profit and operating cash; charge SBC,
  capitalized software, acquisition payments, contingent consideration,
  working-capital requirements, and repurchases against owner returns.

## Valuation framing

Huron should be valued through three layers:

1. **Recurring managed services:** contract retention, staffing requirements,
   revenue visibility, and cash margin after labor and service obligations.
2. **Digital and implementation:** repeatability, software and data investment,
   project margin, renewal, and client switching cost.
3. **Project consulting and advisory:** utilization, billing rates, backlog or
   pipeline, client budgets, and the cycle-normalized margin.

The valuation must use cash after working capital, software, acquisitions,
debt service, SBC, and repurchases. A 15.6% Q2 adjusted EBITDA margin cannot
be capitalized without explaining why first-half operating cash was negative.

### Initial owner-cash screen — 2026-09-14

The FY2025 Form 10-K reports `$193.4M` of operating cash flow, `$10.4M` of
PP&E purchases, and `$111.6M` of net cash paid for acquisitions. The residual
after PP&E and acquisitions was about `$71.3M`; after `$46.5M` of stock-based
compensation, the initial common-owner measure was only about `$24.8M`.
The comparable SBC-adjusted figures were approximately `$78.5M` in FY2023 and
`$98.1M` in FY2024. The recent fall is important because the company still
reports growth and adjusted EBITDA while acquisition cash and working capital
consume the economic return.

In the first half of 2026, operating cash was negative about `$41.7M`, cash
acquisitions were `$27.8M` plus deferred consideration, and share repurchases
were about `$206.7M`. That is a direct test against the idea that adjusted
EBITDA is already distributable owner cash.

A finance quote retrieved at `2026-09-15 00:15 UTC` showed a share price of
`$156.43` and equity value of approximately `$2.563B`. This is a dated market
input, not filing evidence or a price target.

| Screen | Bear | Base | Bull |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Normalized common-owner cash | `$25M` | `$60M` | `$100M` |
| Equity cash multiple | `20x` | `30x` | `40x` |
| Implied equity value | `$0.500B` | `$1.800B` | `$4.000B` |

The base case assumes working capital normalizes, organic RBR remains positive,
and acquisitions produce cash returns above their purchase and integration
cost. The bear case assumes utilization, collections, or client budgets weaken
while professional compensation remains sticky. The bull case requires
managed-services and digital growth to make cash less dependent on acquisitions
and to support a higher recurring-services multiple. At the dated market value,
the company requires a bull-like cash recovery or a multiple well above the
base case. This screen is not a price target.

## Evidence grades and thesis breakers

| Question | Current grade | What would upgrade or weaken it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Institutional control point | B+ | Repeat-client retention and managed-services renewal remain strong |
| Organic demand | B+ | Growth persists after excluding acquired RBR and pricing effects |
| Managed-services durability | B | Renewals and labor economics produce cash after service obligations |
| Digital scalability | B- | Digital growth converts to recurring margin after software investment |
| Labor economics | B- | Revenue per professional and utilization rise without wage or quality pressure |
| Cash conversion | C | Working-capital outflows reverse and remain positive after acquisitions |
| Acquisition discipline | B- | RelateCare, Treliant, WP&C, and other acquisitions earn returns above capital cost |
| Capital allocation | C+ | Buybacks are funded from normalized cash and improve per-share value |

Thesis breakers:

1. RBR growth depends increasingly on acquisitions while organic growth slows.
2. Client collections and unbilled services continue consuming cash as EBITDA
   rises.
3. Managed-services labor cost, turnover, or service-level requirements reduce
   margins.
4. Healthcare, education, or commercial clients delay projects or renegotiate
   fees because of financial pressure.
5. Digital revenue requires rising software and hosting investment without
   better renewal or cash margin.
6. Acquisition accounting, contingent consideration, and amortization recur
   while adjusted metrics continue to exclude them.
7. Buybacks and acquisitions require additional borrowing while diluted cash
   earnings per share fail to improve.
8. Key-person, professional-liability, data-security, or implementation errors
   damage client trust and renewal rates.
