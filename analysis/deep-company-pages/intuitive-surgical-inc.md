## Why repeated use matters more than the first equipment sale

Intuitive Surgical supplies systems used by clinicians, along with the instruments, accessories and services needed to keep using them. A hospital's initial purchase or lease is only one source of revenue. As clinicians perform procedures, they use instruments and accessories, while the equipment requires service and support. In 2025, instruments and accessories
revenue was $6.019B, systems revenue $2.474B, and services revenue $1.572B,
against total revenue of $10.065B. Operating cash flow was $3.031B and
property-and-equipment purchases were $539.8M.

The investment case depends on continued use after a system reaches the hospital. Training, reliable equipment and an established way of performing procedures can encourage hospitals to keep using the system. More use can generate further instrument and service revenue. But a system delivered to a hospital is not proof of that outcome: the hospital needs suitable procedures, trained clinicians, staff and an operating schedule that makes the equipment useful.

The payment arrangement also matters. A direct sale, a fixed lease and a payment tied to use produce different revenue and cash patterns. A low initial payment may help a hospital obtain a system, while leaving Intuitive more dependent on future use to collect its return. That is a business-model question to investigate, not evidence by itself that the accounting is misleading.

Our judgment is that the large instrument and service business provides evidence of demand beyond new equipment sales. The unresolved investment question is how much cash each share can ultimately receive after manufacturing, product development, support, expansion and share compensation. A business can grow successfully while delivering a disappointing investment return if its purchase price assumes even stronger growth. The valuation section therefore tests explicit cash assumptions rather than treating procedure growth as sufficient proof of value.

## From a hospital's equipment decision to repeated revenue

The hospital must decide whether the system fits the care it provides and whether it can use the equipment enough to justify the expense. That decision includes equipment, instruments, servicing, training and staff time. It also depends on what insurers and other payers will reimburse for the procedures. Reimbursement means payment for covered care; it is not necessarily equal to the hospital's full cost.

Two measures should be kept separate. The **installed base** is the collection of systems already placed with customers. **Utilization** describes how much those systems are used. A larger installed base creates the possibility of more procedures; utilization shows whether that possibility is being realized.

For the supplier, the next question is how much revenue and profit each procedure produces. Different procedures and products can use different instruments and have different costs. Procedure growth, instrument revenue growth and profit growth therefore need not be identical. Read those measures together, along with the terms under which hospitals pay.

This connects the company to hospital budgets, payment coverage, clinical acceptance, regulatory approval and staffing. The advantage is repeat use by customers who have already adopted the equipment. The obligation is to keep that equipment useful, supported and competitive while funding the next generation of products.

## 2026 first-half filing update

The six months ended June 30, 2026 show continued operating scale but also
make the normalization burden more visible. Revenue was $5.663B, with product
revenue of $4.757B and service revenue of $906M. Net income was $1.649B and
operating cash flow was $1.973B, compared with $1.297B in the prior-year
period.

The cash increase should not be treated as a clean run-rate step-up. The
first-half cash flow included $370.3M of deferred-tax expense and $419.4M of
share-based compensation in the reconciliation, while inventory used $514.6M,
prepaids and other assets used $179.0M, and accrued compensation and benefits
used $229.7M. Investing included $215.9M of PP&E purchases and $528.2M for an
acquisition, intellectual property, and other investing activities. A simple
OCF less those two visible investment uses leaves approximately $1.229B before
securities activity, taxes, equity claims, and the distinction between growth
and maintenance capital.

This is still strong evidence of a cash-generative installed-base platform, but
it changes the valuation question. The investor must test whether the 2026
working-capital and deferred-tax effects reverse, whether the acquisition adds
incremental procedure or service economics, and whether $419.4M of SBC plus
repurchases represents a persistent owner claim. The quarterly demand case is
stronger; the normalized per-share owner-cash case remains unproven.

## Earnings quality and accounting tests

### 1. The main quality signal is the revenue composition, not adjusted EPS

Instruments and accessories provide a better test of installed-base health than
systems revenue. A system placement can be affected by a single hospital
budget, trade-in, lease structure, or quarter-end timing. Instruments are
consumed through procedures and therefore connect revenue to actual utilization
more directly.

The correct reconciliation is:

`installed systems -> procedures per system -> instruments revenue per procedure -> service revenue -> hospital economics`

The public disclosures provide important pieces but do not fully disclose
revenue per system cohort or profitability by utilization cohort. That missing
granularity is a key research boundary.

### 2. Usage-based lease revenue creates timing and credit questions

The 10-K says operating lease revenue, including Ion systems, was $874M in
2025, of which $531M was variable lease revenue tied to usage-based
arrangements. Usage-based structures can align Intuitive's revenue with
procedures, but they also increase exposure to hospital utilization and credit
risk.

The forensic tests are:

- Separate cash collected from revenue recognized under usage-based leases.
- Track receivables, deferred revenue, lease assets, and allowance for credit
  losses by arrangement type.
- Compare system placements under sale, traditional lease, and usage-based
  contracts.
- Test whether variable lease revenue grows because procedures grow or because
  contract terms shift revenue timing.
- Determine who bears residual value, maintenance, and underutilization risk.

Usage-based revenue is not inherently aggressive accounting. It is a reminder
that the economic risk moved from hospital purchase price toward future
procedure volume.

### 3. Inventory growth is a real investment and a possible reversal point

2025 inventory increased cash usage by $1.063B, primarily to support business
growth and mitigate disruption risks. That can be rational capacity investment
for a company expanding procedure volume and manufacturing. It can also become
a quality problem if demand, product mix, or new-system adoption slows.

The next filings should compare inventory growth with instruments revenue,
systems placements, lead times, turns, obsolescence reserves, and gross margin.
Revenue can remain strong while inventory quietly accumulates ahead of a
slower procedure or product cycle.

### 4. Stock compensation is large enough to include in owner-cash analysis

Share-based compensation expense was $788.2M in 2025, $676.8M in 2024, and
$592.8M in 2023. This is non-cash in the period, but it is not economically
free: it represents future dilution or the cost of issuing equity to employees.
The company also paid $416.3M for share repurchases in 2023 and $2.295B in
2025, according to the companyfacts record and 2025 filing disclosures.

The exact repurchase and share-settlement timing should be reconciled to the
statement of stockholders' equity. A company can report strong EPS growth while
using substantial cash to offset dilution. Owner-cash analysis should show
both repurchases and equity compensation rather than treating repurchases as
an optional afterthought.

of whether each increment of capacity earns a return above its opportunity cost.

## Balance-sheet resilience and liquidity

Intuitive ended 2025 with approximately $3.368B of cash and cash equivalents
and $20.459B of total assets. Total liabilities were approximately $2.517B and
equity approximately $17.942B. The company has no debt-driven capital structure
problem comparable to the highly levered or property-heavy companies in the
archive.

That balance-sheet strength is a meaningful durability advantage. It allows
Intuitive to fund R&D, facilities, inventory, training, clinical development,
and market expansion without depending heavily on refinancing conditions.

The macro risks instead come through demand and the healthcare system:

- Hospitals can defer capital purchases or limit utilization.
- Reimbursement pressure can reduce the financial benefit of procedures.
- Interest rates affect hospital budgets and the value of long-duration growth
  expectations even if Intuitive has ample cash.
- Tariffs and supply-chain disruption matter because a significant majority of
  instruments and accessories are manufactured in Mexico and endoscopes in
  Germany, according to the filing risk disclosures.
- Regulatory or clinical events can damage acceptance more quickly than a
  normal industrial cycle.

## Operating performance: the installed-base test

At the end of 2025, the packet reports more than 11,100 da Vinci systems and
995 Ion systems installed. Q1 2026 worldwide procedures grew approximately
17%, with da Vinci procedures up approximately 16% and Ion procedures up
approximately 39%. Instruments and accessories revenue grew 23% in Q1 2026.
Q2 2026 revenue was $2.89B and GAAP net income attributable to Intuitive was
$818M.

These metrics are mutually reinforcing: systems, procedures, instruments, and
services are all moving in the same direction. But they are not equally
valuable:

- System placements expand future capacity but can carry lower near-term
  margins, financing support, or trade-in effects.
- Procedures show actual utilization but can be influenced by mix, geography,
  clinical specialty, and reimbursement.
- Instruments show repeated use and are closer to a consumable cash engine,
  but still require manufacturing capacity and quality control.
- Services show installed-base attachment but include obligations that require
  field labor and parts.

The next proof should be revenue and gross-profit contribution per installed
system, not only the total system count.

## Lyn Alden lens: durability, liquidity, and institutional constraints

Intuitive benefits from a durable need for less invasive procedures, surgical
capacity, clinical standardization, and technology that can improve hospital
workflow. Its installed base and training ecosystem create inertia because a
hospital that builds surgeon capability and procedure pathways around a system
does not switch as casually as a consumer product buyer.

The burden is not absent. Hospitals must fund systems, operating rooms,
surgeon training, staffing, maintenance, and procedure utilization. Intuitive
also carries regulatory, clinical, product-safety, manufacturing, inventory,
and research burdens. The company's strong balance sheet makes it more
resilient to financial stress, but it cannot force a hospital to use a system
if staffing or reimbursement is weak.

The Alden-style macro read is therefore high quality but not cyclical-proof:
the platform is embedded in an essential institutional workflow, while
purchase timing and utilization remain exposed to hospital liquidity and
healthcare policy.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers and lifecycle

The growth narrative has five distinct pieces:

1. More procedures per existing system.
2. More systems placed into hospitals and ambulatory surgery centers.
3. More recurring instruments and accessories per procedure.
4. More services, learning, and software attached to the installed base.
5. New platforms such as da Vinci 5 and Ion expanding the addressable
   procedure categories.

The valuation model must not combine these into one perpetual growth rate.
Procedure growth is a utilization assumption; system growth is a placement and
capital assumption; instruments growth is a recurring-use assumption; and
platform expansion is a regulatory, clinical, and reinvestment assumption.

### Reinvestment questions

- Is the next dollar of manufacturing capacity aimed at current instruments,
  new systems, or future platforms?
- Does increasing inventory improve service levels and revenue, or does it
  lower turns and create obsolescence risk?
- Are new system placements economically attractive after training, support,
  usage-based financing, and service obligations?
- Is da Vinci 5 an upgrade cycle with high customer returns, or a large capital
  transition that temporarily depresses hospital returns and Intuitive cash?
- Can Ion procedure growth reach scale without requiring disproportionate
  clinical, sales, and service spending?

### Reverse-valuation questions

The operating analysis comes from filings. The separate valuation screen below uses the archive's historical market snapshot, not a live quote. A reverse valuation asks what future results would justify a specified purchase price. It should identify the assumptions about:

- procedure growth over a full cycle;
- system placements and the share under usage-based arrangements;
- instruments revenue per installed system;
- gross margin after manufacturing expansion and tariff exposure;
- service attachment and field-support intensity;
- R&D and clinical-development spending;
- inventory turns and cash conversion;
- share-based compensation and repurchase requirements; and
- the duration of high growth before the platform matures.

The core risk is expectation risk. A wonderful business can still produce a
poor investment result if the market assumes that procedure growth, pricing,
margin, system placement, and new-platform adoption all remain exceptional at
once.

## Management incentives and governance questions

The 2025 filing incorporates executive compensation information by reference
to the proxy and discloses the 2010 Incentive Award Plan, performance stock
units, restricted stock units, and options. The company also has a clawback
policy. The economic review should focus on whether incentives reward:

- systems placed versus systems profitably utilized;
- revenue and adjusted earnings versus cash after inventory and capex;
- procedure growth versus hospital return on invested capital; and
- short-term EPS versus long-term platform safety, quality, and clinical
  adoption.

The $788.2M stock-compensation expense makes the dilution question material.
The company has strong cash resources, but repurchases should be evaluated
against the price paid, the need to offset employee equity, and the alternative
use of cash for facilities, research, or acquisitions.

These are incentive and capital-allocation tests, not allegations of improper
conduct.

## Who controls the relationship and who carries the burden

| Layer | Intuitive | Hospital / care system |
|---|---|---|
| Control point | Surgical platform, instruments, service, training, data, and workflow standard | Patient access, surgeon staffing, operating-room capacity, payer relationships |
| Repeat economics | Instruments, accessories, service, learning, upgrades, and usage-based revenue | Procedure reimbursement and patient throughput |
| Capital burden | Manufacturing, R&D, regulatory, inventory, facilities, quality, and field support | System acquisition, OR investment, staffing, training time, and utilization |
| Main risk | Product acceptance, safety, regulation, supply chain, and platform execution | Hospital liquidity, reimbursement, labor, procedure mix, and capital budgets |
| Switching friction | Surgeon learning, workflow integration, installed instruments, service, and clinical familiarity | Ability to compare alternatives and obtain acceptable procedure returns |

Intuitive controls the technology and workflow layer; hospitals carry the
patient-care and utilization burden. This is a healthcare example of cleaner
middle-layer economics, but the control point remains dependent on the burden
carrier's return.

## Thesis breakers and next-filing watchlist

### Thesis breakers

- Procedure growth slows materially while system placements continue, implying
  utilization rather than capacity is the constraint.
- Instruments and accessories growth trails installed-base growth for several
  periods, suggesting lower utilization or substitution.
- Usage-based lease receivables, credit losses, or deferred revenue grow faster
  than related cash collections.
- Inventory grows faster than procedure and instruments demand, or turns and
  obsolescence reserves deteriorate.
- Hospital capital constraints, reimbursement changes, or staffing shortages
  reduce placements or actual use.
- Gross margin falls as da Vinci 5, Ion, or manufacturing expansion scales.
- Service revenue growth requires disproportionate field labor and parts.
- Regulatory, safety, quality, or clinical-acceptance problems damage trust.
- Stock compensation and repurchases consume a growing share of recurring cash
  without improving per-share economics after dilution.
- New-platform investments increase R&D and capex without expanding procedures
  or returns on incremental capital.

### Next filing fields

1. Worldwide procedures by platform and geography.
2. System placements, installed base, trade-ins, leases, and usage-based
   arrangements.
3. Instruments and accessories revenue per installed system and per procedure.
4. Services revenue, attachment, contract duration, and field-support costs.
5. Gross margin by revenue type where disclosed and total operating leverage.
6. Inventory, turns, reserves, supply commitments, and production capacity.
7. Accounts receivable, usage-based lease collections, deferred revenue, and
   credit-loss allowances.
8. PP&E and capitalized software, separating maintenance from expansion.
9. R&D, regulatory milestones, clinical evidence, and da Vinci 5/Ion adoption.
10. Hospital and ambulatory-surgery-center capital-spending conditions.
11. Stock compensation, diluted shares, repurchases, and incentive metrics.
12. Cash flow after capex, inventory normalization, and equity compensation.

## Final judgment

Intuitive Surgical is a strong healthcare control-point case because its
installed platform converts institutional capital spending into recurring
procedure-linked instruments, accessories, service, and training demand. The
2025 revenue mix and cash flow support that conclusion more strongly than a
simple system-count narrative.

The company is also a useful warning against over-simplification. Systems are
not the same as procedures, procedures are not the same as hospital profit,
and operating cash flow is not the same as unburdened owner cash when inventory,
manufacturing capacity, R&D, capex, and stock compensation are substantial.

> Intuitive currently appears to own one of healthcare's strongest recurring
> workflow layers, but the investment case remains conditional on utilization,
> hospital returns, inventory discipline, platform reinvestment, and the price
> paid for future growth.

## Evidence grade and unresolved questions

- **Filing fact:** FY2025 and first-half 2026 systems, procedures, instruments,
  service revenue, inventory, capex, SBC, cash, repurchases, and lease exposure
  are anchored to the annual and quarterly filings.
- **Disclosed/packet-backed:** procedure growth, Ion/da Vinci adoption, and
  utilization claims are operating evidence but not complete hospital-return
  evidence.
- **Inference:** Intuitive controls a qualified installed-base workflow with
  recurring consumables and service pull-through, while hospitals carry staffing,
  reimbursement, and capital-budget constraints.
- **Unresolved:** the next filing must show procedure-linked utilization,
  inventory normalization, service economics, platform reinvestment, SBC-adjusted
  cash, and hospital payback. Systems growth without utilization or owner cash
  would weaken the platform thesis.
