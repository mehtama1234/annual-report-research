# Johnson & Johnson — drug lifecycle, MedTech breadth, litigation, and reinvestment

Date baseline: 2026-09-13

## The investment question

Johnson & Johnson combines Innovative Medicine and MedTech. Innovative Medicine
depends on research, regulatory approval, launch execution, patent protection,
pricing, and reimbursement. MedTech depends on clinical evidence, procedure
volume, hospital budgets, physician adoption, manufacturing, and quality.

The investment question is whether the combined portfolio creates durable cash
per share after replacing mature medicines, funding clinical development,
maintaining device quality, absorbing litigation, buying new assets, and paying
the dividend.

Diversification can reduce dependence on one product. It does not remove
product lifecycles. The company can report sales and adjusted EPS growth while
the economic cost of replacing lost products appears as acquisitions,
in-process R&D, pipeline spending, or litigation cash.

## What Johnson & Johnson actually controls

The operating chain is:

`unmet clinical need -> evidence and regulatory approval -> physician/patient adoption -> reimbursement and procedure use -> repeat sales or treatment -> cash -> pipeline and portfolio renewal`

J&J controls research programs, clinical development, regulatory submissions,
manufacturing, sales execution, device systems, and commercial relationships.
It does not control trial outcomes, regulators, patent courts, payer pricing,
physician procedure volume, hospital budgets, or the public response to safety
and litigation issues.

Innovative Medicine sales were $60.401B in 2025 and MedTech sales were
$33.792B. Those are not interchangeable engines. Medicine has higher product
and patent-lifecycle risk; MedTech has procedure, evidence, quality, and
hospital-budget risk. A consolidated multiple should reflect both.

year-end. Capital returns therefore need to be judged alongside portfolio
renewal and litigation reserves, not only dividend history.

## Where the economics sit

### Innovative Medicine: science and patent time

The pharmaceutical business earns high returns when a medicine solves an
important problem, receives approval, gains adoption, and remains protected
long enough to recover research and launch spending. The economic asset is not
just current sales; it is the remaining period of profitable exclusivity plus
the probability-weighted pipeline that replaces it.

STELARA erosion illustrates the lifecycle problem. Management reported 2025
underlying growth excluding STELARA pressure, but a strong adjusted growth rate
excluding a declining product is not the same as replacing its cash economics.
Track product-level sales, price, volume, competition, patent and exclusivity
dates, pipeline probability, launch cost, and the required return on acquired
assets.

### MedTech: procedure workflow and installed clinical relationships

MedTech earns through surgical, cardiovascular, orthopedic, vision, and other
products that enter clinical workflows. Physician familiarity, evidence,
training, hospital contracts, and product breadth can create switching friction.
Recurring economics arise from procedure volume, consumables, implants,
replacement products, and service support.

The burden is real: clinical trials, quality systems, recalls, manufacturing,
sales specialists, regulatory submissions, hospital capital budgets, and
procedure utilization. MedTech growth is durable only if new products improve
clinical or economic outcomes enough to earn a place in the workflow.

### Portfolio breadth: risk reduction versus capital replacement

Combining drugs and devices can smooth individual product cycles. It can also
make the group look stable while each segment requires different forms of
reinvestment. A mature medicine can be replaced by an acquisition; a device
franchise can be defended through R&D and salesforce spending; litigation can
require cash unrelated to current product growth.

The correct question is not whether the portfolio is diversified. It is whether
each segment earns an attractive return on the capital needed to preserve its
future cash flow.

## Earnings quality and forensic questions

### 1. Adjusted earnings can exclude the cost of portfolio renewal

J&J reports adjusted operational sales and adjusted EPS to improve comparison
across periods. Those measures can be useful, but they may exclude acquisition
amortization, restructuring, litigation, in-process R&D, and other items. Some
are genuinely unusual; others are recurring costs of operating a science and
device portfolio.

The test is to compare GAAP earnings, adjusted earnings, cash acquisitions,
pipeline R&D, acquired IPR&D, amortization, litigation payments, and share
count. If the company must repeatedly buy or license products to replace patent
loss, those outlays belong in the economic earnings analysis.

### 2. Acquisitions materially changed 2024 and 2025

Acquisitions used $15.146B in 2024 and $17.541B in 2025. The 2025 figure
includes major portfolio additions, while acquired IPR&D used another $385M.
These transactions can create substantial future value, but they also create
goodwill, intangible amortization, integration work, debt, and execution risk.

For each transaction, track purchase price, acquired sales, operating income,
pipeline probability, integration costs, debt funding, amortization, and cash
return. “Growth excluding STELARA” is a useful operating view, but it does not
prove that acquired replacement cash will exceed the capital spent to obtain
it.

### 3. Litigation is a financial claim, not merely an adjusted item

J&J disclosed approximately $3.4B of reserves related to talc matters and
continues to face product-liability, medical-device, pharmaceutical, and other
claims. A reserve is an accounting estimate; the economic burden includes
settlements, legal fees, insurance recoveries, financing, reputation, and
management attention.

The next filings should reconcile cash payments, reserve additions and releases,
case developments, insurance coverage, and the effect on capital allocation.
Repeated litigation cash should not be treated as irrelevant to normalized
owner earnings.

### 4. Working capital weakened the 2025 cash conversion

In 2025, accounts receivable used $1.781B and inventories used $1.450B of cash.
Accounts payable and accrued liabilities provided $2.377B, while other assets
and liabilities were a large net use. This does not indicate improper reporting.
It does mean operating cash flow should be tested against receivable days,
inventory turns, channel inventory, returns, rebates, and payment timing.

### 5. Product growth must be separated from price, mix, and currency

Operational sales growth was 5.3% in 2025, while adjusted operational growth
was 4.2%. The difference reflects currency and other adjustments. For a
portfolio with patent loss and device launches, reported sales should be split
into volume, price, mix, new products, acquired revenue, divestitures, and
currency.

The strongest evidence is product-level growth with stable or improving cash
margin, not a consolidated figure supported by price increases or acquisitions.

### 6. Dividend durability depends on renewal spending

J&J paid $12.381B of dividends in 2025 against $24.530B operating cash flow,
but also spent $4.832B on PP&E, $17.541B on acquisitions, and $385M on acquired
IPR&D. The dividend can be funded in a given year while the portfolio is
under-reinvested or leverage rises.

Compare dividends with cash after sustaining R&D, quality, litigation,
maintenance capex, and the amount required to replace lost products. A long
dividend record is evidence of capital-access discipline, not proof that every
future payout is covered by recurring owner cash.

## Lyn Alden lens

J&J benefits from durable healthcare needs, but its cash cycle is governed by
science, regulation, patent time, hospital budgets, public policy, and legal
claims. Higher rates raise the cost of large acquisitions and reduce the value
of long-duration pipeline cash flows. Inflation increases clinical-trial,
manufacturing, labor, and sales costs.

The strongest version of the thesis is a portfolio where internal R&D and
MedTech innovation replace mature products at returns above the cost of capital
and acquisitions remain selective. The weaker version is a company that uses
scale and adjusted EPS to conceal that patent replacement and litigation require
continuous external capital.

## Damodaran lens and valuation

The narrative is “a diversified, high-quality healthcare company whose drugs,
devices, and pipeline support durable growth and a dependable dividend.”
Translate it into:

`clinical evidence -> approval and adoption -> product cash flow -> patent/procedure lifecycle -> replacement R&D or acquisition -> litigation and quality claims -> common cash per share`

At the September 11, 2026 market snapshot, J&J's equity value was approximately
$648.0B. A normalized common-earnings screen is:

| Normalized annual common earnings | Multiple | Implied equity value |
|---:|---:|---:|
| $15B bear case | 18x | $270B |
| $20B base case | 22x | $440B |
| $27B strong case | 26x | $702B |

These are scenario outputs, not price targets. The base case requires
Innovative Medicine to replace patent losses, MedTech to produce real organic
growth, litigation cash to remain manageable, and acquisitions to supplement
rather than substitute for internal innovation.

The current market value therefore assumes substantial confidence in pipeline
replacement and the durability of the dividend after portfolio renewal.

## Incentives and thesis breakers

Management incentives emphasize operational sales, adjusted EPS, cash flow,
innovation, pipeline progress, MedTech growth, and capital deployment. These
metrics can reward real progress, but they can also favor acquisitions,
adjusted exclusions, or buybacks before the replacement economics are proven.

The thesis weakens if:

- Innovative Medicine growth depends on repeated acquisitions rather than
  successful internal launches;
- sales excluding patent-pressure products grow but acquired cash returns remain
  below the cost of capital;
- MedTech growth is driven by price or acquisitions while procedure volume and
  organic utilization weaken;
- litigation reserves and cash payments recur at a level that materially reduces
  owner cash;
- working-capital uses persist while adjusted earnings rise;
- debt remains elevated after acquisitions and dividends; or
- quality, regulatory, or product events reduce trust and require recurring
  remediation.

## Evidence grades and next filing questions

- **Filed fact:** 2025 sales, segment sales, GAAP net earnings, operating cash
  flow, capex, acquisitions, IPR&D, working capital, dividends, repurchases,
  debt, and talc reserves are anchored to the 2025 Form 10-K.
- **Current operating evidence:** Q1 and Q2 2026 sales, segment performance,
  adjusted EPS, and pipeline claims come from the preserved packet and quarterly
  filings; they require later cash and lifecycle confirmation.
- **Inference:** J&J controls valuable science and clinical workflows, but
  diversification lowers single-product risk without eliminating renewal,
  litigation, regulatory, and capital-allocation burdens.
- **Unresolved:** product-level organic growth, pipeline replacement returns,
  MedTech procedure economics, litigation cash, acquired intangible value,
  debt, and dividend coverage after portfolio renewal.

The next filings should answer: Did growth excluding patent-pressure products
come from internal launches or acquisitions? Did MedTech growth convert into
cash after quality and hospital-budget costs? Did acquired businesses earn above
funding cost? Did litigation reserves and payments rise? Did cash per share grow
after acquisitions, R&D, capex, dividends, and buybacks?
