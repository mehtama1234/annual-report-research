# JPMorgan Chase — deposit scale, market earnings, credit reserves, and capital discipline

## The investment question

JPMorgan Chase combines consumer and commercial banking, credit cards, payments, investment banking, trading, asset management, and a large technology operation. Its control points are low-cost deposits, customer relationships, payment rails, underwriting knowledge, market liquidity, and the ability to spread fixed compliance and technology costs over a very large balance sheet.

The same scale creates obligations. The bank must fund loans, absorb credit losses, maintain liquidity and regulatory capital, pay employees, invest in technology, manage market and counterparty risk, and satisfy legal and supervisory requirements. The investment question is whether normalized earnings after those costs justify the valuation through a full credit and liquidity cycle.

Related first-principles explanation: [Financial intermediation, fees, float, and liquidity](../first-principles/financial-intermediation-fees-float-and-liquidity.md).

## Filed operating bridge

JPMorgan reported FY2025 total net revenue of $182.447B and net income of $57.048B. Net income applicable to common stockholders was $55.681B and diluted EPS was $20.02. The bank ended 2025 with approximately $4.425T of assets and $2.559T of deposits.

| Fiscal year | Total net revenue ($B) | Net income ($B) | Common net income ($B) | Provision for credit losses ($B) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2023 | 158.10 | 49.55 | 47.76 | 9.32 |
| 2024 | 177.56 | 58.47 | 56.87 | 10.68 |
| 2025 | 182.45 | 57.05 | 55.68 | 14.21 |

Net income is more informative than industrial free cash flow for a bank because deposits, loans, securities, and regulatory capital are operating resources rather than simple working capital. The common-owner denominator is normalized earnings after credit losses, funding costs, compensation, technology, legal and compliance costs, capital requirements, and prudent balance-sheet growth.

## Q2 2026 checkpoint: exceptional earnings still require normalization

JPMorgan reported Q2 2026 net income of `$21.2B`, or `$7.70` per share. Net
income excluding significant items was `$16.9B`, or `$6.14` per share. Reported
revenue was `$57.3B`, managed revenue was `$58.0B`, and reported expenses were
`$27.3B`.

The quarter shows both scale and normalization risk. Credit costs were `$2.5B`,
including `$2.4B` of net charge-offs and a `$149M` net reserve build. Average
loans grew `10%` year over year and average deposits grew `7%`. Those figures
support continued operating momentum, but they also increase the amount of
balance sheet that must be tested through a weaker credit cycle.

Capital remained strong: the standard and advanced CET1 ratios were `14.1%`
and `14.2%`, cash and marketable securities were approximately `$1.5T`, and
ROTCE was `29%` (`23%` excluding significant items). The capital ratios support
resilience; they do not make all earnings distributable because regulatory
capital, stress requirements, loan growth, and risk-weighted assets change with
the portfolio.

The Q2 evidence should therefore be read in three layers:

1. recurring net interest income, deposits, payments, and client activity;
2. market and investment-banking revenue that can be unusually strong in a
   favorable environment; and
3. significant items, including Visa and certain equity-investment effects,
   that should not be used as ordinary earnings without a separate recurrence
   judgment.

The next filing should reconcile reported earnings to common earnings after
credit costs, deposit repricing, market normalization, capital accumulation,
technology and control spending, and the cost of any acquired or expanding
consumer-credit portfolio. For a bank, “cash conversion” means that earnings
remain available after the balance sheet has been provisioned and capitalized;
it does not mean that reported net income can be treated as unrestricted cash.

## Where the economics sit

The basic chain is: customer deposits and funding, loans or market activity, interest and fee revenue, credit and market losses, employee and technology expense, capital retention, and common distributions. Each business line has a different exposure.

Net interest income was $95.443B in 2025, up from $92.583B in 2024 and $89.267B in 2023. The spread depends on loan yields, deposit pricing, funding mix, competition for deposits, and the shape of the yield curve. A large deposit franchise can lower funding cost, but customers can move cash when rates or confidence change.

Noninterest revenue was $87.004B in 2025. Markets, investment banking, payments, asset management, and card fees provide diversification, but several lines depend on issuance, trading activity, asset values, and client risk tolerance. A record year in markets is not a permanent run rate.

JPMorgan spent $54.487B on compensation, $11.029B on technology, communications, and equipment, $12.356B on professional services, and $5.531B on marketing in 2025. Technology spending is partly a growth investment and partly a required cost of security, resilience, compliance, fraud control, and competition. It should not be treated as discretionary simply because it is labeled investment.

## Earnings quality and forensic questions

1. **Credit-loss cycle:** Provision for credit losses rose to $14.212B in 2025 from $10.678B in 2024. Reconcile charge-offs, reserve builds, delinquency, nonaccrual loans, portfolio mix, macroeconomic scenarios, and loan growth. A reserve release can make earnings look better without improving credit quality.
2. **One-time securities gains:** 2024 total net revenue included a $7.9B net gain related to Visa shares and a $1.0B contribution of Visa shares to the JPMorgan Chase Foundation. Comparisons must remove those items before judging recurring revenue growth.
3. **Apple Card and acquired portfolios:** The 2025 allowance included approximately $2.2B related to the Apple Card transaction and Card Services growth. The acquisition can expand scale, but the return depends on credit performance, rewards, customer behavior, integration, and capital usage.
4. **Markets normalization:** Trading and investment-banking results respond to volatility, issuance, client inventories, and risk appetite. Value a through-cycle contribution, not the best recent quarter.
5. **Deposit funding:** Track deposit mix, rate paid, uninsured deposits, sweep behavior, liquidity buffers, wholesale funding, and deposit beta. Low-cost deposits are an asset only if they remain stable under competitive rates and stress.
6. **Fair-value and counterparty risk:** Securities, derivatives, leveraged lending, and market positions can generate earnings before losses are realized. Examine valuation adjustments, collateral, counterparty concentration, and stress scenarios.
7. **Technology and compliance:** The bank’s $11.0B technology expense and professional-services spending support the operating platform. Cutting them may raise near-term earnings while increasing future operational, cyber, regulatory, and fraud risk.
8. **Capital returns:** Dividends and buybacks must be evaluated after stress capital requirements, loan growth, market-risk capital, and possible rule changes. A high payout can be destructive if the bank needs capital shortly afterward.
9. **Management incentives:** Read the proxy for weights on ROTCE, EPS, revenue, expense efficiency, client growth, risk outcomes, capital, and conduct. Incentives that reward revenue and leverage without sufficient risk adjustment can encourage low-quality growth.

## Evidence grades and balance-sheet resilience

| Claim | Current evidence | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2025 revenue, common net income, deposits, assets, credit-loss provision, compensation, and technology expense | 2025 Form 10-K and annual-report packet | Filed fact |
| The deposit franchise supports lower-cost funding and broad client relationships | Deposit scale and business disclosures | Filed fact plus economic interpretation; deposit stability remains a stress test |
| 2025 earnings represent a durable through-cycle level | Three-year earnings and provision history | Unresolved question because markets, investment banking, rates, and credit conditions vary |
| Apple Card and other portfolio growth will earn acceptable risk-adjusted returns | Transaction and allowance disclosures | Requires portfolio vintage, loss, rewards, integration, and capital evidence |
| Capital returns are distributable after required capital growth | Earnings and return disclosures are available, but future stress and rule requirements remain uncertain | Unresolved question |

For a bank, balance-sheet resilience is part of the product. The stress sequence
is: deposit costs rise or deposits leave; funding becomes more expensive; loan
growth slows; credit losses and reserves increase; market or counterparty losses
consume capital; and regulators or management restrict distributions. A strong
quarterly net-income number does not remove those claims.

The current evidence supports scale and diversification, not immunity. JPMorgan
ended 2025 with about `$2.559T` of deposits and `$4.425T` of assets, while
provision for credit losses rose to `$14.212B`. The next filing should be read
for deposit pricing, uninsured and operational deposit behavior, loan and
securities duration, delinquency, charge-offs, reserve coverage, liquidity
buffers, CET1, and capital returns together.

Management incentives should be compared with the risk-adjusted denominator.
ROTCE and EPS can improve through leverage, reserve timing, securities gains,
or expense deferral even when the long-term risk has not improved. The proxy
should show how performance pay incorporates credit performance, conduct,
capital strength, technology and control investment, and diluted-share results.
If revenue growth is rewarded without those constraints, the apparent scale
advantage can encourage risk to be taken before its cost is visible.

## Lyn Alden lens

JPMorgan benefits from nominal economic growth, transaction volume, credit creation, capital-market activity, and a trusted deposit franchise. It is also a direct liquidity-cycle business. Higher rates can increase asset yields but raise deposit competition and funding cost. A recession can reduce lending and investment banking while increasing charge-offs. A market shock can increase trading revenue and counterparty risk at the same time.

Scale and diversification improve resilience, but they do not eliminate leverage. The relevant balance-sheet test is whether the bank can continue lending, settle payments, and meet withdrawals while absorbing a material credit and market shock without issuing equity at an unattractive price or cutting capital returns.

## Damodaran lens and valuation

The narrative is “the strongest diversified bank with a durable deposit franchise, payments network, markets platform, and technology advantage.” The valuation must specify normalized net interest margin, deposit cost, loan growth, credit losses, noninterest revenue through a cycle, efficiency ratio, compensation, technology, capital ratio, payout, and required return on equity.

At a market value of approximately $959.8B, the equity is about 17.2 times 2025 common net income and about 15.3 times reported net income. A simple common-earnings screen is:

| Normalized annual common earnings | Multiple | Implied equity value |
|---:|---:|---:|
| $45B bear case | 12x | $540B |
| $57B base case | 15x | $855B |
| $70B strong case | 18x | $1.26T |

These are scenario outputs, not price targets. The base case assumes the bank can retain strong deposit and payments economics while normalizing market revenue, credit provisions, technology expense, regulatory capital, and capital returns.

## Thesis breakers and next evidence

The thesis weakens if deposit costs rise faster than asset yields; if credit losses and reserves grow faster than loan economics; if markets and investment banking provide an inflated share of earnings; if Apple Card or other growth portfolios underperform; if technology and compliance costs keep rising without productivity; if regulatory capital requirements constrain distributions; or if conduct and control failures produce recurring legal and remediation costs.

The next filings should answer: Did net interest income hold after deposit repricing? Did charge-offs and reserve coverage match portfolio risk? Did markets revenue normalize? Did the Apple Card portfolio earn an acceptable risk-adjusted return? Did CET1 remain strong after capital returns and balance-sheet growth? Did technology spending improve efficiency and resilience? And do management incentives reward risk-adjusted common returns rather than volume or headline EPS?
