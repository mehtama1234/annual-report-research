# Lam Research: deposition and etch control with high service leverage and China exposure

## Bottom line

Lam Research sells semiconductor-manufacturing equipment used mainly in
deposition, etch, clean, packaging, and related process steps. Its economic
position comes from qualified tools, process knowledge, a large installed base,
and customer-support capability. As memory and logic structures become more
three-dimensional, customers may need more process steps and more tool
intensity even when unit volumes do not rise at the same rate.

The control-point thesis is credible. It is not cycle-proof. Fiscal 2026
revenue was $23.233B, operating cash flow was $5.858B, and cash used for
capital expenditures and related investing was about $922M, leaving a simple
cash-after-capex screen of about $4.935B. Customer-support revenue was $8.347B,
or 35.9% of sales. But China represented 34% of fiscal 2026 revenue, four
customers represented 16%, 15%, 12%, and 12%, and deferred revenue declined
from $2.68B to $2.43B.

At the September 14, 2026 market snapshot, equity value was about $350.5B.
That is approximately 71 times the fiscal 2026 cash-after-capex screen. The
price requires sustained process complexity, service growth, high margins,
customer spending, and export-control resilience.

## How to read the company

`device architecture -> deposition/etch requirement -> qualified Lam tool -> wafer yield and throughput -> installed-base service -> cash`

Lam reports two economically different revenue layers:

- **Systems:** new leading-edge equipment in deposition, etch, clean, and
  other wafer-fabrication markets.
- **Customer support-related revenue and other:** customer service, spare
  parts, upgrades, and non-leading-edge Reliant equipment.

Fiscal 2026 systems revenue was $14.885B, up 29.5%. Customer-support revenue
was $8.347B, up 20.2%. This is a meaningful recurring and upgrade layer, but it
still depends on the size, age, utilization, and technology relevance of the
installed base.

37.4% operating margin. Systems revenue was $4.250B and support-related
revenue was $2.472B. China was 26% of that quarter's revenue, compared with
34% for the full fiscal year.

## Earnings quality and forensic review

### 1. Process complexity supports demand, but customer fab budgets still set the cycle

Lam's deposition, etch, and clean tools are used in NAND, DRAM, foundry, logic,
and advanced packaging. Fiscal 2026 revenue mix was 54% foundry, 39% memory,
and 7% logic/integrated-device manufacturing under the company's disclosed
market categories.

The technical thesis is that vertical memory, gate-all-around logic, high-
bandwidth memory, and advanced packaging require more process steps and more
precise control. The cycle risk is that customers decide when to build and
qualify capacity. A stronger process requirement does not prevent a fab from
delaying the project.

### 2. Customer support is large but not independent of tool demand

Support-related revenue reached $8.347B in fiscal 2026, or nearly 36% of total
revenue. It includes services, spares, upgrades, and non-leading-edge equipment.
The installed base is a genuine economic asset because field support and spare
parts can be difficult to replace after a tool is qualified.

The test is whether support revenue per chamber, renewal, upgrade activity,
and service margin remain stable through a tool downturn. Support revenue can
decline if customers reduce utilization, retire older systems, delay upgrades,
or shift process generations. It should not be treated as software-like
recurrence without installed-base and utilization evidence.

### 3. Deferred revenue and Japan acceptance create timing questions

Deferred revenue declined from $2.68B at June 29, 2025 to $2.43B at June 28,
2026. The company recognized $2.09B of opening deferred revenue during fiscal
2026. It also states that shipments to customers in Japan remain in inventory
until customer acceptance rather than entering deferred revenue; the estimated
future revenue from those shipments was $490.2M at year-end.

These policies can be legitimate and consistent with the contracts. They make
the cash and revenue bridge important. Compare shipments, acceptance, deferred
revenue, Japanese inventory, receivables, and customer deposits. Revenue growth
that depends on shipping before acceptance or on a rising pool of unrecognized
equipment deserves a lower cash-conversion grade.

### 4. Working capital explains why profit did not become more cash

Fiscal 2026 operating cash flow included a $93.9M inventory use, a $417.5M
accounts-payable source, and a $286.4M reduction from deferred profit. Fiscal
2025 had a $1.148B deferred-profit source. That comparison shows why fiscal
2025 cash flow was unusually strong relative to fiscal 2026.

The owner test is not just OCF less capex. It is whether the cash result is
repeatable without depending on customer down payments, supplier timing, or a
temporary release of deferred profit.

### 5. Customer concentration and China are linked risks

Four customers represented 16%, 15%, 12%, and 12% of fiscal 2026 revenue. No
other customer exceeded 10%. China represented 34% of fiscal 2026 revenue,
after 34% in 2025 and 42% in 2024.

The company says export controls can limit products and services, create
licensing uncertainty, cause customers to delay or cancel orders, and increase
competition from domestic Chinese suppliers. If a major customer is also in a
restricted geography or device segment, customer concentration and policy risk
can reinforce each other.

### 6. R&D, capacity, and tariffs are required costs

Fiscal 2026 R&D was $2.376B, up from $2.096B in 2025. Capital expenditures and
investing cash use rose to $922.2M, primarily because of US lab investments and
global manufacturing-facility growth. The company also cited aluminum and
steel tariff-related spending as a partial offset to gross-margin improvement.

The economic cost of preserving the position includes R&D, process engineering,
field support, labs, manufacturing capacity, inventory, supplier commitments,
and export-control compliance. A valuation based only on current free cash
flow can overstate owner cash if the current capacity investment is necessary
to support the claimed served-market expansion.

## Alden lens: process intensity inside a semiconductor liquidity cycle

Lam benefits from long-term growth in computing, memory, AI accelerators,
advanced packaging, and semiconductor complexity. But the company remains
downstream from fab financing, customer capital expenditure, memory pricing,
inventory, and geopolitical policy.

The stress case is a simultaneous memory correction, delayed foundry projects,
China restrictions, weaker customer utilization, higher tariffs, and supplier
cost pressure. Lam can keep its technical position while revenue, margins,
cash, and buybacks decline during that stress.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers and valuation

The valuation chain is:

`process complexity -> tool content and placements -> installed-base support and upgrades -> gross profit -> R&D/capacity/inventory -> owner cash`

Fiscal 2026 cash after the company's capital/investing cash use was about
$4.935B. At the September 14, 2026 market snapshot of $350.5B, the equity
traded at roughly 71 times that cash screen.

Illustrative equity-value cases:

| Normalized owner cash | Multiple | Implied equity value |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $3.8B bear | 45x | $171B |
| $5.0B base | 60x | $300B |
| $6.2B bull | 70x | $434B |

The base case is below the current market value, while the bull case requires
cash above fiscal 2026 levels and assumes the market continues to award a high
multiple to a cyclical equipment supplier. The reverse valuation must test
whether process complexity, support revenue, and market-share gains can offset
semiconductor-cycle, China, customer, and reinvestment risk.

## Possible financial-engineering questions

These are tests, not accusations:

- Does deferred profit or customer down-payment timing make current cash look
  stronger than normalized cash?
- Are Japanese shipments held in inventory until acceptance, and how quickly do
  they convert after acceptance?
- Does support revenue remain durable when customers reduce utilization or
  retire older chambers?
- Are inventory, receivables, and supplier terms moving faster than revenue?
- Do buybacks offset stock-based compensation on a per-share basis?
- Are China restrictions and tariffs recurring structural costs being treated as
  temporary items?
- Does capital spending remain necessary to support the served-market narrative,
  reducing the cash available for distributions?

## What would prove this wrong

Downgrade the thesis if future filings show that:

1. Support revenue and margins fall with tool utilization rather than providing
   meaningful installed-base stability.
2. Deposition and etch share gains fail to offset a normal WFE downturn.
3. Deferred revenue, Japanese inventory, receivables, or customer advances rise
   while collections and gross profit weaken.
4. China restrictions reduce product and service revenue faster than other
   geographies replace it.
5. Customer concentration produces pricing concessions or abrupt order delays.
6. R&D, facilities, labs, and inventory consume most incremental gross profit.
7. A competing deposition, etch, packaging, or process architecture reduces
   Lam's qualification advantage.
8. Buybacks and dividends are maintained through peak-cycle cash while debt or
   dilution rises.
