# Leidos Holdings, Inc. — diversified government execution, backlog conversion, and debt-funded capability expansion

Date baseline: 2026-09-14  
Primary evidence: [Leidos company packet](../../extracted/technology/information-technology-services/leidos-holdings-inc/company-packet.md), [Leidos FY2025 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1336920/000133692026000030/ldos-20260102.htm), [Leidos Q1 FY2026 Form 10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1336920/000133692026000169/ldos-20260403.htm), and [Leidos source ledger](../../extracted/technology/information-technology-services/leidos-holdings-inc/source-ledger.md)

## Investment question

Leidos provides digital modernization, cyber, mission software, defense
systems, managed health, homeland, transportation, and other government
services. About `87%` of FY2025 revenue came from U.S. government contracts.
The company therefore has stronger institutional embedment than an ordinary IT
services provider, but its economics remain tied to appropriations, contract
estimates, specialized labor, collection timing, debt, and acquisitions.

The investment question is whether Leidos can convert a roughly `$49B` backlog
and a diversified mission portfolio into durable owner cash after execution
costs, working capital, capital spending, the Entrust acquisition, interest,
stock compensation, and dilution.

## What Leidos controls

The operating sequence is:

`government requirement -> funded contract or vehicle -> cleared and specialized labor plus software and systems -> delivery and acceptance -> billing -> collection -> renewal, recompete, or follow-on award -> cash`

Leidos controls technical labor, program knowledge, integration capability,
trusted delivery, contract-vehicle access, and relationships across defense,
intelligence, health, homeland, civil, and commercial customers. Government
agencies control appropriations, requirements, contract awards, modifications,
and payment timing. Leidos also carries the risk of estimating labor and other
costs on fixed-price and incentive contracts.

## Definitions

- **Backlog** is remaining contractual work and certain options. It is future
  revenue visibility, not profit or cash.
- **Funded backlog** is the portion currently authorized for funding. It is a
  better near-term signal than total backlog, but it still requires staffing,
  delivery, billing, and collection.
- **Book-to-bill** compares awards with revenue. It can show demand, but it
  does not show whether new work earns an adequate return.
- **Cost-to-cost recognition** records much contract revenue over time using
  costs incurred relative to estimated total costs. A change in expected cost
  can change current revenue and profit.
- **Recurring-capital residual** here means operating cash flow less payments
  for property, equipment, and software. Acquisitions, debt, interest, stock
  compensation, buybacks, and dividends are then examined separately.

## Operating evidence

| Period | Revenue / demand evidence | Cash and balance-sheet signal |
|---|---:|---|
| FY2023 | Government-heavy operating base | Operating cash `$1.187B`; capital payments `$207M`; recurring-capital residual about `$980M` |
| FY2024 | Continued government and mission-services mix | Operating cash `$1.435B`; capital payments `$149M`; residual about `$1.286B` |
| FY2025 | `87%` of revenue from U.S. government contracts; about `47,000` employees | Operating cash `$1.750B`; capital payments `$125M`; residual about `$1.625B`; acquisitions `$293M` |
| Q1 FY2026 | Backlog about `$48.4B`; Entrust added about `$371M` of Homeland backlog | Cash `$457M`; debt about `$6.3B` after Entrust-related financing |
| Q2 FY2026 | Backlog about `$48.7B`; funded backlog `$9.7B` at FY2025 year-end was the latest annual anchor | Cash `$748M`; debt about `$6.0B` |

The backlog is substantial relative to annual revenue, but it is not an
economic asset that can be valued without execution assumptions. The company
must supply specialized labor, absorb contract-cost risk, finance receivables,
and collect from government programs. The 2026 quarter chain also shows the
trade-off directly: capability expansion through Entrust came with a higher
debt burden.

## Three-year cash bridge

| Fiscal year | Operating cash flow | Property, equipment, and software payments | Recurring-capital residual | Acquisition cash |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| FY2023 | `$1.187B` | `$207M` | `$980M` | `$6M` |
| FY2024 | `$1.435B` | `$149M` | `$1.286B` | none |
| FY2025 | `$1.750B` | `$125M` | `$1.625B` | `$293M` |

Leidos generated a much stronger recurring-capital residual than CACI in
FY2025, and the disclosed acquisition cash was smaller. That is the favorable
part of the comparison. The qualification is important: FY2025 operating cash
benefited from tax effects associated with the One Big Beautiful Bill Act and
favorable working-capital changes. Net income was `$1.462B`; deferred taxes
provided `$369M`; accounts payable and accrued liabilities used `$308M`; and
income taxes used `$146M`. The full `$1.750B` should therefore not be treated
as a clean steady-state owner-cash run rate.

The 2026 Entrust transaction changes the forward test. The company used new
senior notes of approximately `$600M` at `4.10%` and `$800M` at `5.00%` for the
transaction. The acquired capability may improve the Homeland and trusted-
identity position, but the return must exceed interest, integration cost,
amortization, and the opportunity cost of leverage.

## Earnings quality and financial-engineering risks

### Backlog is visibility, not margin

Total backlog remained near `$49B` at FY2025 year-end and `$48.7B` in Q2
FY2026. Funded backlog was about `$9.7B` at FY2025 year-end. The next filing
should show whether funded work converts into revenue, margin, billings, and
cash across National Security & Digital, Health & Civil, Commercial &
International, and Defense Systems. Review options, recompetes, fixed-price
exposure, cost-to-complete changes, unbilled receivables, and contract assets.

### Government concentration creates a specific form of cyclicality

Government demand can be durable while annual execution is uneven. Continuing
resolutions, shutdowns, delayed appropriations, procurement protests, and
program reprioritization can delay awards or payment without immediately
destroying backlog. The relevant test is not simply whether federal demand is
large. It is whether funded programs remain diversified across agencies and
missions and whether Leidos can keep labor productive during timing gaps.

### Tax and working-capital benefits need normalization

The FY2025 cash increase was helped by deferred-tax effects and working-capital
movements. A tax benefit can be real but nonrecurring. Receivables, payables,
accrued payroll, and contract assets can also move cash between periods. Compare
cash with normalized taxes, receivable days, unbilled work, contract liabilities,
and the timing of payroll and benefits before assigning a durable multiple.

### Acquisition accounting can hide the cost of capability expansion

Leidos had approximately `$6.342B` of goodwill and `$458M` of intangible
assets at FY2025 year-end. FY2025 amortization of intangibles was `$130M`.
These costs reflect prior acquisitions and are economic evidence of the price
paid for capabilities, even when adjusted earnings exclude them. The Entrust
transaction adds a new test: disclose acquired revenue, retention, contract
awards, margins, integration expense, amortization, and incremental cash
returns separately enough to evaluate the purchase.

### Stock compensation and capital returns affect common-owner cash

Stock compensation was `$95M` in FY2025, up from `$85M` in FY2024 and `$77M`
in FY2023. Leidos also repurchased about `$900M` of stock in FY2025 through
open-market purchases and an accelerated share repurchase, and paid about
`$211M` of dividends. Repurchases can be sensible when shares are undervalued,
but they should be compared with debt, acquisition funding, and cash per
diluted share. A higher per-share result is not strong evidence if it depends
on debt-funded repurchases while operating cash is temporarily elevated.

### A cash-accounting policy change should be traced

The FY2025 filing changed the accounting policy presentation for cash and
recast prior periods. That is not by itself a red flag, but it creates a
reconciliation requirement: confirm that reported cash, restricted cash,
working-capital movements, and the three-year cash bridge use comparable
definitions.

## Balance-sheet resilience and capital requirements

At January 2, 2026, Leidos reported about `$1.108B` of cash, `$2.708B` of
receivables, `$6.342B` of goodwill, and `$4.648B` of long-term debt after
unamortized issuance costs. Cash interest paid was about `$220M` in FY2025.
The Q1 FY2026 packet then showed debt around `$6.3B` after Entrust financing.

The company also had about `$526M` of right-of-use assets. Leases, secure
facilities, specialized equipment, and program infrastructure support delivery
even when they do not appear as traditional capital expenditures. The debt
covenant framework allows a higher leverage ratio temporarily after a material
acquisition, which makes acquisition timing and debt reduction part of the
investment case rather than a footnote.

## Lyn Alden lens

Leidos is exposed to public-sector spending, not household credit creation or
ordinary consumer liquidity. Persistent geopolitical risk, cyber dependence,
health-system complexity, and government digital modernization can support
long-run demand. But public spending is mediated by appropriations, fiscal
constraints, procurement rules, and political priorities.

The balance-sheet question is therefore central. A backlog can look stable
while debt, interest, labor cost, or collection timing reduces the cash
available to common owners. Higher rates also reduce the value of acquisition-
funded growth because more of each program dollar is claimed before equity.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers

The narrative becomes:

`government modernization and mission complexity × funded program demand × Leidos award and execution share × qualified labor and systems capacity -> revenue -> labor, subcontractors, contract costs, overhead, taxes, interest, and reinvestment -> owner cash`

The valuation should separately model:

1. organic revenue and margin by segment;
2. funded backlog conversion and award quality;
3. fixed-price and cost-to-cost estimate risk;
4. receivables and contract-asset conversion;
5. Entrust revenue, integration, and incremental return;
6. debt, interest, and refinancing capacity; and
7. stock compensation, repurchases, dividends, and diluted cash per share.

At the September 14, 2026 market snapshot, Leidos equity value was about
`$16.2B`. The valuation register uses FY2025 recurring-capital residual of
`$1.625B` as the starting measure, but uses lower normalized bear and base
cases to reflect tax timing, working capital, Entrust, debt, and dilution. The
base case is not a financial signoff; it is an expectations audit.

## Management incentives and thesis breakers

Management is incentivized to emphasize revenue, backlog, funded backlog,
adjusted EBITDA, adjusted EPS, free cash flow, and capability expansion. Pair
each with organic growth, segment margin, estimate changes, receivables, debt,
acquisition return, SBC, and diluted share count.

The thesis breaks if:

- backlog remains large but funded conversion, staffing, or collections weaken;
- fixed-price or incentive-contract estimates reduce margins or create losses;
- FY2025 tax and working-capital benefits do not repeat and normalized cash
  falls materially;
- Entrust adds revenue but not incremental cash returns above interest and
  integration cost;
- debt and interest rise faster than recurring-capital residual;
- specialized labor shortages force uneconomic subcontracting or delay work;
- government concentration causes program timing or policy changes to dominate
  growth; or
- adjusted EPS rises while cash per diluted share falls after SBC, debt, and
  acquisitions.

## Next-filing evidence ladder

| Question | Confirming evidence | Falsifying evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Is backlog high quality? | Funded backlog converts into revenue, margin, billings, and cash without recurring estimate losses | Backlog stays high through options while funded work, margin, or collection weakens |
| Is diversification real? | Health, homeland, defense, and digital work each contribute funded demand and acceptable margins | One agency, program, or acquisition explains most of the growth |
| Was Entrust worth the price? | Acquired capability adds awards, retention, margin, and cash after interest and integration | Debt and amortization rise without incremental owner cash |
| Is cash repeatable? | OCF remains strong after tax, receivable, payable, payroll, and contract-asset normalization | Temporary tax or working-capital items explain most of the increase |
| Is leverage manageable? | Debt declines while the company funds required technical investment and dividends | New debt or repurchases are needed before prior acquisitions prove returns |
| Is per-share economics improving? | Cash per diluted share rises after SBC, buybacks, dividends, and acquisition spending | Adjusted EPS rises while dilution, debt, or acquisition cash use rises faster |

## Bottom line

Leidos is a broad government-execution platform rather than a narrow defense-
technology contractor. Its backlog, segment diversity, and FY2025 recurring-
capital residual make it a stronger cash-conversion counterpart to CACI on the
reported numbers.

The harder conclusion is that reported cash is not yet a clean owner-cash
answer. FY2025 benefited from tax and working-capital effects, the company has
large goodwill and debt claims, stock compensation is material, and Entrust
increased forward leverage. The durable thesis requires funded backlog to
convert through qualified labor and accurate contract estimates, Entrust to
earn its financing cost, debt to decline, and cash per diluted share to rise
without relying on temporary cash benefits.
