# McKesson Corporation Full Forensic Analysis Memo

Date baseline: 2026-09-13  
Primary source: [McKesson FY2025 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/927653/000092765325000036/mck-20250331.htm)  
Companion packet: [company-packet.md](/home/mehtama1/git-repo/annual-report-research/extracted/healthcare/drug-stores/mckesson-corporation/company-packet.md)

## Bottom line

McKesson is a healthcare middle-layer infrastructure company: it routes drugs,
specialty therapies, oncology services, medical-surgical products, and
prescription technology between manufacturers, pharmacies, providers, and
patients. Its control point is dependable channel access, regulated logistics,
specialty handling, data, and workflow—not high gross margin on the physical
product.

FY2025 revenue reached $359.1B, but the headline is economically misleading:
pharmaceutical distribution is enormous and low-margin, while specialty,
oncology, technology, and services carry more of the strategic value. The
company generated $6.085B of operating cash flow, spent $537M on PP&E and
$322M on capitalized software, and paid $24M for acquisitions, leaving about
$5.202B before financing and shareholder returns. That cash conversion is
strong, but it is exposed to working-capital swings, customer credit, drug
mix, reimbursement policy, litigation, and acquisition-led portfolio change.

The central forensic question is whether McKesson is a durable, cash-generative
coordination layer or a thin-margin distributor whose adjusted earnings and
growth narrative depend on scale, working-capital timing, buybacks, and
specialty acquisitions.

## What McKesson controls

The economic sequence is:

`manufacturer access -> inventory and regulated logistics -> pharmacy/provider workflow -> specialty and oncology services -> cash conversion`

McKesson's U.S. Pharmaceutical segment generated $327.7B of FY2025 revenue;
Prescription Technology Solutions generated $5.2B, Medical-Surgical Solutions
$11.4B, and International $14.7B. The distribution relationship can be
sticky because pharmacies and providers cannot easily tolerate supply failure,
but the distributor does not control manufacturer pricing, payer policy,
customer solvency, or regulatory settlement risk.

## Three-year reported financial picture

Amounts are $ billions except where noted.

| Fiscal year ended March 31 | Revenue | Net income attributable to McKesson | Operating cash flow | PP&E capex | Capitalized software | Acquisitions |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2023 | 276.7 | 3.560 | 5.159 | 0.390 | 0.168 | 0.867 |
| 2024 | 309.0 | 3.002 | 4.314 | 0.431 | 0.256 | 0.272 |
| 2025 | 359.1 | 3.295 | 6.085 | 0.537 | 0.322 | 0.024 |

The cash bridge is approximately:

`$6.085B OCF - $0.537B PP&E - $0.322B software - $0.024B acquisitions = $5.202B`

This is a useful screen, not normalized owner earnings. It needs working-capital
normalization, debt repayments, litigation and opioid-settlement payments,
SBC, buybacks, and the capital required to grow specialty and technology
platforms.

## Earnings quality and forensic tests

### 1. Revenue scale is not economic power by itself

McKesson's revenue is dominated by pass-through pharmaceutical product. A
16% revenue increase can reflect drug price inflation, specialty mix, channel
volume, or acquisitions without creating comparable profit growth. The proper
comparison is gross profit, operating profit, cash conversion, and return on
incremental working capital by segment.

Track gross margin, inventory turns, receivable days, supplier terms, customer
concentration, and the contribution of specialty and technology. Growth that
requires proportionally more inventory and receivables is less durable than
growth funded by service and workflow economics.

### 2. Working capital is a major source of apparent cash quality

A distributor can generate large cash inflows by collecting receivables,
stretching payables, or reducing inventory after a demand or price cycle. The
FY2025 OCF increase of $1.8B versus FY2024 must therefore be decomposed into
profit, receivables, inventory, payables, rebates, and settlement timing.

The next filing should reverse one-time working-capital benefits and show
normalized cash generation through a drug-price, shortage, and reimbursement
cycle.

### 3. Specialty growth carries more value and more concentration risk

Oncology, multispecialty, biopharma services, and prescription technology can
raise the quality of the revenue mix, but they also bring customer
concentration, reimbursement, patient-access, inventory obsolescence, and
regulatory risk. Specialty economics should be tested for gross-to-net
adjustments, rebates, returns, chargebacks, bad debt, and acquisition
integration costs.

The strategic narrative is attractive only if specialty and service margins
remain strong after required technology, compliance, distribution, and
working-capital investment.

### 4. Portfolio actions can improve optics without proving returns

McKesson's annual materials describe portfolio pruning, operational efficiency,
and the Apollo transaction around Medical-Surgical Solutions. Divestitures can
sharpen the business, but the analysis must separate sale proceeds, stranded
costs, dis-synergies, tax effects, and the earnings removed from the continuing
portfolio. Acquisitions should be evaluated on post-integration cash return,
not adjusted EPS accretion.

### 5. Litigation and regulatory liabilities are operating capital claims

Healthcare distribution is exposed to opioid settlements, government
investigations, controlled-substance compliance, pricing disputes, product
recalls, and customer credit. These are not merely legal footnotes: they can
consume cash, increase compliance staffing, restrict operations, and alter the
value of customer relationships. Reconcile provisions, cash payments, reserves,
insurance recoveries, and remaining obligations.

## Alden lens: essential demand, thin spreads, large liquidity machine

Drug distribution demand is structurally resilient, but the business operates
on thin spreads and large balance-sheet flows. A liquidity shock can arise not
from demand collapse but from inventory funding, customer defaults, supplier
terms, interest expense, reimbursement changes, or regulatory cash claims.

The durability chain is:

`essential therapy demand -> channel continuity -> inventory/receivable funding -> gross-profit spread -> cash conversion -> compliance and capital resilience`

The key macro question is whether McKesson can maintain service quality and
credit discipline when rates, drug mix, working-capital needs, and policy
uncertainty rise simultaneously.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to valuation

The valuation must split McKesson into economic engines:

`low-margin distribution volume -> specialty/oncology gross profit -> technology and service recurring revenue -> working-capital requirement -> compliance/capital burden -> owner cash`

At the September 11, 2026 market snapshot, MCK equity value was approximately
$105.1B. That is roughly 17.3x FY2025 operating cash flow and 20.2x the listed
residual after PP&E, software, and acquisitions. These are screening ratios,
not price targets. They imply that the market expects specialty growth,
durable cash conversion, and disciplined capital returns to offset low
distribution margins and legal/regulatory burdens.

The reverse valuation should vary pharmaceutical gross margin, specialty mix,
receivable and inventory intensity, bad debt, settlement cash, software capex,
acquisition returns, debt, share count, and segment separation outcomes.

## Incentives and thesis breakers

Management is naturally rewarded for revenue, adjusted EPS, operating cash flow,
free cash flow, specialty growth, acquisitions, buybacks, and portfolio
optimization. Revenue and EPS can improve through drug price/mix, working-
capital timing, leverage, and add-backs without equivalent improvement in
durable per-share economics.

The thesis is materially weakened if future filings show:

- revenue and adjusted EPS growth without gross-profit or normalized cash
  growth;
- receivables, inventory, rebates, or payables supplying an increasing share
  of operating cash;
- specialty growth requiring more capital, reserves, bad-debt provisions, or
  customer concessions than disclosed;
- litigation, opioid, controlled-substance, or compliance cash claims recur at
  levels treated as “nonrecurring”; or
- buybacks and acquisitions outpace normalized cash after working-capital and
  technology requirements.

## Evidence grade and unresolved questions

- **Filing fact:** FY2025 segment revenue, gross profit, operating cash flow,
  working capital, capex, acquisitions, litigation, debt, and capital returns
  are anchored to the Form 10-K.
- **Disclosed/packet-backed:** specialty growth, technology, and portfolio
  strategy claims are management-reported and require post-growth cash proof.
- **Inference:** McKesson controls a high-volume healthcare channel and
  specialty workflow, but its thin-spread economics are exposed to receivables,
  inventory, rebates, controlled-substance liabilities, and customer liquidity.
- **Unresolved:** the next filing must show whether specialty growth produces
  incremental cash after working capital, bad debt, settlements, technology,
  acquisitions, and buybacks. Rising scale without normalized gross profit and
  cash would weaken the thesis.

## Primary-filing verification and distribution-cash bridge

The preserved FY2025 SEC 10-K XBRL facts reproduce revenue of **$359.051B**,
operating cash flow of **$6.085B**, PP&E purchases of **$537M**, capitalized
software included in productive-asset spending of roughly **$322M**, net
acquisition payments of **$24M**, and repurchases of **$3.146B**. Year-end cash
was **$5.691B** and long-term debt was **$5.654B**.

After PP&E, capitalized software, and acquisitions, the residual was
approximately **$5.202B** before buybacks, litigation, working-capital
normalization, and economic SBC. The filing therefore supports McKesson's
cleaner current screen relative to Cencora and Cardinal, while confirming that
the key risk is not demand disappearance but thin-spread cash timing across
receivables, inventory, payables, rebates, and settlements.
