# MetLife, Inc. — insurance risk transfer with asset-liability and capital constraints

Research date: 2026-09-14  
Evidence window: FY2025 and FY2026 Q2  
Ticker: MET  
Evidence grade: A for reported financials, investment balances, adjusted earnings, and capital returns; B for reserve adequacy, variable investment income durability, private-asset marks, longevity and morbidity assumptions, and future capital release.

## Bottom line

MetLife transfers mortality, morbidity, longevity, retirement, and employee-benefit risks from customers and institutions onto a regulated insurance balance sheet. It earns through premiums, fees, investment income, underwriting, asset management, and the spread between assets and policyholder obligations.

The control point is the liability relationship and the trust that allows MetLife to invest funds over long periods. The burden is the balance sheet: reserves, capital requirements, duration matching, credit risk, liquidity, reinsurance, market marks, and assumptions about future claims. A life insurer is not an asset manager with free assets. Much of its investment portfolio supports policyholder promises.

FY2025 net income was $3.173B, adjusted earnings excluding notable items were $5.986B, adjusted EPS was $8.89, adjusted ROE was 16%, and shareholder returns were nearly $4.4B. Q2 FY2026 net income was $705M, adjusted earnings were $1.6B, adjusted EPS was $2.43, net investment income was $6.7B, and holding-company cash and liquid assets were $3.4B. The investment case depends on how much of adjusted earnings can be converted into capital that remains available after reserve growth, risk-based capital, reinsurance, and market stress.

Related first-principles explanation: [Insurance liabilities and distributable capital](../first-principles/insurance-liabilities-and-distributable-capital.md).

## What MetLife controls

The operating chain is:

`customer protection or retirement need -> insurance contract -> premium and fee collection -> invested assets -> claims and benefits -> capital release or reinvestment`

MetLife controls product design, underwriting, distribution, claims administration, asset allocation, reinsurance, retirement-risk transfer, and capital deployment. Its scale and diversification across Group Benefits, Retirement and Income Solutions, Asia, Latin America, EMEA, and MetLife Investment Management reduce dependence on one product or geography.

MetLife does not control mortality, morbidity, longevity, lapse behavior, interest rates, credit losses, equity and private-asset marks, currency, regulation, or the timing of policyholder cash flows. A profitable policy portfolio can still create liquidity pressure if assets fall in value or liabilities reprice differently.

The gap between net income and adjusted earnings is not automatically a shenanigan. It reflects market risk benefit remeasurement, investment and derivative gains or losses, changes in assumptions, and other notable items. But the investor must determine whether the excluded items are genuinely outside the underwriting and investment model or are normal consequences of carrying long-duration liabilities.

## Underwriting and investment spread

MetLife’s recurring economics come from the relationship between policy premiums and benefits, plus the investment income earned on assets supporting obligations. Net investment income rose to $22.559B in 2025 and $6.7B in Q2 2026. Higher rates can improve reinvestment income, but they can also reduce the fair value of existing bonds, alter hedging costs, affect policyholder behavior, and pressure capital.

Variable investment income is less durable than contractual spread income. MetLife disclosed that 2025 variable investment income was below its business-plan goal because of returns in real estate and other funds and private equity. A quarter with high private-asset returns should not be capitalized as ordinary earnings without testing realizations, valuation marks, cash distributions, and future commitments.

## Balance sheet and earnings quality

At June 30, 2026, MetLife held approximately $322.183B of fixed-maturity securities at fair value, against amortized cost of $347.781B, plus other investments and contractholder-directed assets. The difference between fair value and amortized cost is economically relevant because unrealized losses can reduce capital flexibility even when accounting treatment separates them from earnings.

The liability side requires equal attention. Policyholder benefits, future policy benefits, market risk benefits, separate-account obligations, and reinsurance recoverables determine how much of the asset portfolio belongs to common shareholders. Reserve releases or assumption changes can lift earnings while reducing future protection. The proper test is whether reserve assumptions remain adequate under lower rates, higher longevity, worse mortality or morbidity, higher lapses, and weaker asset returns.

Reinsurance transfers risk but also creates counterparty and collateral dependence. Pension risk transfer and longevity reinsurance can generate large volumes and fees, yet they add long-duration commitments. The investor should reconcile premium volume to reserve growth, capital consumption, reinsurance recoverables, risk-based capital, and future benefit payments.

## Capital returns and incentives

MetLife returned nearly $4.4B in 2025 and more than $1.1B in Q2 2026 through repurchases and dividends. Capital return is valuable only after preserving required capital and supporting the growth of insurance liabilities. A high adjusted ROE can result from releasing capital, benefiting from market returns, or growing liabilities; it does not by itself prove that the next dollar of retained capital earns a high return.

Management uses adjusted earnings, adjusted ROE, book value, adjusted book value, and a core free-cash-flow ratio. These measures are useful operating indicators but require a bridge to statutory capital, reserve adequacy, holding-company liquidity, and common-shareholder value. The investor should ask whether buybacks occur below adjusted book value, whether share count falls, and whether capital returns reduce resilience under stress.

## Lyn Alden lens: liquidity, duration, and financial-cycle resilience

MetLife can benefit from long-duration liabilities and higher reinvestment yields. It is vulnerable when rates, spreads, credit, policyholder behavior, and asset liquidity move together. The company must be able to pay claims and benefits without selling assets at distressed prices.

The liquidity test is holding-company cash, subsidiary dividends, statutory capital, collateral requirements, reinsurance recoverables, asset duration, and the ability to issue debt or preferred capital. The stress test is not a normal recession alone; it is a period of falling asset values, rising lapses, higher collateral needs, and reduced capital-market access.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers

The narrative is: MetLife is a diversified, well-capitalized retirement and protection platform that can grow adjusted earnings, release capital, and compound book value through disciplined risk transfer.

That narrative must become measurable assumptions:

- recurring premiums and fees excluding large pension-risk-transfer transactions;
- underwriting margins, mortality, morbidity, longevity, lapse, and expense ratios;
- contractual spread income versus variable investment income;
- private-equity, real-estate, and other alternative-asset valuation and cash realization;
- reserve adequacy, market-risk-benefit remeasurement, and assumption changes;
- reinsurance recoverables, counterparty quality, collateral, and capital relief;
- statutory capital, risk-based capital, holding-company liquidity, and dividends from subsidiaries;
- debt, preferred capital, repurchases, dividends, and diluted shares;
- terminal adjusted earnings after rates and variable investment income normalize.

## Cash conversion and distributable capital

For MetLife, owner cash is not simply net income less capital spending. The starting point is cash that regulated subsidiaries can dividend to the holding company after reserve growth, statutory capital needs, collateral, reinsurance support, and stress requirements. Holding-company cash can then fund debt service, dividends, repurchases, acquisitions, and corporate costs. A large shareholder return is evidence of current distribution, not proof that the same amount can be distributed after new insurance liabilities are written.

The cash test is therefore a bridge: adjusted earnings -> statutory earnings and capital -> subsidiary dividends -> holding-company cash -> dividends and repurchases. The investor should reconcile that bridge each period and compare it with common shares outstanding, adjusted book value, reserve growth, and risk-based capital. This prevents an insurer's investment income or temporary capital release from being treated as recurring owner cash.

MetLife’s market value was approximately $61.730B on September 11, 2026. A simple adjusted-earnings screen is:

| $5.0B bear case | 8x | $40B |

These are scenario outputs, not price targets. The base case requires recurring underwriting and spread earnings to support capital release after reserve growth and market stress. It does not assume that all variable investment income or pension-risk-transfer volume is permanent.

## Thesis breakers and next evidence

The thesis weakens if reserve strengthening becomes recurring; if variable investment income is required to meet earnings targets; if private-asset marks rise without cash realization; if credit losses or duration mismatches reduce capital; if reinsurance recoverables become difficult to collect; if statutory capital limits dividends and buybacks; if adjusted earnings grow while book value and cash capital weaken; or if shareholder returns depend on releasing capital needed for new liability growth.

The next filings should answer: What portion of adjusted earnings came from underwriting, contractual spread, and variable investment income? Did fixed-maturity unrealized losses change? Did reserves, reinsurance recoverables, and risk-based capital remain strong? Did holding-company liquidity remain within target after the new buyback authorization? Did adjusted book value and common shares outstanding improve after capital returns?
