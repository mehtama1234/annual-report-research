# NRG Energy, Inc. — customer-backed power, retail scale, and the post-LS Power denominator

As of 2026-09-13. FY2025 figures are filed GAAP figures unless explicitly identified otherwise; dollar amounts are in millions.

## How to read this memo

In this memo, a control point means the specific customer relationship, asset,
or process that gives a company the ability to earn money. GAAP means the accounting rules used in the filed financial statements.
Operating cash flow is cash produced by day-to-day operations. PP&E means
property, plant, and equipment—the physical assets used to generate and deliver
power. Bring Your Own Power (BYOP) is NRG’s proposed structure in which a large
customer helps support the generation built for its additional demand. The
residual below is a screening measure; it does not include the post-period LS
Power acquisition.

## Investment question

NRG is the clearest customer-backed power case in the utility cohort. It
combines retail electricity and natural-gas customers, generation, demand
response, smart-home relationships, and proposed large-load projects. The
question is whether customer alignment produces cleaner economics than either
regulated rate-base expansion or merchant scarcity after LS Power integration,
capex, working capital, debt, and retail volatility.

## What the filing and IR record reveal

- NRG’s control point is the customer-and-supply interface: it can combine
  retail load, generation, demand response, smart-home services, and new
  customer-specific power projects.
- Bring Your Own Power is economically meaningful because it attempts to make
  the incremental large-load customer fund or support the generation required
  by its demand. That can protect existing customers and reduce stranded-capital
  risk, but only if the contract is binding and creditworthy.
- The LS Power acquisition added roughly 13 GW of generation assets and CPower.
  It also changed the lifecycle, financing, depreciation, integration, and
  maintenance profile. “Bigger platform” is not itself a return calculation.
- The company serves roughly 8 million electricity, natural-gas, and smart-home
  customers and has approximately 25 GW of generation according to company
  materials. The customer book can stabilize demand exposure, but retail
  margins remain sensitive to weather, procurement, churn, regulation, and
  affordability.
- The proposed 1.2 GW Texas hyperscaler project illustrates the model’s test:
  customer alignment must reach signed terms, financing, interconnection,
  construction, energized load, and collected cash.

## Forensic earnings-quality questions

### 1. What exactly is FCFbG after the acquisition?

Management’s FCF before growth measure is useful for operating comparison, but
the FY2025 filed bridge leaves $766M before the LS Power platform. Reconcile
adjusted FCF to GAAP OCF, collateral, working-capital movements, maintenance
capital, project capital, acquisition financing, interest, and common-owner cash.

### 2. Does the LS Power cohort earn its capital cost?

The transaction expands generation and customer capability, but it also adds
depreciation, debt, interest, integration, environmental obligations, and fleet
maintenance. Track acquired MW, availability, realized margin, capex, cash
interest, and post-acquisition owner cash—not only adjusted EBITDA contribution.

### 3. Is BYOP genuinely customer-funded?

Inspect take-or-pay provisions, deposits, construction contributions, credit
support, curtailment rights, interconnection milestones, and termination
payments. If NRG builds first and the hyperscaler can delay or reduce load,
the risk has been transferred back to the company or its financing partners.

### 4. Are retail profits durable or weather-assisted?

Separate customer count from gross margin per customer, weather-normalized
usage, hedging, bad debt, churn, regulatory recovery, and customer-acquisition
cost. A large retail book can be a hedge for generation, but it can also turn
into a working-capital and affordability burden during extreme conditions.

### 5. Are capital returns covered?

2025 dividends and repurchases exceeded the mechanical residual by roughly
$956M. Test whether the difference came from cash accumulated in prior periods,
asset sales, debt, collateral release, or genuine recurring free cash. This is
especially important as the company funds integration and new generation.

## Incentives, customer funding, and cohort position

NRG's investor case emphasizes customer-backed growth, FCF before growth, and
capital returns. Each measure needs a boundary. A customer announcement is not
a funded project; FCF before growth is not cash after the LS Power purchase and
maintenance burden; and buybacks are not evidence of excess cash when returns
exceeded the FY2025 mechanical residual by about $956M.

The BYOP structure should therefore be tested as a contract and financing
mechanism, not as a theme. The next filing or project disclosure should show
customer credit support, deposits or construction contributions, take-or-pay
terms, interconnection responsibility, cancellation rights, and the timing of
energized load. If those protections are weak, the company retains the risk of
building generation before demand and collecting enough cash to service the
associated debt.

NRG belongs in the [utility, merchant-power, and large-load first-principles
essay](../first-principles/utility-rate-base-merchant-power-and-large-load.md)
and the [nuclear-scarcity versus regulated and merchant power comparison](../cross-sector/nuclear-scarcity-versus-regulated-and-merchant-power-forensic-comparison-2026-09-13.md).
Compared with regulated utilities, NRG has more customer and market exposure;
compared with pure merchant generators, it has a retail relationship and
demand-response tools. Those relationships can improve utilization and
customer alignment, but they do not remove fuel, collateral, maintenance,
acquisition, or leverage risk.

## Lyn Alden / Damodaran / forensic synthesis

- **Lyn Alden lens:** NRG benefits from essential demand and physical power
  scarcity, but rate conditions, leverage, customer affordability, and fuel or
  market volatility determine whether that demand is financially resilient.
- **Damodaran lens:** value the retail relationship, existing generation, CPower
  demand response, LS Power acquisition, BYOP projects, and Vivint/smart-home
  platform as distinct lifecycle cohorts with different reinvestment and
  terminal-value assumptions.
- **Forensic lens:** the main category errors are treating customer announcements
  as energized load, acquired EBITDA as organic cash, FCFbG as unrestricted
  common cash, and capital returns as proof that the enlarged platform is
  self-funding.

## Valuation framing

The 2026-09-11 market snapshot was approximately $23.85B of equity value at
$113.46 per share, using the official IR closing-price record and 210.2M shares
reported at July 31, 2026. That is about 31x the FY2025 mechanical residual of
$766M, before the post-LS Power capital structure. A normalized range of $0.7B
/$1.3B/$2.0B at 18x/22x/26x implies $12.6B/$28.6B/$52.0B. The market therefore
requires successful integration and a durable post-acquisition cash lift, not
just a larger generation footprint.

## Thesis breakers and next checks

1. LS Power generation and CPower fail to earn above the acquisition’s full
   financing, maintenance, and integration burden.
2. BYOP projects are announced but not contractually customer-funded or
   energized on schedule.
3. Retail weather normalization, churn, bad debt, or affordability pressure
   reduces the customer platform’s cash contribution.
4. Debt and collateral requirements consume the cash that management labels
   available for growth or repurchases.
5. Generation availability, fuel costs, or market rules weaken the assumed
   reliability margin.
6. Capital returns remain above filed residual cash during the integration and
   construction cycle.

Next filing checks: LS Power purchase accounting and pro forma debt; acquired
fleet availability and cash; CPower volumes and margin; BYOP contract terms and
customer contributions; retail gross margin and receivables; capex split;
interest and collateral; goodwill; asset-retirement obligations; and buyback
coverage after integration.
