# Option Care Health, Inc. — alternate-site infusion, therapy routing, and owner cash

Date baseline: `2026-09-14`. Primary evidence: [Option Care source ledger](../../extracted/healthcare/specialized-health-services/option-care-health-inc/source-ledger.md), [Option Care company packet](../../extracted/healthcare/specialized-health-services/option-care-health-inc/company-packet.md), the FY2025 Form 10-K, and the Q1–Q2 FY2026 filings and results materials listed in that ledger.

## Investment question

Option Care provides home and alternate-site infusion services, specialty-drug
administration, nursing, pharmacy operations, and care coordination. It sits
between the therapy manufacturer, payer, prescriber, patient, and site of
care.

The investment question is whether the company can convert complex recurring
therapy into durable owner cash after drug procurement, nursing labor, pharmacy
operations, patient support, payer authorization, reimbursement pressure,
inventory, receivables, quality, acquisitions, debt, and capital required to
maintain national service density.

This is not primarily a drug-IP business. It earns from executing a therapy
pathway and moving care out of a hospital when clinically and economically
appropriate. The valuable control point is workflow reliability, not ownership
of the underlying medicine.

## What the patient, provider, and payer are buying

The patient is buying a therapy delivered at home or another alternate site,
with nursing, pharmacy preparation, supplies, education, scheduling, and
follow-up. The prescriber and payer are buying a reliable way to administer a
complex therapy while managing cost, safety, adherence, and utilization.

Option Care's operating sequence is:

`therapy prescribed -> payer authorization -> drug sourcing and pharmacy
preparation -> nurse and site scheduling -> administration -> monitoring and
documentation -> claim and collection -> repeat therapy -> care coordination
-> owner cash`

Option Care controls pharmacy and nursing workflow, national coverage, payer
and provider relationships, scheduling, and clinical execution. It does not
control drug pricing, payer policy, physician prescribing, patient adherence,
or every therapy outcome.

## Operating denominator

Revenue alone is insufficient because specialty-drug revenue can include a
large pass-through component with a different margin from nursing and
coordination services. Track:

- therapy episodes, patients, and administrations;
- revenue by therapy and site of care;
- drug acquisition cost and gross margin;
- nursing hours, productivity, and service intensity;
- payer authorization, denial, and collection rates;
- inventory, drug expiration, and working capital;
- patient adherence, adverse events, readmissions, and quality;
- pharmacy and branch density;
- operating cash after receivables, inventory, and capital expenditure;
- acquisitions, integration, debt, interest, and buybacks; and
- diluted cash per share after stock compensation.

The correct owner-cash denominator is collected contribution after drug cost,
nursing, pharmacy, supplies, support, working capital, quality, and required
technology or facility investment.

## Therapy economics and site-of-care migration

Moving an infusion from a hospital to a home or alternate site can reduce
facility cost and improve patient convenience. It can also shift responsibility
to pharmacy preparation, nursing, supplies, transportation, scheduling,
training, monitoring, and patient or caregiver adherence.

The bridge is:

`therapy episodes × drug and service reimbursement -> revenue -> drug cost,
nursing, pharmacy, supplies, administration, support, authorization, and
quality -> working capital and receivables -> technology, facilities,
acquisitions, debt, and maintenance capital -> owner cash`

Site-of-care migration creates value only if the total cost and clinical
outcome improve after the entire alternate-site system is funded. A lower
hospital bill does not automatically mean a higher Option Care margin or safer
patient outcome.

Therapy mix is central. A high-cost drug with a thin retained margin can create
large revenue and working-capital requirements. A clinically intensive service
can carry better contribution but require more nursing labor and support. The
company should be analyzed by therapy, payer, site, and service intensity where
the filing permits.

## Payer authorization and cash conversion

Complex therapies often require prior authorization, documentation, utilization
management, and coordination among prescriber, payer, pharmacy, and patient.
The company may incur drug and labor cost before reimbursement is final.

Track:

- billed, approved, denied, and paid claims;
- days sales outstanding and payer concentration;
- inventory purchased before authorization or administration;
- drug returns, expiration, rebates, and chargebacks;
- patient responsibility and charity or bad-debt adjustments;
- retroactive rate changes and contract settlements; and
- cash conversion by therapy and payer.

Revenue recognition does not settle the collection question. A profitable
episode on paper can consume cash if the drug is purchased early, the claim is
delayed, or the payer disputes authorization.

## Nursing and pharmacy labor

Option Care's workflow requires pharmacists, nurses, pharmacy technicians,
coordinators, and support staff. Nursing availability affects capacity,
service quality, and the ability to deliver new therapies.

The labor bridge is:

`authorized patients and episodes -> scheduled administrations -> nursing and
pharmacy hours -> wage and productivity -> successful treatment and collection
-> repeat referral and contribution`

More episodes can require proportionally more labor if therapy complexity or
travel increases. National scale may improve recruiting, training, pharmacy
utilization, and scheduling, but it does not remove local labor shortages or
the cost of maintaining clinical quality.

## Earnings-quality and financial-engineering tests

### 1. Revenue growth can be drug-price or mix growth

Separate therapy volume, drug price, service fees, mix, acquisitions, and
reimbursement. A higher revenue number with unchanged or lower retained
contribution is not durable growth.

### 2. Gross margin must include therapy and service mix

Drug procurement, rebates, chargebacks, nursing, pharmacy, and supplies can
move margin in opposite directions. Track the mix instead of assuming that
consolidated margin represents every episode.

### 3. Receivables and inventory can make cash lag earnings

A therapy may be delivered before a payer pays. Inventory may be purchased
before authorization or held for continuity. Compare operating cash with net
income after receivables, inventory, payables, rebates, and claims timing.

### 4. Adjusted EBITDA must be charged for service capacity

Acquisition integration, restructuring, technology, implementation, and
retention costs may be called nonrecurring. If the company must incur them to
maintain a national clinical network, the owner-cash model must include them at
the required run rate.

### 5. Care quality is an economic variable

Medication errors, adverse events, missed visits, readmissions, failed
administrations, and poor documentation can create remediation, legal,
regulatory, payer, and referral costs. Lower service cost is not good if it
reduces successful treatment or payer trust.

### 6. Acquisitions can add density or complexity

Acquisitions may add patients, therapies, branches, referral relationships, and
clinical staff. Test retention, overlap, integration cost, payer contracts,
working capital, and cash return. Revenue consolidation is not evidence of
incremental value without a return calculation.

## Lyn Alden lens: healthcare liquidity and durability

The demand for complex therapies can be durable because it is tied to chronic
conditions, specialty treatment, and clinical need. The cash flow remains
liquidity-sensitive because payers control authorization and reimbursement,
patients can face affordability and adherence barriers, and the provider must
fund drug inventory and skilled labor.

Site-of-care migration may expand during efforts to reduce healthcare cost,
but policy or payer rules can change which therapies are covered at home. A
recession may not remove medical need, but it can increase payer pressure,
authorization friction, staffing difficulty, and collection risk.

The durable control point is a trusted national clinical workflow that can
deliver complicated therapy safely outside a hospital. Its durability depends
on payer economics, clinician supply, quality, and working-capital resilience.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers and lifecycle

The narrative should become:

`eligible therapy demand × episodes × retained service contribution -> nursing,
pharmacy, drug, support, authorization, and quality cost -> working capital,
technology, maintenance capital, acquisitions, debt, and dilution -> owner cash`

The model should separate:

1. therapy volume from drug price and mix;
2. drug pass-through from retained service revenue;
3. home care from alternate-site and other settings;
4. organic growth from acquisitions;
5. nursing and pharmacy capacity from corporate overhead;
6. receivables and inventory investment from reported profit; and
7. maintenance systems from expansion and buyback spending.

Option Care is a specialized-service growth platform. Reinvestment creates
value if national scale, payer access, pharmacy density, and clinical workflow
raise collected contribution per episode after labor, quality, working capital,
and capital. Revenue growth that requires proportionally more drug inventory or
nursing support is less valuable than growth with improving retained
contribution.

## Management incentives and thesis breakers

Management has reason to emphasize revenue, therapy breadth, alternate-site
scale, adjusted EBITDA, adjusted EPS, guidance, national coverage, and cash
discipline. Pair each with episodes, retained contribution, payer collection,
inventory, nursing capacity, quality, capex, acquisitions, debt, and diluted
shares.

The thesis weakens if:

- revenue grows mainly through drug price, mix, or acquisitions while service
  episodes and retained contribution weaken;
- payer denials, authorization delays, rebates, chargebacks, or receivables
  consume more cash;
- nursing and pharmacy labor cost rises faster than reimbursement;
- home or alternate-site growth increases adverse events, missed treatments,
  readmissions, or support cost;
- therapy growth requires increasing inventory and working capital without
  adequate cash return;
- acquisitions add revenue but not payer access, density, productivity, or
  per-share cash;
- liquidity depends on an expanded credit facility while debt and inventory
  claims rise; or
- adjusted EBITDA improves while GAAP earnings, operating cash, or diluted
  cash per share do not.
