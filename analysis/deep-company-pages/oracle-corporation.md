# Oracle Corporation — enterprise software control financed through AI-cloud expansion

Research date: 2026-09-14  
Evidence window: FY2025, FY2026, and Q1 FY2027  
Ticker: ORCL  
Evidence grade: A for reported financials and operating metrics; B for the ultimate economics of large AI contracts, customer-supplied equipment, and the return on the current capacity buildout.

## Bottom line

Oracle controls important enterprise records and processes through databases, enterprise applications, and long-lived customer relationships. Its software is deeply integrated into accounting, supply chains, human resources, procurement, and other institutional processes. That creates switching costs: replacing Oracle can require changing data structures, application logic, controls, employee skills, and regulatory documentation.

Oracle is now trying to extend that control into cloud infrastructure. The strategy can increase the value of the installed software base and create a larger cloud relationship. It also changes the financial model. FY2026 revenue was $67.357B and operating cash flow was $31.977B, but capital expenditures were $55.663B. Reported free cash flow was negative $23.686B. The company raised $43B of debt and $5B of equity financing during FY2026. This is not a small temporary investment program relative to the existing cash engine.

The central investment question is whether Oracle can earn an adequate return on AI infrastructure while preserving the high-retention economics of database and application software. Large remaining performance obligations, or RPO, provide evidence of contracted demand. They do not by themselves prove that the contracts will generate attractive cash after equipment, power, facilities, interest, preferred financing, customer incentives, depreciation, and execution costs.

## What Oracle controls

The main operating chain is:

`enterprise data and process -> Oracle database or application -> migration and integration work -> cloud deployment -> recurring support, application, and infrastructure revenue`

Oracle controls the database layer, application logic, enterprise support relationship, migration tools, and increasingly the cloud environment where customers run the workload. It can use one layer to sell another: database customers can be offered cloud infrastructure, application customers can be offered a common data and security environment, and AI customers can be offered access to infrastructure connected to enterprise data.

The company does not control customer IT budgets, cloud pricing, the supply of GPUs, power availability, construction schedules, the cost of debt, customer credit quality, or the future usefulness of any particular chip or facility. It also does not control how much of the large contract value becomes actual usage.

The change from FY2025 to FY2026 is the key forensic fact. Operating cash increased by $11.156B, but capital expenditures increased by $34.448B. Growth consumed more cash than the existing operations generated after capital investment.

## Revenue mix and current operating direction

FY2026 revenue included approximately $34.0B of cloud revenue, $24.5B of software revenue, $5.7B of services revenue, and $3.1B of hardware revenue. Cloud infrastructure revenue grew 77% to $18.1B; cloud applications revenue grew 11% to $15.9B. The cloud infrastructure rate is much faster, but it also carries the heavier equipment and financing burden.

Q1 FY2027 continued the shift. Revenue was $19.345B, cloud revenue was $11.607B, and cloud infrastructure revenue was $7.388B, up 121% year over year. Cloud applications revenue was $4.219B, up 10%. Q1 operating cash flow was $23.103B, but capital expenditures were $28.499B, leaving negative free cash flow of roughly $5.396B before other claims.

The Q1 result shows why quarterly cash flow must be read carefully. Operating cash was unusually high because deferred revenue from customer prepayments with a significant financing component increased by $11.363B. That is useful funding, but it is not free profit. Oracle must provide future service and may also incur financing, equipment, energy, and maintenance costs while doing so.

## RPO: visibility, financing, and quality

RPO reached $638B at FY2026 year-end, up 363% year over year. Oracle said most of the increase came from large AI contracts. It also said that $75B of prepaid and customer-supplied hardware in those contracts reduced the capital Oracle had to raise for the AI data centers.

This is important evidence, but it creates several questions rather than closing the analysis:

1. What portion of RPO is fixed payment, usage-based payment, or contingent on capacity being delivered?
2. What portion will be recognized as revenue in the next year and at what margin?
3. Do customer prepayments carry a financing component, and what is the economic cost of providing the capacity?
4. When customers supply GPUs, who owns the equipment, who bears failure and replacement risk, and how are availability and depreciation reflected?
5. Can Oracle earn an adequate return on facilities and networking even when the hardware is customer-funded?
6. What happens if AI demand, model prices, or customer usage changes before the contracted capacity is fully used?

RPO is therefore a demand and funding indicator, not a substitute for normalized owner cash. A large number can coexist with negative free cash flow if the company must build capacity before revenue and cash margins arrive.

## Cash conversion and accounting quality

### Customer prepayments are useful but carry an obligation

Oracle states that customers are generally billed in advance for cloud services and license support. That improves liquidity and makes operating cash flow look stronger than current-period earnings in periods of rapid contract growth. The accounting is appropriate: revenue is recognized as service is delivered. The investor must still distinguish cash collected today from cash that can be distributed today.

In FY2026, the cash-flow statement separately identified a $4.592B increase in deferred revenue from customer prepayments with a significant financing component. In Q1 FY2027, the same item increased by $11.363B. This is a central item for the owner-cash analysis because it acts as customer-funded financing while creating future service obligations.

### Non-GAAP earnings remove recurring compensation

Oracle excluded $4.811B of stock compensation from FY2026 non-GAAP earnings. In Q1 FY2027 it excluded $1.127B. Oracle’s own release states that stock-based compensation recurs in future periods. It is therefore not a clean one-time adjustment. The correct analysis shows GAAP operating income, non-GAAP operating income, stock compensation, diluted shares, and repurchases together.

### Depreciation is rising with the infrastructure build

FY2026 depreciation was $7.623B compared with $3.867B in FY2025. Q1 FY2027 depreciation was $3.156B compared with $1.351B in the prior-year quarter. Rising depreciation is expected when a company adds data-center assets, but it is evidence that the accounting cost of the installed capacity is beginning to enter earnings. Compare depreciation with capital spending, useful lives, utilization, and the cash needed to replace the equipment.

### Financing can move the burden without removing it

Oracle’s financing supports expansion, but debt interest and preferred-stock economics reduce the cash available to common shareholders. At August 31, 2026, Oracle reported $117.712B of non-current notes payable and other borrowings, $7.625B of current notes payable and other borrowings, and $30.594B of operating-lease liabilities. It also had $36.369B of cash and $708M of marketable securities.

The balance sheet is not a simple insolvency story. It is a return and refinancing story. The company must convert contracted demand into enough operating cash to cover equipment replacement, interest, leases, preferred dividends, taxes, and common-owner distributions. If it must repeatedly issue debt or equity to fund growth, the common-owner claim becomes more dependent on financing markets and less dependent on internally generated cash.

## Lyn Alden lens: liquidity, cycles, and durability

Oracle’s installed software and support base is more durable than a purely usage-driven cloud business. Database and enterprise applications can remain embedded through budget cycles because they support records and processes that customers cannot casually remove. That gives Oracle a stabilizing cash source.

The new cloud infrastructure program adds different macro exposures: interest rates, debt-market access, power prices, construction bottlenecks, GPU supply, customer prepayments, and the risk that capacity becomes less valuable as computing prices fall. The company can be strategically right about long-term AI demand and still produce weak common-owner returns if the cost and timing of capacity buildout are unfavorable.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers

The narrative is: Oracle is the trusted database and enterprise-application platform, and AI is causing customers to place more workloads inside Oracle Cloud. The valuation must translate that narrative into separate operating assumptions:

- retention and pricing for database support and applications;
- migration from on-premise licenses to cloud subscriptions;
- cloud infrastructure utilization and price per unit of compute;
- RPO conversion into revenue and cash;
- customer prepayment and customer-supplied GPU arrangements;
- capital expenditures, depreciation, useful lives, and replacement requirements;
- interest expense, preferred dividends, lease obligations, and dilution;
- stock compensation and the change in diluted common shares;
- the share of future growth funded by operating cash versus debt and equity.

At a market value of approximately $450.840B on September 14, 2026, Oracle was valued at about 19 times FY2026 revenue and about 19 times FY2026 GAAP net income. A cash-based scenario is more demanding because FY2026 free cash flow was negative:

| Normalized annual owner-cash screen | Multiple | Implied equity value |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $5B bear case | 20x | $100B |
| $15B base case | 30x | $450B |
| $30B strong case | 30x | $900B |

These are scenario outputs, not price targets. The base case requires Oracle to convert its large RPO into recurring cash after the infrastructure program, financing cost, preferred claims, and stock compensation. The strong case requires both rapid cloud growth and materially better cash conversion than FY2026.

## Thesis breakers and next evidence

The thesis weakens if RPO growth does not convert into revenue and cash; if large AI contracts require continuing debt and equity financing; if customer prepayments mask weak unit economics; if cloud infrastructure growth remains high while cash after capital spending stays negative; if depreciation and interest rise faster than cloud gross profit; if customer-supplied hardware transfers operational risk to Oracle without adequate compensation; if software support declines faster than cloud applications grow; if stock compensation and preferred claims reduce common-owner cash; or if AI capacity becomes less valuable before the facilities earn an acceptable return.

The next filings should answer: How much of the $638B RPO became revenue? How much was fixed versus usage-based? What was the cash cost of capital additions after customer prepayments and short-term financing? Did cloud infrastructure margin improve as utilization rose? What portion of the $75B prepaid or customer-supplied hardware arrangement is economically risk-free to Oracle? Did debt, preferred financing, and diluted shares rise faster than cash per common share? And did the application and database franchise remain stable while Oracle funded the infrastructure build?
