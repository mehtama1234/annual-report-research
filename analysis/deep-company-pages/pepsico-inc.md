# PepsiCo — packaged food, beverage scale, and the affordability ceiling

## The investment question

PepsiCo is a large food-and-beverage system with two different economic jobs. Convenient foods provide frequent consumption and strong shelf presence. Beverages provide global brand reach and channel scale. The question is whether that scale creates recurring owner cash after the company funds ingredients, packaging, labor, retailer support, distribution, acquisitions, debt, and product reformulation.

The central risk is that reported sales can remain stable while the consumer buys fewer units, chooses cheaper packages, or shifts toward private label. PepsiCo therefore has to be read through volume, price/mix, gross margin, operating profit, cash flow, retailer concentration, and the returns on portfolio acquisitions.

Related first-principles explanation: [Routine consumer brands, bottlers, and owner cash](../first-principles/routine-consumer-brand-bottler-and-owner-cash.md).

## What the filings show

PepsiCo reported FY2025 net revenue of $93.9B, core operating profit of $14.9B, and company-defined free cash flow of $8.2B. Operating cash flow was $12.087B, capital spending was $4.415B, and proceeds from property sales were $528M. The company paid $7.6B of dividends. This is a large cash engine, but the distribution of that cash leaves limited room for error if operating profit weakens or acquisition spending rises.

| Fiscal year | Company free cash flow ($M) | Operating cash flow ($M) | Capital spending ($M) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 2023 | 8,122 | 13,442 | 5,518 |
| 2024 | 7,531 | 12,507 | 5,318 |
| 2025 | 8,200 | 12,087 | 4,415 |

The 2025 increase in free cash flow did not come from higher operating cash flow. It came partly from lower capital spending and $528M of property-sale proceeds. That does not make the cash false, but it means the recurring cash denominator should be tested against normal capital needs rather than accepted at face value.

PepsiCo also paid $1.95B for poppi and $1.2B for Garza Food Ventures, or Siete, in 2025. Those acquisitions may strengthen functional beverages and better-for-you food, but their value depends on incremental volume, gross profit, and cash after integration and marketing. They should not be treated as free growth.

### The cash bridge in plain terms

The reported 2025 cash path is:

`$12.087B operating cash - $4.415B capital spending + $528M property-sale proceeds = $8.200B company free cash flow`

Operating cash is the cash produced by the reported business before capital
spending. Capital spending pays for factories, equipment, technology, and other
long-lived assets. Property-sale proceeds are cash, but they are not repeat
operating receipts. On this presentation, free cash flow was therefore about
`$5.8B` before property-sale proceeds. That is a screening adjustment, not a
replacement for PepsiCo's own definition: the next filing should show whether
the lower capital spending level can continue without deferring needed plant,
distribution, or capacity work.

The 2023-to-2025 record also shows why one year is insufficient. Operating cash
fell from `$13.442B` in 2023 to `$12.087B` in 2025, while company free cash flow
rose from `$8.122B` to `$8.200B` because capital spending declined. The central
question is whether this is a temporary investment cycle or a lower recurring
cash base being presented more favorably by lower spending.

### Control point and burden carrier

| Layer | PepsiCo's role | Main burden to test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Brand and product portfolio | Creates consumer choice through brands, formulas, package design, and marketing | Brand support, reformulation, portfolio acquisitions, impairment, and regulation |
| Manufacturing and distribution | Produces and moves a large portion of food and beverage products | Ingredients, packaging, labor, plants, vehicles, inventory, and execution |
| Retail channel | Provides access to households and controls shelf and promotion conditions | Retailer bargaining power, trade spending, payment terms, and channel inventory |
| Common shareholder | Receives cash after operations and reinvestment | Lower volume, higher support costs, debt, dilution, and acquisition underperformance |

This is why PepsiCo cannot be evaluated only on brand recognition or reported
revenue. The company controls important product and distribution relationships,
but it still funds substantial physical operations and must negotiate with large
retailers.

## Operating economics

Approximately 56% of net revenue came from the United States and 44% from outside the United States. The portfolio was approximately 58% food and 42% beverage. That balance gives PepsiCo more ways to respond to a weak category, but it also creates more input, labor, manufacturing, and distribution obligations than a concentrate-led beverage company.

Walmart and its affiliates represented about 14% of consolidated net revenue in 2025. Retailer scale can provide reach and volume, but it also gives the retailer negotiation power over price, promotion, shelf placement, payment terms, and inventory. A PepsiCo sale is therefore not equivalent to a consumer choosing the product at full economic value. The filing must be read for trade spending, promotional allowances, customer concentration, and channel inventory.

Management's 2026 message emphasizes value, affordability, productivity, and innovation. Q1 and Q2 2026 materials reported improving volume, including international organic growth of 7%, convenient-food volume growth of 3%, and beverage volume growth of 2% in Q2. These are encouraging operating signals, but they must be separated from temporary comparisons, distribution gains from acquired brands, and price actions that may reduce future frequency.

## Forensic earnings-quality and financial-engineering questions

1. **Price versus volume:** Revenue growth is not enough. Test whether volume and market share hold after price increases and package changes.
2. **Cash-flow dependence on working capital:** Reconcile operating cash flow for inventory, receivables, payables, promotional accruals, and tax payments. Favorable working-capital timing can make a stable business look temporarily stronger.
3. **Acquisition returns:** Track poppi and Siete separately. Required evidence is acquired revenue, incremental operating profit, integration cost, working-capital needs, impairment testing, and post-acquisition volume.
4. **Adjusted profit:** Core operating profit and core EPS are useful management measures, but restructuring, acquisition, impairment, and productivity charges should be compared across years. A recurring “one-time” charge is an operating cost.
5. **Capital spending:** The 2025 reduction in capital spending helped free cash flow. Check whether maintenance and capacity spending is being deferred while the company describes productivity.
6. **Debt and shareholder returns:** PepsiCo returned $8.6B through dividends and repurchases in 2025 while issuing $8.2B of long-term debt and paying $4.1B of debt borrowings. The question is not whether debt is allowed; it is whether shareholder returns remain covered by recurring cash after investment.
7. **Stock compensation and incentives:** Read the proxy for performance metrics, payout thresholds, and dilution. If adjusted EPS and revenue receive more weight than volume, return on invested capital, or cash after acquisitions, incentives can encourage price-led growth and portfolio expansion.

The unresolved items are not accusations of accounting manipulation. They are
the specific places where the next annual report, proxy, or quarterly filing
must add evidence.

## Balance-sheet resilience and management incentives

PepsiCo's 2026 plan calls for about `$8.9B` of shareholder returns, including
roughly `$7.9B` of dividends and `$1.0B` of repurchases. The 2025 record also
shows `$8.2B` of long-term debt issued and `$4.1B` of debt borrowings paid. The
relevant resilience test is whether operations can fund dividends, normal
capital spending, acquisitions, and debt service together when volume is weak
and input costs rise. A plan that remains feasible only through new borrowing,
asset sales, or lower maintenance investment is less resilient than the
headline cash number suggests.

Management incentives should be read in the proxy alongside the operating
claims. The important questions are whether executive pay rewards revenue and
adjusted EPS more heavily than physical volume, market share, return on
invested capital, and cash after acquisitions; whether productivity savings can
be reported while necessary brand or plant investment is deferred; and whether
repurchases offset stock issuance or merely keep the diluted share count from
rising. The current company packet identifies these questions but does not
contain the proxy scorecard, so this remains an open diligence item.

## Lyn Alden lens

PepsiCo has defensive categories and global distribution, but defensive does not mean insensitive to purchasing power. In an inflationary or slower-growth environment, households can keep buying snacks and drinks while trading down, reducing package size, changing channels, or buying fewer premium products. The relevant resilience measure is cash after affordability actions and retailer support, not merely reported revenue.

The international footprint adds diversification but also currency translation, local pricing, political, tax, and supply-chain exposure. Debt-funded shareholder returns become less flexible when rates rise or operating cash flow falls. The balance sheet should therefore be judged against a weaker-margin and weaker-volume case, not only management's base outlook.

## Damodaran lens and valuation

The narrative is “large brands, frequent consumption, international breadth, and productivity-funded reinvestment.” To value it, that narrative must become assumptions about unit volume, price/mix, gross margin, operating margin, capital spending, working capital, acquisition spending, taxes, and terminal growth.

At a market value of approximately $186.6B, the equity value is about 22.8 times FY2025 reported free cash flow. A simple scenario screen is:

| Normalized annual owner cash | Multiple | Implied equity value |
|---:|---:|---:|
| $6.5B bear case | 16x | $104B |
| $8.5B base case | 20x | $170B |
| $10.5B strong case | 24x | $252B |

These are not price targets. They show that the current valuation requires PepsiCo to preserve a large cash base while funding affordability, reinvestment, acquisitions, and shareholder returns. The base case assumes cash recovery without requiring permanent price increases.

## Thesis breakers and next evidence

The thesis weakens if North American volume and market share decline while price/mix carries revenue; if productivity savings replace necessary brand and capacity investment; if poppi or Siete require repeated impairment or fail to produce incremental returns; if retailer concessions expand; if working capital repeatedly supplies operating cash; or if debt-funded dividends and buybacks persist during weak cash conversion.

The next filings should answer: Did volume grow without disproportionate promotion? Did gross margin recover after affordability actions? Did acquisitions add profit after marketing and integration? Did capital spending return to a normal level? Did operating cash flow improve without a working-capital release? And does the proxy show incentives aligned with recurring cash and invested-capital returns?
