# Planet Fitness — affordable membership, franchise density, and owner cash

Date: `2026-09-14`. Primary evidence: [Planet Fitness company packet](../../extracted/services/recreation/planet-fitness-inc/company-packet.md) and [Planet Fitness source ledger](../../extracted/services/recreation/planet-fitness-inc/source-ledger.md).

## Investment question

Planet Fitness sells low-friction, recurring fitness access through a branded
membership network. It is not primarily selling equipment or premium coaching.
The customer pays for affordable access, a welcoming environment, routine,
confidence, and a manageable self-maintenance habit.

The company controls the brand, membership relationship, franchise system,
pricing architecture, equipment standards, advertising, and club expansion.
Independent franchisees own about `90%` of clubs and carry much of the labor,
rent, local execution, and equipment burden.

The question is whether corporate recurring fees and franchise growth create
durable common cash after member acquisition, marketing, technology, support,
stock compensation, debt, buybacks, and the health of franchisees.

## Operating model and control point

`affordable price -> member joins -> club attendance and habit -> annual/monthly
fees -> franchisee club economics -> corporate royalty and support fee -> brand
and network investment -> owner cash`

Planet Fitness had about `20.8M` members and `2,896` clubs at FY2025 year-end;
`2,604` clubs were franchisee-owned and `292` corporate-owned. The corporate
company therefore captures a fee and brand layer while franchisees provide
physical access, local staff, rent, cleaning, and member service.

The control point is not membership count alone. It is the combination of low
price, low intimidation, convenient locations, consistent club experience,
franchise density, and a brand that turns occasional intent into recurring
payment.

## Operating evidence

| Period | Demand and network | Financial evidence | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| FY2025 | Revenue growth `12.1%`; about `20.8M` members; `2,896` clubs; `181` clubs opened; same-club sales `6.7%`; new Classic Card price after a `50%` increase | Net income `$219.1M`; adjusted EBITDA `$551.6M`; system-wide sales about `$5.3B` | Price, membership, and unit growth remained strong, but franchisee returns and retention need the next test |
| Q4 2025 | Revenue `$376.3M`, same-club sales `6.7%`, adjusted EBITDA `$146.3M`; net member growth `1.1M` for the year | Net income `$60.4M`; adjusted net income `$69.0M` | Annual close supports a recurring-value and network-density thesis |
| Q1 2026 | Revenue `$337.2M`, same-club sales `3.5%`, members about `21.5M`, clubs `2,909` | Net income `$51.6M`; adjusted EBITDA `$139.9M` | Management said member growth was slower than expected and paused a planned Black Card increase |
| Q2 2026 | Revenue `$365.2M`, same-club sales `1.7%`, system-wide sales about `$1.4B`, `23` new clubs, clubs `2,930` | Net income `$67.1M`; adjusted EBITDA `$152.8M`; cash and marketable securities `$544.4M` | Unit growth continued while same-club momentum weakened; membership and pricing repair became the main test |

The sequence matters. FY2025 showed strong value and pricing evidence. By Q2
2026, new-club growth continued but same-club sales slowed. This does not
prove the model is broken; it shows why membership growth, retention, pricing,
attendance, and franchisee economics must be analyzed together.

## Membership economics

New members were offered a Classic Card starting at `$15` per month and a PF
Black Card starting at `$24.99` per month. The low entry price supports broad
participation, but low price also means small changes in retention, payment
processing, marketing, rent, labor, or franchisee margin matter.

The customer value chain is:

`membership fee -> expected attendance and habit -> club capacity and service
cost -> renewal -> franchisee cash -> corporate royalty and advertising fee`

Track members, net additions, churn, annual fees, dues per member, Black Card
mix, attendance, payment failures, promotions, and same-club sales. A member
who pays but does not attend can be profitable for the club, but persistent
underuse may weaken renewal or brand trust. A price increase can lift revenue
per member while reducing future additions or franchisee volume.

## Franchisee economics and burden transfer

Independent franchisees own most clubs and fund local execution. They pay rent,
labor, utilities, cleaning, equipment, insurance, local marketing, and debt.
Corporate Planet Fitness receives royalties and other fees with less direct
property and labor burden.

That burden transfer is economically useful only if franchisees earn adequate
returns. If club-level economics weaken, franchisees may defer equipment,
reduce staffing, close clubs, resist new openings, or reduce service quality.
The corporate fee stream could look stable before the network's health becomes
visible.

Next filings should disclose or permit estimation of franchisee sales,
occupancy, club-level margins, closures, openings, remodels, equipment needs,
franchisee liquidity, and royalty collection.

## Cash conversion and financial engineering

The corporate model can report high adjusted EBITDA because much of the local
club cost sits with franchisees. That is not automatically misleading. It does
mean the analyst must ask whether corporate fees adequately fund brand support,
marketing, technology, equipment standards, and franchise health.

Q2 2026 included about `$200.0M` of share repurchases for roughly `4.0M`
shares, while cash and marketable securities ended at `$544.4M`. The buyback
should be evaluated against debt, SBC, diluted shares, franchisee support,
required technology, and the slowing same-club trend—not only against adjusted
EPS.

The forensic questions are:

1. Does adjusted EBITDA reconcile to GAAP income and operating cash without
   recurring exclusions?
2. Are share repurchases reducing diluted shares after stock compensation?
3. Does the company capitalize costs or classify support spending in a way that
   understates the cost of maintaining the network?
4. Are franchise fees collected reliably, or are weak franchisees receiving
   concessions or payment extensions?
5. Does equipment placement and club opening create future royalty cash or
   simply increase corporate support requirements?
6. Did the large price increase improve lifetime member economics after churn
   and acquisition cost?

## Macro and liquidity lens

Fitness participation is discretionary but linked to health, confidence,
appearance, routine, and social comfort. The low price can make Planet Fitness
more resilient than premium fitness during household pressure, but members can
still cancel, delay joining, or trade down.

Franchisees are more exposed to rent, labor, utilities, local competition,
equipment replacement, and debt. A credit contraction can slow club openings
even if consumer demand remains stable. The durability test is whether the
brand and franchise system remain healthy when the company cannot rely on
continuous unit expansion.

## Damodaran narrative-to-numbers bridge

The narrative is “affordable, recurring, franchise-scaled wellness access.”
Translate it into:

`members × dues and annual fees × retention × Black Card mix -> franchisee
revenue -> club cost and return -> corporate royalty, advertising, and support
cash -> debt, SBC, buybacks, and diluted owner cash`

Forecast separately:

- gross and net membership additions;
- churn, attendance, and payment collection;
- price and mix changes;
- same-club sales and franchisee margin;
- new club openings, closures, and equipment placement;
- corporate versus franchisee-owned club economics;
- marketing, support, technology, and stock compensation; and
- buybacks, debt, and cash per diluted share.

The lifecycle question is whether penetration can continue through new clubs
and member growth, or whether the network is reaching a maturity point where
retention and per-member economics matter more than unit count.

## Valuation framing

Value corporate Planet Fitness on normalized royalty, membership, advertising,
and corporate-club cash after support, technology, marketing, debt, SBC, and
buybacks. Do not capitalize system-wide sales as corporate revenue. Do not
assume each new club produces the same royalty if franchisee returns decline.

The key sensitivity is the relationship between price, member retention, club
opening, and franchisee health. A small recurring fee can support a high value
if retention is strong and capital needs are low; it can also produce a weak
return if growth requires repeated price promotions, heavy acquisition cost, or
franchisee support.

### Initial owner-cash screen — 2026-09-14

The FY2025 Form 10-K reports `$418.4M` of operating cash flow and `$163.7M`
of PP&E purchases. The reported residual is about `$254.8M`; after `$12.3M`
of stock compensation, the initial common-owner measure is about `$241.4M`.
The comparable SBC-adjusted residuals were approximately `$143.1M` in FY2023,
`$179.9M` in FY2024, and `$241.4M` in FY2025. FY2023 also included about
`$43.3M` of net acquisition cash, which is why the historical bridge keeps
acquisition spending separate.

FY2025 common-stock repurchases were about `$500.4M`, more than twice the
SBC-adjusted residual. Long-term debt was approximately `$2.482B` at year-end.
The royalty platform may be asset-light at the parent level, but this capital
return pace leaves less room for franchisee support, equipment programs, and
debt reduction if the club system weakens.

A finance quote retrieved at `2026-09-14 23:15 UTC` showed a share price of
`$51.16` and equity value of approximately `$3.947B`. This is a dated market
input, not filing evidence or a price target.

| Screen | Bear | Base | Bull |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Normalized common-owner cash | `$140M` | `$220M` | `$300M` |
| Equity cash multiple | `14x` | `18x` | `22x` |
| Implied equity value | `$1.960B` | `$3.960B` | `$6.600B` |

The base case assumes membership, dues, advertising, and royalty cash grow
without a comparable rise in corporate support. The bear case assumes
franchisee closures, weaker club returns, price-led additions, or debt pressure
reduce the amount distributable to common owners. The bull case requires club
openings, retention, and franchisee economics to improve while the parent
reduces the mismatch between repurchases and cash generated. The dated market
value is approximately the base case; the repurchase and debt burden make that
base case conditional. This screen is not a price target.

## Management incentives and thesis breakers

Management can emphasize system-wide sales, membership, revenue, same-club
sales, adjusted EBITDA, new clubs, equipment placements, and buybacks. Pair
each with net member growth, churn, price realization, franchisee return,
corporate cash, SBC, debt, and diluted shares.

The thesis breaks if:

- membership growth depends on discounts while churn rises;
- same-club sales remain weak after pricing and marketing changes;
- the Black Card or Classic Card price architecture reduces new-member volume;
- franchisee closures, distress, concessions, or underinvestment increase;
- club openings add royalty revenue but require rising corporate support;
- equipment, rent, labor, or insurance pressure reduces club-level returns;
- adjusted EBITDA grows while cash per diluted share does not; or
- buybacks continue while balance-sheet resilience and franchisee health weaken.
