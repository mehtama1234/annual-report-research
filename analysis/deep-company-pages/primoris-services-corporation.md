# Primoris Services — backlog, renewable-project losses, and field-execution cash

Date baseline: 2026-09-13  
Primary annual filing: [Primoris 2025 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1361538/000110465926018677/prim-20251231x10k.htm)  
Annual report: [Primoris 2025 Annual Report](https://ir.prim.com/~/media/Files/P/Primoris-IR-v2/annual-reports/2025-annual-report-primoris-services-corporation.pdf)  
Companion packet: [Primoris company packet](/home/mehtama1/git-repo/annual-report-research/extracted/industrial-goods/heavy-construction/primoris-services-corporation/company-packet.md)

## Bottom line

Primoris builds and maintains utility, energy, communications, renewable,
industrial, and heavy-civil infrastructure. Its control point is field
execution: labor, equipment, project management, self-performance, permitting,
and the ability to deliver work under a contract. It does not own the power
grid, the data center, or the renewable asset after construction.

FY2025 was strong on reported scale: revenue was $7.575B, net income was
$274.9M, diluted EPS was $5.02, adjusted EBITDA was $531.1M, and backlog was
$11.9B. Operating cash flow was $470.4M and capital expenditures were $129.9M.
But the 2026 filings show why backlog cannot be treated as future profit: Q2
2026 produced a $24.2M net loss and $11.4M adjusted EBITDA while backlog reached
$13.9B. Management attributed the pressure to a limited number of challenged
renewables projects involving scope, redesign, sequencing, and execution.

Current judgment: Primoris has real exposure to grid expansion, gas generation,
renewables, communications, and data-center infrastructure. The shareholder
case depends on contract discipline and field productivity, not merely on the
size of the opportunity or backlog. The central forensic question is whether
reported contract profit is converted into cash after contract assets, retainage,
equipment, labor, warranty, claims, debt, and loss-making project resets.

## What Primoris controls

`customer infrastructure need -> bid and contract -> labor, equipment, and procurement -> field execution -> billed milestone and collected cash`

Primoris controls estimating, project selection, self-perform capability,
equipment availability, labor deployment, project management, and customer
relationships. It does not control weather, subsurface conditions, permitting,
customer design changes, commodity prices, interconnection timing, or the
behavior of subcontractors and suppliers.

The company operates through Utilities and Energy. Utilities includes power
delivery, gas operations, and communications. Energy includes renewables,
industrial construction, heavy civil, maintenance, and pipeline services. This
mix matters because a master-service agreement, a utility line project, a solar
construction contract, and a natural-gas generation project have different
margin, working-capital, and loss risks.

## Three-year reported cash picture

Dollars in millions.

| Fiscal year | Revenue | Net income | Operating cash flow | Capital expenditures | OCF less capex | Cash acquisitions |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2023 | — | 126.1 | 198.5 | 103.0 | 95.5 | 9.3 |
| 2024 | — | 180.9 | 508.3 | 126.5 | 381.8 | 0.0 |
| 2025 | 7,575 | 274.9 | 470.4 | 129.9 | 340.5 | 0.0 |

The 2024 cash increase included favorable working-capital timing, including a
$251.2M increase in contract liabilities from billing terms on certain new
projects. In 2025, contract assets increased by $162.2M while accounts
receivable decreased by $111.3M and accounts payable and accrued liabilities
increased by $148.9M. These movements are normal in construction, but they mean
cash conversion depends on billing and payment timing, not only on reported
contract margin.

Primoris paid $329.3M of long-term debt in 2025 and $224.5M in 2024. Debt
reduction improved the balance sheet, but it also consumed cash that cannot be
distributed or reinvested at the same time. The 2025 cash balance was $535.5M,
up from $455.8M at year-end 2024, including cash and restricted cash in the
cash-flow statement of $541.3M.

## Backlog is visibility, not value

Backlog reached $11.9B at year-end 2025, nearly $3.0B of new work was booked in
Q4, and Q2 2026 backlog reached $13.9B, including $8.2B of master-service-
agreement backlog. The size is economically useful only if each contract has:

1. a defined scope and price;
2. a realistic cost estimate;
3. sufficient labor and equipment capacity;
4. a customer able and willing to pay;
5. manageable change-order and delay terms; and
6. a margin that survives execution.

The 10-K distinguishes fixed backlog from work where scope and contract value
are not adequately defined. The next filing should show fixed backlog,
MSA-backed work, optional work, cancellations, burn rate, gross-margin changes,
and the portion exposed to renewable-project redesign or customer funding.

## Earnings quality and forensic tests

### 1. Percentage-of-completion estimates can move profit before cash

Construction revenue and profit depend on estimates of total contract cost,
percent complete, expected change orders, claims, and losses. A small change in
expected cost on a large project can reverse previously recognized margin or
create a current loss. The Q2 2026 loss is therefore important evidence that
backlog and prior profit recognition do not eliminate estimate risk.

Required tests are contract-asset and contract-liability roll-forwards,
unbilled receivable aging, retainage, billed-to-cost progress, cumulative
catch-up adjustments, loss provisions, claims, and cash collections by segment.

### 2. Contract assets and liabilities can make cash look better or worse

In 2024, contract liabilities increased materially because customers paid or
were billed before Primoris performed the work. In 2025, contract assets rose
as revenue exceeded billing on projects. A strong cash year can reflect advance
billings that later require execution; a weak cash year can reflect legitimate
work performed before billing or collection.

The right question is not whether working capital helped. It is whether the
balance reverses predictably as projects are billed and paid, or whether
contract assets, receivables, and claims keep growing faster than revenue.

### 3. Renewable-project losses test management’s bidding discipline

Management said lower margins on certain renewables projects reduced 2025
gross margin and then described Q1 and Q2 2026 pressure from challenged
renewable projects. This is not automatically misconduct. It is evidence that
contract selection, fixed-price exposure, scope definition, and execution
discipline determine whether strong demand creates value.

Track original bid margin, current estimated margin, cost-to-complete changes,
change-order recovery, project delay responsibility, write-downs, and whether
new awards are being accepted at lower margins to keep backlog growing.

### 4. Self-performance improves control but raises fixed burden

Primoris says it self-performs most work and added more than 2,800 employees in
2025. Self-performance can improve quality, schedule control, and margin capture.
It also creates labor, training, equipment, maintenance, safety, and utilization
costs when project volume changes.

The next filing should connect workforce growth, certified-labor availability,
equipment utilization, wage rates, overtime, safety claims, and project margin.
An apparent operating advantage is weaker if labor and equipment are retained
ahead of executable work.

### 5. Equipment capex is a real growth requirement

Primoris spent $129.9M on property and equipment in 2025, including $75.8M for
construction equipment and $35.3M for facilities. Management expects $120M to
$140M of 2026 capex, including $90M to $110M for construction equipment.

This is not a software-like asset-light model. The equipment supports revenue,
but depreciation, maintenance, replacement, idle capacity, and financing must
be charged before judging owner cash. Sale proceeds from equipment can also
make investing cash look healthier without creating recurring earnings.

## Alden macro and durability lens

Primoris is exposed to power demand, grid replacement, natural-gas generation,
renewable policy, communications construction, industrial reshoring, weather,
rates, and public or utility capital budgets. The current demand environment is
strong, but contractors are usually downstream from the capital decision and
upstream from execution failure.

Liquidity resilience depends on collected cash, contract-asset quality,
equipment financing, surety and bonding capacity, revolver availability, debt
service, and the ability to slow equipment purchases when work weakens. A
record backlog does not protect the balance sheet if customers delay, projects
lose money, or receivables are disputed.

## Damodaran narrative-to-numbers lens

The narrative is “Primoris is a field-execution platform for the power,
communications, and energy buildout.” The model must specify:

1. revenue by Utilities and Energy and by contract type;
2. fixed-price versus reimbursable or MSA work;
3. backlog conversion and cancellation;
4. gross margin after cost-to-complete revisions;
5. labor and equipment productivity;
6. working-capital requirements and collection timing;
7. maintenance and growth equipment capex;
8. debt, surety, lease, and interest requirements; and
9. normalized acquisition and shareholder-return policy.

Primoris is in a capacity-expansion lifecycle, not a mature cash-harvest phase.
Growth is valuable only if each incremental dollar of equipment, labor,
working capital, and contract risk produces cash returns above the funding cost.

## Valuation screen and expectations

The September 11, 2026 market snapshot showed equity value of approximately
$4.065B. A simple screen using OCF less equipment and facility capex gives
$340.5M in 2025, but it does not fully charge debt reduction, working-capital
growth, leases, or project-loss risk.

| Case | Normalized owner-cash screen | Equity cash multiple | Implied equity value |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Bear | $220M | 10x | $2.2B |
| Base | $320M | 14x | $4.5B |
| Bull | $420M | 18x | $7.6B |

The current value is near the base-case screen. That means the market is not
paying only for 2025 cash; it is paying for successful backlog conversion,
renewables-loss containment, continued utility and gas-generation demand, and
cash returns after equipment and working-capital needs.

## Incentives, thesis breakers, and next filings

Management has an incentive to emphasize bookings, backlog, adjusted EBITDA,
self-performance, and end-market growth. Those are useful operating measures,
but they can obscure contract loss, margin catch-up, equipment idle time, and
collection risk. The shareholder test is cash after the work is completed.

The thesis weakens if:

- backlog grows while fixed backlog, billing, and collections do not;
- renewable-project cost revisions continue or spread to utilities and gas work;
- contract assets, receivables, or claims grow faster than revenue;
- labor additions and equipment purchases outrun executable work;
- adjusted EBITDA rises while OCF less capex and debt service falls;
- surety, warranty, safety, or legal claims require recurring cash; or
- acquisition or equity financing is needed to support ordinary project growth.

The next filings should disclose backlog composition, project-loss provisions,
cost-to-complete changes, contract assets and liabilities, retainage, receivable
aging, equipment utilization, capex by type, debt and lease maturities, surety
capacity, segment margins, workforce productivity, and cash conversion.

## Evidence grade

- Control point: **B+** — self-perform labor, equipment, and project management
  are clearly disclosed, but customers and contract terms control demand.
- Operating economics: **B-** — 2025 was strong, while 2026 proves that backlog
  can coexist with severe project-level margin failure.
- Cash conversion: **B** — OCF is meaningful, but working-capital timing and
  equipment spending require a multi-year view.
- Balance-sheet resilience: **B** — debt reduction and cash improved, while
  bonding, leases, contract claims, and project losses remain relevant.
- Capital allocation: **B** — equipment spending is necessary, but returns by
  project cohort and the cost of capacity expansion need more evidence.
- Valuation: **B** — the current value requires normalized execution and cash,
  not merely record backlog.
