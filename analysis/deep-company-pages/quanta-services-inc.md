## Bottom line

Quanta is the field-execution layer of the electrification and infrastructure
cycle. It designs, engineers, procures, builds, repairs, and maintains
electric transmission and distribution, substations, generation, large-load
centers, communications, pipeline, and energy infrastructure. Its economic
control point is not a patented component; it is the ability to assemble
craft-skilled labor, equipment, project management, permitting knowledge, and
customer relationships on work that is difficult to coordinate and costly to
fail.

The strategic demand case is strong. 2025 revenue was $28.480 billion, and
June 2026 remaining performance obligations reached $33.55 billion, with
approximately 70% expected to be recognized within the following twelve
months. Q2 2026 revenue was $9.557 billion and first-half operating cash flow
was $1.487 billion.

The forensic case is more demanding. Quanta recognized 63.8% of 2025 revenue
over time using percentage-of-cost estimates. It held $1.534 billion of
contract assets and $411.1 million of unapproved change-order and claim
revenue at June 30, 2026. The company also spent $3.05 billion on acquisitions,
$609 million on capex, and $149 million on an integral equity investment in
2025. The result is a business with strong accounting earnings and backlog
visibility but a high reinvestment and estimation burden.

Current judgment: Quanta appears to have durable infrastructure relevance and
real execution capability, but its equity economics depend on project
estimate discipline, labor productivity, cash collection, acquisition returns,
and capital intensity. Backlog is valuable evidence of demand, not evidence
that the eventual margin or owner cash has already been earned.

## What the company actually controls

Quanta is a decentralized, labor-intensive infrastructure-solutions operator.
Its operating companies combine design, engineering, procurement,
construction, upgrade, repair, and maintenance services. The control point is
the execution system:

`customer need / permitted project -> engineering and procurement -> craft labor and fleet -> construction -> acceptance / collection -> maintenance relationship`

The company’s value rises when customers need a single accountable operator
for a complex, safety-critical project and when qualified labor, equipment,
and local execution capacity are scarce. Its burden rises at the same time:
Quanta must hire and retain craft workers, own or lease trucks and specialty
equipment, manage subcontractors, absorb weather and supply-chain disruption,
and estimate final costs before a fixed-price project is complete.

Quanta’s work is therefore closer to a portfolio of operating contracts than
to a simple recurring-revenue platform. The customer relationship can be
sticky, but project-level economics remain exposed to cost overruns, change
orders, claims, delays, safety events, and final acceptance.

The first read is attractive but capital-heavy:

- Revenue grew 20.3% in 2025, gross profit grew 21.8%, and operating income
  grew 19.7%.
- Operating margin remained approximately 5.7%. This is a high-volume,
  low-single-digit-margin execution model in which modest project-cost errors
  can materially affect equity earnings.
- Operating cash flow grew 7.1%, much more slowly than revenue and operating
  income, while capex remained above $600M.
- Net income attributable to common stock rose 13.7%, but adjusted EBITDA
  also adds back $181.9M of stock compensation and $94.1M of acquisition and
  integration costs, among other items.
- The 2025 cash-flow statement reports $2.230B of OCF, but cash acquisition
  payments were $3.052B, asset acquisitions were $103.4M, integral equity
  investments were $148.9M, and capex was $609.2M. After those listed uses,
  the screening residual was approximately **negative $1.684B** before
  financing, dividends, and share-related cash claims.

This does not mean acquisitions are maintenance spending. It means that the
headline free-cash-flow number is not the cash available after the capital
allocation program required to build the current company.

Management’s 2025 free-cash-flow framing was approximately $1.67B because it
starts with OCF, subtracts capex, and does not treat the acquisition program
as a deduction from free cash flow. That can be a useful operating measure,
but it is not an owner-cash measure for a company whose strategy repeatedly
uses acquisitions to add capabilities and revenue.

The company’s own liquidity discussion recognizes the timing problem. Cash
flows are affected when project costs must be paid before receivables are billed
and collected, and unpaid or delayed change orders and claims can reduce OCF.
The cash bridge must therefore be read alongside contract assets, retainage,
unbilled receivables, contract liabilities, and the acquisition ledger.

carried large unbilled and retainage balances. A favorable DSO does not prove
that every contract asset will convert without dispute or cost leakage.

## Earnings quality and accounting-risk tests

### 1. Percentage-of-completion accounting creates real estimate sensitivity

Approximately 63.8% of 2025 revenue was recognized as performance obligations
were satisfied over time, generally using costs incurred divided by total
estimated costs. This is normal for the business and is audited, but it means
reported gross profit depends on estimates of remaining labor, materials,
equipment, schedule, claims, and project completion.

Quanta explicitly lists negative estimate drivers: unknown site conditions,
scope disputes, permitting delays, material and skilled-labor costs,
third-party failures, engineering errors, weather, quality issues, rework, and
liquidated damages. A revised estimate can recognize previously earned profit,
reverse prior revenue/profit, or create a full contract loss once probable and
estimable.

Required tests:

- Compare current-period estimate changes with project completion and cash
  collections, not only reported margin.
- Separate approved change orders from unapproved claims and test subsequent
  approval, billing, and collection.
- Track gross-margin revisions by segment and project cohort where disclosed.
- Look for repeated favorable revisions during growth periods followed by
  late-period losses, impairments, or warranty/rework charges.
- Reconcile revenue recognized over time with contract assets, retainage,
  unbilled receivables, and contract liabilities.

The 2025 filing says estimate changes positively affected revenue by 0.2% in
2025, 0.2% in 2024, and 0.4% in 2023. That is not evidence of manipulation,
but the small percentage at company level can conceal large offsetting project
revisions.

### 2. Contract assets and claims are an important quality-of-earnings lens

At June 30, 2026, contract assets were $1.534B and contract liabilities were
$4.242B. Quanta had recognized $411.1M of revenue from unapproved change orders
and claims, down from $983.6M at December 31, 2025. The decrease was primarily
related to customer approval of a balance associated with a large Canadian
renewable-transmission project completed in 2024.

The reduction is encouraging, but it shows why the account matters. Contract
assets can represent earned but not yet billable economics; claims can
represent management’s estimate of additional consideration. The next filing
must show whether claims become cash, are written down, or are replaced by new
claims from newer projects.

Retainage adds another layer: year-end 2025 retainage was $994.1M expected
within one year and $228.7M expected later. At June 2026, retainage was $1.19B
within one year and $312.7M beyond one year. Customer acceptance, not merely
physical completion, is required for collection.

### 3. Contract liabilities are helpful liquidity, but not free profit

Contract liabilities increased from $3.258B at year-end 2025 to $4.242B at
June 30, 2026, primarily because of favorable billing terms on large projects
and, to a lesser extent, acquisitions. Quanta recognized $2.53B of revenue in
the first half of 2026 related to contract liabilities outstanding at the
prior year-end.

Advance and milestone billings can improve cash conversion and reduce funding
needs. They can also reverse as the company performs, so they should not be
capitalized as recurring owner cash without checking the full contract cycle.
The clean test is whether cash remains strong after contract liabilities stop
growing and large projects move from advance billing to completion.

### 4. Adjusted EBITDA contains economically recurring acquisition and equity costs

Quanta’s 2025 adjusted EBITDA reconciliation added $181.9M of non-cash stock
compensation and $94.1M of acquisition/integration costs, in addition to
depreciation, amortization, and other items. Management itself notes that
acquisition costs vary with acquisition activity and that contingent
consideration changes with post-acquisition performance.

Acquisition costs may be nonrecurring for one transaction, but the strategy is
recurring. Stock compensation is non-cash in the period but creates dilution,
tax-withholding cash payments, or future repurchase pressure. Amortization is
non-cash but reflects the price paid for customer relationships, backlog,
trade names, and other acquired intangibles.

The forensic conclusion is that adjusted EBITDA is useful for operating
comparison but insufficient for valuation until the acquisition cadence,
capital requirements, and dilution are charged against the economics.

## Acquisition economics and financial engineering

Quanta’s acquisition program is strategically coherent: it adds electrical,
mechanical, fabrication, right-of-way, engineering, and specialized execution
capabilities. It is also large enough to dominate owner cash.

In 2025, disclosed cash consideration included approximately $1.264B for
Dynamic Systems and $1.998B for other acquisitions, with additional common
stock and contingent consideration. At June 30, 2026, acquisitions completed
in the first half carried estimated total consideration of $1.430B: $1.086B
cash, $128.3M common stock, and $215.3M contingent consideration. Those deals
added $482.8M of goodwill and $635.2M of identifiable intangible assets,
including $464.7M of customer relationships and $86.2M of backlog.

The return question is not whether the acquired businesses add revenue. The
2025 filing reports $1.115B of revenue and $83.1M of pre-tax income from
businesses acquired in 2025, but that is not a standalone return on the full
purchase price and does not show the capital required to integrate and grow
them. The filing also reports post-acquisition measurement adjustments and
contingent consideration, which keep the final purchase price moving after
close.

Tests for the next filings:

- acquired revenue, gross profit, and cash contribution by acquisition cohort;
- incremental capex and working capital required by acquired businesses;
- contingent consideration payments versus original return assumptions;
- retention of acquired customers, labor, and management;
- goodwill and intangible headroom after margin or backlog changes;
- organic growth excluding acquired revenue and cross-selling claims.

## Capital structure and Lyn Alden durability lens

At December 31, 2025, Quanta had $439.5M of cash, $6.848B of combined
current and long-term debt, and $7.317B of goodwill. At June 30, 2026, cash was
$506.4M and debt was approximately $6.105B, before considering operating
leases, insurance and other non-current liabilities, and non-controlling
interests.

The company had $2.418B of available revolving commitments at year-end before
adding cash, and expects operating cash, cash on hand, borrowing capacity, and
capital-market access to fund requirements. That is adequate liquidity, but
the system remains dependent on credit access because annual acquisitions and
capex can exceed the cash left after operations.

Through the macro/liquidity lens:

- Quanta benefits from durable grid reliability, transmission, maintenance,
  load growth, and resilience spending—areas less discretionary than a single
  commercial construction cycle.
- The company is also exposed to financing conditions, utility rate bases,
  large-load-center timing, public policy, customer capital budgets, and
  contractor labor availability.
- Inflation can increase nominal project spending, but it can also raise wage,
  equipment, materials, insurance, and subcontractor costs faster than fixed
  contract pricing adjusts.
- A downturn can be dangerous because work in progress, retainage, claims,
  and equipment commitments remain while new awards slow.

Durability is therefore strongest in recurring repair and maintenance and
regulated or mission-critical infrastructure, and weaker where the backlog is
dependent on a concentrated, discretionary, or highly levered customer build.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers

### Narrative

The bullish narrative is that electricity demand, data centers,
electrification, grid modernization, domestic manufacturing, and resilience
spending create a multi-year infrastructure supercycle. Quanta’s scale,
craft-skilled workforce, operating-company network, and acquisition capability
make it a scarce execution platform.

### Operating translation

That narrative must become measurable in:

- organic revenue and gross-profit growth by segment;
- backlog and RPO conversion without margin deterioration;
- labor productivity, utilization, wage inflation, and safety performance;
- project estimate revisions, claims approval, retainage collection, and DSO;
- cash flow after capex, acquisitions, contingent consideration, and dilution;
- incremental return on acquired businesses;
- net leverage after the build cycle; and
- recurring maintenance and repair revenue versus one-time project work.

### Lifecycle and reinvestment

Quanta is a mature consolidator with a cyclical infrastructure growth overlay.
Its reinvestment rate is high: trucks, specialty equipment, facilities,
technology, working capital, acquisitions, and craft labor capacity all support
growth. The company may create substantial value if the return on that
reinvestment exceeds the cost of capital, but the return cannot be inferred
from RPO alone.

The lifecycle risk is that a strong market permits acquisitions at high
prices, raises labor and equipment costs, and encourages capacity expansion
before the work cycle is fully proven. A later normalization can expose the
difference between acquired revenue and acquired economic profit.

### Valuation expectations

An appropriate valuation should begin with normalized cash after a realistic
maintenance-capex charge and a recurring acquisition allowance. Management’s
adjusted FCF is a useful starting operating measure, not the final denominator.

| Case | Economic assumption | What must be proven |
|---|---|---|
| Bear | Backlog conversion slows, project margins normalize lower, and M&A remains necessary to sustain growth | OCF falls toward capex and debt-funded acquisitions remain the growth engine |
| Base | Grid and load-center work persists, project execution remains disciplined, and acquisitions moderate | OCF after capex becomes consistently positive after a full contract cycle |
| Bull | Quanta converts scarce execution capacity into higher-margin recurring work and reduces acquisition intensity | Incremental cash returns rise while leverage and dilution fall |

No valuation case should capitalize all RPO, treat claims as cash, add back
recurring acquisition costs, or ignore the replacement cost of fleet and
skilled labor capacity.

## Management incentives and capital allocation

Management incentives include revenue growth, adjusted EBITDA, adjusted EPS,
cash flow, backlog, and leverage. Those measures can align with long-term
value, but they also create predictable areas to examine: accelerating
project estimates, emphasizing adjusted earnings, using acquisition growth to
maintain headline expansion, and funding acquisitions through debt or stock.

2025 stock-based compensation was $181.9M, and the 2026 first-half filing
reports $126.0M. The first half also included $153.0M of cash tax-withholding
payments related to stock compensation. These are visible economic claims even
when excluded from EBITDA.

Quanta repurchased common stock in the prior-year period and continues to pay
dividends, while also pursuing large acquisitions. The capital-allocation
sequence should be tested as:

`maintain fleet and safety capacity -> fund profitable working capital -> reduce debt -> acquire at proven returns -> repurchase shares`

Buying stock while acquisitions and capex exceed operating cash can be
reasonable if the shares are deeply undervalued, but it increases financing
dependence and should not be called self-funded capital return without a full
cash bridge.

## Cross-sector position

Quanta sits downstream of equipment suppliers and upstream of the asset owner’s
operating infrastructure.

- Versus Vertiv, Quanta owns more field-labor and project-estimate risk but is
  closer to the actual grid and load-center build. Vertiv sells engineered
  equipment; Quanta converts equipment, labor, permits, and customer scope
  into an accepted operating asset.
- Versus WESCO, Quanta carries less stocked-inventory risk but more labor,
  safety, contract-estimate, and project-completion risk. WESCO can monetize
  procurement density; Quanta monetizes scarce execution capacity.
- Versus Sterling, Quanta has broader utility and energy exposure, while both
  show how backlog can grow faster than final cash economics if acquisitions,
  equipment, labor, and project timing are not separated.
- Versus utilities and asset owners, Quanta captures contractor and service
  economics without owning the regulated rate base, but it also lacks the
  asset owner’s long-lived contracted cash flow.

The cross-sector pattern is that infrastructure control can sit with the
execution intermediary, but the intermediary carries the hardest burden to
prove: the work must be safely completed at the estimated cost and collected
after acceptance.

## Thesis breakers and next-filing tests

### Bullish confirmation

- RPO and total backlog convert into revenue with stable or improving gross
  margins.
- Contract assets, unapproved claims, retainage, and DSO convert into cash
  without repeated reversals.
- OCF remains well above maintenance capex after contract liabilities stop
  expanding.
- Acquired businesses show post-close cash returns above the cost of capital.
- Net debt and diluted shares decline or remain controlled while growth
  continues.
- Craft labor productivity and safety remain strong as the work mix becomes
  more complex.

### Falsifiers

- Revenue and RPO rise while contract assets, retainage, and unbilled
  receivables grow faster or age materially.
- Favorable estimate revisions reverse into project losses, rework, or claims.
- Unapproved change orders remain large, recur, or fail to become approved cash.
- Backlog grows mainly through acquisitions or lower-margin work.
- Capex, equipment leases, and acquisition spending consume nearly all OCF.
- Goodwill and acquired intangibles rise faster than acquired cash earnings.
- Labor, insurance, safety, or equipment costs exceed contract pricing.
- Debt and stock dilution rise while management continues aggressive M&A or
  buybacks.

### Next filing fields

1. Revenue and gross profit by segment, contract type, and organic/acquired
   contribution.
2. RPO and backlog definitions, cancellations, concentration, and expected
   margin.
3. Contract assets, liabilities, unbilled receivables, retainage aging, and
   claims approval/collection.
4. Project estimate revisions and contract losses by period.
5. DSO, cash conversion, working-capital movements, and customer advances.
6. Fleet/equipment capex, leases, maintenance versus growth capital.
7. Acquisition cohort revenue, margin, cash return, contingent consideration,
   and integration cost.
8. Goodwill headroom and acquisition intangible amortization.
9. Stock compensation, withholding cash, diluted shares, and repurchases.
10. Net debt, interest cost, maturities, availability, and covenant headroom.

## Skeptical reader test

What would make this analysis wrong?

It would be too conservative if Quanta’s current acquisition and capex spend
is a temporary capacity build that produces a long period of higher-margin,
recurring maintenance and grid work, while contract liabilities and claims
convert cleanly into cash. The company may be rationally sacrificing current
owner cash to secure scarce execution capacity in a structurally undersupplied
market.

The bullish view would be wrong if backlog is mostly a visibility artifact,
large projects require repeated favorable estimates and customer advances to
support reported margins, and acquisitions merely replace the cash generated
by the legacy business. Quanta can be indispensable to the infrastructure
build and still deliver mediocre shareholder returns if the cost of labor,
equipment, financing, and acquired growth absorbs the relationship economics.
