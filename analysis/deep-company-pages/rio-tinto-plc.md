# Rio Tinto — iron-ore cash engine, critical-minerals rotation, and mega-project burden

As of 2026-09-13. FY2025 figures are filed IFRS figures in U.S. dollars;
dollar amounts are in millions unless otherwise stated.

## How to read this memo

Attributable profit is the portion that belongs to Rio Tinto owners after
non-controlling interests. Underlying EBITDA and underlying earnings exclude
selected items and are useful for operating comparison, but they are not owner
cash. Free cash flow is operating cash after property, plant, equipment,
intangible, and lease-principal spending, plus relevant asset-sale proceeds.
CuEq is copper-equivalent production, a way to combine different metals for an
operating comparison; it does not turn copper, iron ore, aluminium, and lithium
into one economic product.

## Investment question

Rio Tinto is a global mining and materials portfolio dominated by Pilbara iron
ore, with aluminium, copper, and lithium growth projects. Its possible advantage
is scale, logistics, long-lived assets, project capability, and the ability to
fund new materials from existing cash. The central question is whether
diversification improves through-cycle owner cash or simply adds large capital
projects, host-country obligations, environmental risk, and execution claims to
an iron-ore-dependent cash base.

Free cash flow fell to $4.025B in 2025 from $5.553B in 2024 and $7.657B in 2023
because growth, replacement, and sustaining capital increased. That is the key denominator for a diversified
miner: operating cash can be very high while common-owner cash remains modest
after the projects required to preserve and change the portfolio.

## What the annual and quarterly record reveals

- Pilbara iron ore remains the cash anchor, supported by mines, rail, ports,
  blending, shipping, and operating scale. Weather and logistics can interrupt
  sales even when mine production is strong.
- Copper, aluminium, and lithium are increasingly important to the growth story.
  In H1 2026 those businesses contributed more than half of underlying EBITDA,
  but that result remains sensitive to metal prices and project ramp-up.
- Oyu Tolgoi, Simandou, Western Range, and lithium projects create a large
  capital and schedule burden. First production or first ore is not the same as
  a completed project return; ramp rate, recovery, costs, partner funding, and
  final capital determine value.
- The $7.6B Arcadium acquisition changed the portfolio and increased net debt.
  It should be evaluated by the cash return from acquired lithium assets, not by
  the strategic label of critical minerals.
- Rio's ordinary dividend remains large, but 2025 free cash flow of $4.0B was
  below the $6.5B ordinary dividend. The difference was funded by balance-sheet
  capacity and other cash sources; this is not automatically unsafe, but it is
  a direct capital-allocation question.
- H1 2026 production growth and lower copper unit-cost guidance are encouraging,
  but they occur alongside more project capital and a changing commodity mix.
- Rio is both a mine owner and a logistics operator. The valuable relationship
  may be the entire export corridor, not just ore in the ground. That also means
  ports, rail, power, weather, host governments, and customers carry economic
  importance.

## Forensic earnings-quality questions

### 1. How much cash comes from iron ore prices?

Rebuild revenue and operating cash by price, volume, grade, recovery, freight,
currency, royalties, taxes, and logistics. Test the portfolio at mid-cycle iron
ore, copper, aluminium, and lithium prices.

### 2. Is free cash flow after the real renewal burden?

Reconcile the $4.0B FCF measure to operating cash, growth, replacement,
sustaining, intangible, lease, environmental, closure, and exploration spending.
Include partner funding and the capital required by non-controlled entities.
Production without reserve replacement and closure funding is not durable owner
cash.

### 3. Does diversification create returns?

For each copper, lithium, aluminium, and iron-ore project, record capital spent,
partner share, start date, ramp, unit cost, realized price, cash return, and
revised budget. Compare the return with the value of returning cash or buying
existing assets.

### 4. Are partners funding or merely sharing claims?

Identify joint operations, equity-accounted units, non-controlling interests,
shareholder loans, and project debt. Cash received from partners can reduce Rio's
funding, but future distributions and control rights determine what belongs to
Rio owners.

### 5. Are reserves and closure obligations visible?

Track depletion, reserve replacement, grades, recovery, mine life, rehabilitation,
asset-retirement obligations, tailings, water, and community commitments. A
large reserve base is not a cash flow until it is permitted, funded, built, and
operated at an acceptable return.

### 6. Are dividends covered through the cycle?

Compare the ordinary dividend with mid-cycle FCF after replacement and
sustaining capital, project funding, debt, taxes, and environmental claims.
Separate a policy payout ratio from cash actually available in the year.

## Incentives, portfolio allocation, and cohort position

Rio's scale and diversification can create an incentive to describe a large
project pipeline as strategic progress before the project has earned a return.
The acquisition of Arcadium, the expansion of copper and lithium, and the
continued dividend all compete for cash with Pilbara renewal, rehabilitation,
debt reduction, and project completion. A larger portfolio is not automatically
more resilient if its incremental capital earns less than the cash returned or
the debt used to fund it.

The correct management scorecard is cash per diluted share after sustaining and
replacement capital, partner claims, taxes, closure obligations, acquisitions,
and debt. For each project, compare the approved capital budget and expected
return with the eventual capital, ramp, output, and cash. For each dividend or
buyback, compare the payout with mid-cycle cash rather than the year's iron-ore
or copper price.

Rio belongs in the [diversified-versus-focused mining comparison](../cross-sector/diversified-miner-versus-focused-miner-forensic-comparison-2026-09-13.md)
and the [commodity scarcity and embedded service first-principles essay](../first-principles/commodity-scarcity-embedded-service-and-owner-cash.md).
Compared with Kinross and Newmont, Rio has greater logistics and commodity
diversity but a larger project and partner burden. Compared with Wheaton and
Franco-Nevada, it controls more of the physical production system and therefore
carries depletion, labor, energy, closure, and host-country risk directly.

## Lyn Alden / Damodaran / forensic synthesis

- **Lyn Alden lens:** Rio links industrial demand, energy transition, trade,
  geopolitics, weather, ports, and host-country policy. Scale improves access to
  capital and logistics, but large projects and net debt reduce flexibility when
  commodity prices fall.
- **Damodaran lens:** value Pilbara iron ore, aluminium, copper, lithium, and
  project options separately. Use mid-cycle prices, production decline, reserve
  replacement, sustaining capital, project probabilities, partner ownership,
  taxes, and debt.
- **Forensic lens:** the central risks are treating underlying EBITDA as common
  cash, treating first production as project success, treating partner funding
  as free cash, and treating a 60% payout ratio as dividend coverage when FCF is
  below the dividend.

## Valuation framing

The 2026-09-11 market snapshot was approximately $162.31B of equity value at
$99.96 per ADR. Against FY2025 free cash flow of $4.0B, the equity value is
about 40.6x. A normalized FCF range of $6B / $10B / $14B at 12x / 15x / 18x
implies $72B / $150B / $252B. The current value requires stronger cash than
2025, durable project returns, and a credible shift toward copper, aluminium,
and lithium without a proportionate increase in capital burden or debt.

## Thesis breakers and next checks

1. Iron-ore prices normalize while Pilbara costs, royalties, and logistics stay
   high.
2. Oyu Tolgoi, Simandou, lithium, or other projects require more capital or
   produce less cash than planned.
3. Copper, aluminium, or lithium growth increases reported production without
   improving per-share owner cash.
4. Partner funding, affiliate dividends, or project distributions are delayed
   or unavailable to Rio owners.
5. Environmental, community, permitting, weather, or host-country obligations
   increase the renewal burden.
6. Dividends and acquisitions continue while FCF remains below capital-return
   requirements and net debt rises.
