## Bottom line

S&P Global owns financial decision infrastructure: ratings, indices, commodity
prices, market intelligence, and data workflows that make financing and
portfolio decisions standardized and defensible. It is closer to a paid
standard and judgment layer than to a bank. Its cleaner economics are real,
but they are not frictionless: ratings revenue is issuance-sensitive, data
products require technology and talent, indices share economics with partners,
acquisitions reshape the portfolio, and credibility/regulatory failure can
destroy the moat.

FY2025 revenue was $15.336B, operating income was $6.478B, net income was
$4.471B, and operating cash flow was $5.651B. The filing shows $195M of capex
and $2.023B of acquisitions, producing approximately **$3.433B after those
listed uses**. That bridge excludes $1.549B of disposition proceeds, $321M of
distributions to noncontrolling interests, debt movements, and the substantial
$5.001B share-repurchase program.

## The control point

`issuer / investor / commodity participant -> trusted rating, benchmark, price,
index, or data workflow -> financing, allocation, hedging, and monitoring
decision`

The scarce asset is not simply a database. It is the market's willingness to
coordinate around S&P's reference points. Ratings affect issuance and mandates;
indices determine benchmark exposure and licensing; commodity assessments
inform contracts; and Market Intelligence products become embedded in analyst,
portfolio, and corporate workflows. Once a reference point is embedded, the
switching cost includes governance, compliance, model changes, and reputation.

## Current-quarter quality check

Q2 2026 revenue was $4.146B, and the company reported adjusted diluted EPS
growth of 23% with adjusted operating-profit margin up 200 basis points. These
figures support continued operating momentum, but they do not remove the
denominator questions in the annual filing. The next quarterly bridge should
separate Ratings issuance and surveillance, recurring Market Intelligence and
Energy subscriptions, index asset-linked fees, acquisition contribution, and
the effect of the Mobility portfolio transition.

The important test is whether the quarter's margin improvement survives after
With Intelligence integration, restructuring, technology and data investment,
noncontrolling interests, stock compensation, and debt. A strong adjusted
quarter is evidence of current execution; it is not by itself evidence that
the $5.001B buyback program was funded by durable post-acquisition cash.

The 2025 filing reports external revenue and segment operating profit as
follows:

|---|---:|---:|---|

| Ratings | $4.549B | $3.013B | Issuance cycle, surveillance, CLO/corporate volumes, pricing, legal, and regulatory credibility |
| Energy | $2.299B | $0.943B | Commodity benchmark relevance, data subscriptions, pricing, and energy-cycle demand |
| Mobility | $1.747B | $0.378B | Portfolio separation, auto-data demand, stranded cost, and eventual standalone economics |
| Indices | $1.839B | $1.271B | Asset-linked fees, derivative activity, data subscriptions, licensing, and market levels |

The largest quality distinction is between recurring and transaction-linked
revenue. Ratings transaction revenue and index asset-linked fees can surge
with issuance and market levels; surveillance, subscriptions, benchmark
licensing, and workflow products are more durable but still require trust and
renewal. The next filings should disclose enough mix and retention detail to
test whether the margin is being carried by durable franchises or a favorable
capital-markets cycle.

## Earnings quality and financial-engineering risks

### Acquisition and disposition asymmetry

S&P spent $2.023B on acquisitions while receiving $1.549B from dispositions.
The net cash burden looks modest only if the sale proceeds are allowed to
offset the purchases. That is useful capital allocation analysis but not
recurring owner cash. With Intelligence, Visible Alpha, and prior portfolio
transactions should be evaluated on incremental fee revenue, margin, retention,
and cash return rather than on adjusted EPS accretion.

### Restructuring and “one-time” costs

The filing reports a $157M pre-tax 2025 restructuring charge, including $85M
of reserve at year-end, alongside segment-level legal and severance items.
Restructuring may improve the cost base, but recurring restructurings can make
adjusted margins overstate steady-state operating expense. The test is cash
spent versus realized savings, headcount and service quality, and whether the
program reappears after each acquisition or separation.

### Ratings legal and incentive exposure

Ratings profitability is exceptionally high, but the economic moat depends on
credibility. Legal costs were $42M in the Ratings segment in 2025, and the
business faces conflicts, regulation, issuer concentration, methodology risk,
and the possibility that customers or regulators reduce reliance on ratings.
The incentive test is whether management is rewarded for transaction volume,
adjusted operating profit, AUM/index fees, or durable ratings quality and cash
per share.

### Share repurchases and noncontrolling interests

S&P repurchased $5.001B of common stock and paid $1.170B of common dividends in
2025, while paying $321M to noncontrolling interests. Those distributions are
large relative to the $3.433B residual after capex and acquisitions. The
buybacks may be value-creating if ratings and index cash are durable, but they
also raise the hurdle for debt and capital allocation: shareholders should not
confuse a shrinking share count with value creation if the company overpays or
funds returns with leverage and asset sales.

### Balance-sheet burden

Long-term debt was $13.088B and cash was $1.745B. Goodwill was $36.475B and
net intangible assets excluding goodwill were $16.271B. The balance sheet is
not a bank balance sheet, but it is acquisition-heavy and sensitive to
impairment, interest cost, integration execution, and the value of acquired
data franchises. Accounts receivable rose to $3.441B from $2.867B while revenue
rose 8%; collection and contract timing deserve monitoring.

## Alden macro/liquidity lens

S&P benefits from institutional complexity, regulation, debt issuance,
passive investing, commodity risk, and the demand for defensible external
judgment. Higher rates can increase ratings and refinancing demand, but they
can also suppress issuance, valuations, M&A, and asset-linked index fees. A
credit contraction tests Ratings transaction revenue directly.

Its liquidity profile is stronger than a lender's because it does not fund a
large loan book or absorb ordinary credit losses. The risk migrates into
reputation, technology, data integrity, regulatory permissions, legal exposure,
and acquisition financing. In a stress scenario, recurring surveillance,
indices, and subscriptions may cushion the cycle, but a multi-year issuance
drought would still expose the high-margin model.

## Damodaran lens

The proper model separates the company into economic engines:

`recurring data/subscription/benchmark fees + normalized ratings issuance and
surveillance + indices asset-linked/licensing economics + Energy/Mobility
portfolio cash - technology/talent/regulatory costs - acquisitions - debt,
NCI, restructuring, and dilution`

The lifecycle question is whether S&P is still reinvesting at high incremental
returns or using acquisitions and buybacks to mask mature organic growth. A
premium multiple is justified by durable renewal, pricing, benchmark centrality,
and high returns on data/technology investment. It is not justified by a single
strong issuance year, a disposition gain, or adjusted margin expansion after
recurring restructuring.

S&P belongs in the [standards, ratings, and clearing-infrastructure
comparison](../cross-sector/standards-ratings-versus-clearing-infrastructure-forensic-comparison-2026-09-13.md)
and the first-principles essay on [software, process control, and physical
compute](../first-principles/software-process-control-and-physical-compute.md).
It captures payment for an accepted reference point without funding a normal
loan book, but it carries credibility, data integrity, regulatory, technology,
acquisition, and issuance-cycle risk.

## Valuation and thesis breakers

At the September 11, 2026 market snapshot, SPGI was approximately $410.71 per
share with an equity value near $121.365B. The market is valuing S&P as a
trusted information platform with durable cash conversion.

| Case | Normalized owner cash | Multiple | Implied equity value |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Bear | $3.0B | 28x | $84.0B |
| Base | $4.0B | 32x | $128.0B |
| Bull | $5.0B | 36x | $180.0B |

The base case is close to the market value, so the investment debate is mostly
about durability and reinvestment rather than a simple cheapness screen.

Thesis breakers:

- ratings issuance and transaction revenue remain weak while surveillance and
  subscription growth cannot protect margin;
- index asset-linked fees or benchmark licensing lose pricing power or face
  credible substitutes;
- data subscriptions fail to renew after acquisitions, or acquired businesses
  require repeated restructuring and dilution;
- accounts receivable and deferred revenue indicate weaker collection or
  contract quality than reported growth suggests;
- ratings credibility, methodology, cyber resilience, or regulatory standing is
  impaired; or
- debt-funded repurchases and dispositions support per-share results while
  normalized owner cash declines.

These are forensic reconciliation questions, not allegations of misconduct.
S&P's moat may be stronger than a typical data vendor, but the price requires
the market to keep treating its reference points as indispensable.
