## Bottom line

Sterling is a specialty-construction and site-execution platform moving toward
mission-critical e-infrastructure. It prepares sites, installs utilities and
electrical systems, and executes transportation and building work. Its current
control point is the combination of site-development knowledge, electrical
capability, project management, and local labor that allows data centers,
semiconductor facilities, and advanced manufacturing campuses to become usable.

The demand signal is unusually visible. 2025 revenue was $2.490B, operating
income $405.9M, net income attributable to common stockholders $290.2M, and
operating cash flow $440.0M. Signed backlog was $3.01B and combined backlog
$3.31B at year-end. By Q2 2026, signed backlog reached $4.33B and combined
backlog $5.62B; mission-critical work represented 92% of E-Infrastructure
backlog according to the local quarterly packet.

The skeptical read is equally clear. CEC added electrical and mechanical
capability through a $562M acquisition, including $443M cash and $79M stock,
with up to $80M of earn-out consideration. In 2025, cash acquisitions were
$482.3M while capex was $77.3M. The company also changed the accounting for a
joint venture, moving RHB from consolidation to equity-method treatment in
2025. Sterling therefore has a strong backlog story, but it must prove organic
growth, acquired capability, contract cash conversion, and sustainable margins
separately.

Current judgment: Sterling is a high-conviction physical-capacity case, not yet
a clean owner-cash case. Signed backlog is meaningful evidence of demand;
combined backlog and future phases are weaker evidence; adjusted EBITDA and
adjusted EPS require reconciliation to acquisition cash, capex, working capital,
earn-outs, noncontrolling interests, and stock compensation.

### Q2 2026 call cross-check: visibility is layered, not equivalent

The Q2 call adds management's own prioritization claim: Sterling said it was
being selective rather than chasing every available project, while reporting
$4.3B of signed backlog, $5.6B of combined backlog, and more than $1.4B of
high-probability future phases. This strengthens the demand and customer-capital
visibility read, but it does not upgrade future phases to contracted revenue.
The forensic implication is sharper: the call's confidence must be tested against
conversion, project margin, contract capital, and acquisition cash—not against
the headline $7B-plus opportunity pool.

## What the company actually controls

Sterling reports three operating segments:

- **E-Infrastructure Solutions:** site development and electrical/mechanical
  services for data centers, semiconductor, manufacturing, and other complex
  facilities.
- **Transportation Solutions:** heavy highway, roadway, and related civil work.
- **Building Solutions:** residential and building-related construction exposed
  to housing affordability and homebuilder demand.

The execution chain is:

`land / customer capex -> award -> estimate and contract -> site, utility, and electrical work -> measured progress -> billing / collection -> next campus phase`

The valuable relationship sits with project owners, developers, hyperscalers,
manufacturers, and general contractors. The scarcity is not simply dirt-moving
equipment. It is the ability to coordinate site conditions, permitting,
subcontractors, labor, electrical integration, schedule, safety, and customer
acceptance. The company can capture more value when it bundles site and
electrical work, but it also inherits more execution and estimate risk.

The reported picture needs several adjustments:

- Revenue grew 17.7% in 2025, gross profit 34.3%, and operating income 53.4%.
- OCF declined 11.5% despite higher operating income and adjusted EBITDA. The
  gap is a direct reminder that project growth and accounting earnings do not
  automatically become cash.
- 2025 operating income included $15.9M of equity-method earnings from the
  50%-owned RHB subsidiary, including $8.6M of basis-step-up depreciation and
  amortization. In 2024, RHB's members' share was treated as a $20.9M expense.
  This accounting change complicates year-over-year operating comparisons.
- E-Infrastructure 2025 revenue was approximately $1.467B and operating income
  $346.0M, versus $923.7M and $203.4M in 2024. The segment now drives the
  consolidated margin profile, but its improvement included a $19.4M CEC
  contribution net of $3.0M intangible amortization.
- Stock-based compensation rose from $14.6M in 2023 to $24.2M in 2025. It is
  non-cash in the current period but remains an owner claim when dilution or
  repurchases are considered.

The negative residual is not proof that CEC was a bad acquisition. It shows that
Sterling's 2025 growth program consumed more cash than current-period operating
cash after capex. The company may earn attractive returns later, but the
valuation must explicitly model the acquired revenue, margin, integration costs,
working capital, earn-out, and post-close cash contribution.

The financing burden was also visible. Sterling repurchased $74.2M of stock,
paid $40.1M to noncontrolling-interest owners, paid $21.0M of withholding taxes
on equity awards, and repaid $24.9M of debt in 2025. Share consideration for
acquisitions was $79.5M, so acquisition funding is not purely a cash question;
it also affects dilution and per-share economics.

OCF was supported by changes in “Contract Capital,” including receivables, net
contracts in progress, and accounts payable. The annual filing says project mix
and the percentage of work completed can cause working-capital requirements to
vary significantly. That makes a single-year OCF multiple unreliable until the
full contract cycle is observed.

## Earnings quality and accounting-risk tests

### Percentage-of-completion estimates

Sterling recognizes much project revenue over time. The company estimates total
revenue, cost, gross profit, incentives, delay penalties, and change orders as a
contract progresses. Nearer completion, visibility improves; early-period
profit can therefore be revised as labor, material, schedule, and site
conditions become clearer.

The appropriate forensic test is a project-cohort roll-forward:

- compare original gross-margin estimates with final margins;
- track favorable and unfavorable estimate changes;
- separate approved change orders from unapproved claims;
- compare contract assets and retainage with collected cash;
- test whether job losses, rework, or warranty costs appear late; and
- track whether newly acquired projects carry different estimate behavior.

The filing's accounting policy explicitly states that management judgments are
required for revenue and earnings from construction contracts, goodwill, and
purchase accounting. This is normal for the industry, but it is the area where
headline adjusted earnings can be least informative.

### Backlog hierarchy

Sterling defines signed backlog as the unearned revenue expected from executed
contracts. It excludes unsigned awards until contracts are executed. Combined
backlog adds unsigned awards, while future-phase opportunities are a still
weaker visibility layer. A project can also be canceled, terminated, or have
scope adjusted, and backlog may not represent a contractual right to the total
displayed revenue.

The evidence hierarchy is therefore:

`signed backlog > unsigned awards / combined backlog > high-probability future phases > management pipeline`

At year-end 2025, signed backlog was $3.01B and combined backlog $3.31B, with
broader visible work approaching $4.5B. By Q2 2026, signed backlog was $4.33B,
combined backlog $5.62B, and future-phase opportunities exceeded $1.4B. The
spread between those layers is not revenue and should not receive the same
valuation weight.

### Joint-venture deconsolidation

Beginning January 1, 2025, Sterling no longer consolidated RHB's results and
instead used equity-method accounting. RHB revenue and backlog therefore no
longer appear in Sterling's consolidated totals, while Sterling records its
share of income and distributions separately. This creates a potential optical
break in growth and backlog comparisons. The correct analysis reconciles 2024
reported figures to a comparable basis before concluding that 2025 growth was
organic or acquired.

### Acquisition and goodwill risk

CEC's $562M purchase price consisted primarily of $443M cash and $79M stock,
with up to $80M of operating-income-based earn-out payments. The purchase-price
allocation included approximately $227.8M of identifiable intangible assets and
$308.9M goodwill in the acquisition table. Consolidated year-end goodwill was
approximately $585M and intangibles $555M.

This does not establish overpayment. It establishes a return hurdle. Sterling
must earn enough incremental after-tax cash to cover the cash price, dilution,
intangible amortization, working capital, integration, and earn-outs. Goodwill
impairment is a lagging indicator; a project or acquisition can underperform
long before an impairment is recorded.

## Balance-sheet resilience and liquidity

Sterling ended 2025 with $390.7M of cash and cash equivalents, down from
$664.2M in 2024. Total current assets were approximately $1.034B, including
$501.2M of accounts receivable and $101.2M of contract assets. Net property and
equipment was $278.3M, investment in the unconsolidated subsidiary $105.8M,
goodwill $585.2M, and identifiable intangible assets approximately $555M.

The company has credit-facility capacity and uses borrowing for working capital,
capex, and acquisitions. The credit agreement restricts additional debt,
acquisitions, dividends, repurchases, liens, and other actions. Liquidity is
therefore adequate only if project collections, contract funding, and lender
capacity remain aligned. The 2025 cash decline after a strong earnings year is
the most important balance-sheet signal to carry into the next filing.

## Lyn Alden lens: physical demand versus liquidity sensitivity

Sterling benefits from durable physical requirements: data-center power and
site preparation, semiconductor reshoring, advanced manufacturing, and utility
infrastructure. It is closer to the physical bottleneck than a software vendor,
but its demand still depends on customer capex budgets, project financing,
interest rates, policy support, land, power availability, and labor.

The macro chain is:

`enterprise / public capex -> signed award -> labor and materials -> project progress -> billing -> cash collection`

E-Infrastructure may be structurally stronger than transportation or housing,
but concentration increases cycle risk. Transportation revenue declined 20% in
Q2 2026 as management reallocated resources toward higher-margin work. Building
Solutions remains exposed to housing affordability. A weaker capex environment
could therefore hit the strongest segment after Sterling has already reduced
diversification in lower-margin lanes.

## Damodaran lens: narrative-to-numbers and lifecycle

The narrative is “Sterling is a scarce execution partner for mission-critical
campuses.” The valuation translation is:

`signed profitable backlog -> recognized revenue -> project margin -> working-capital cash -> maintenance capex -> acquisition and labor reinvestment`

The company is in a high-growth/mix-transition phase. A Damodaran-style model
should not carry 2025 E-Infrastructure margins or Q2 2026 adjusted EBITDA
straight into perpetuity. It should separately model:

- CEC acquired growth versus same-store E-Infrastructure growth;
- signed backlog conversion versus unsigned awards and future phases;
- project-margin stability as labor and subcontractor capacity tightens;
- capex for equipment, facilities, and project-specific requirements;
- working capital when contract liabilities stop growing;
- RHB equity-method earnings and distributions;
- earn-outs, goodwill, intangible amortization, and integration costs; and
- stock consideration, SBC, repurchases, and diluted per-share returns.

The key incremental-return question is whether bundling site and electrical work
creates enough pricing power and productivity to earn high returns after CEC's
purchase price and the capital needed to staff the work.

## Management incentives and financial engineering

The operating incentives likely reward revenue, adjusted EBITDA, adjusted EPS,
backlog, segment mix, acquisitions, and shareholder return. These can align
with owners, but they create predictable presentation risks:

- backlog layers can make future-phase opportunity look closer to contracted
  revenue than it is;
- cost-to-cost accounting can pull profit forward before final costs are known;
- adjusted EBITDA removes amortization and acquisition costs that may recur as
  Sterling continues its roll-up strategy;
- joint-venture deconsolidation can alter reported revenue and backlog without
  changing the underlying economic relationship; and
- share-funded acquisitions and repurchases can make adjusted EPS growth look
  stronger than total owner returns.

The evidence supports these as diligence questions, not misconduct allegations.
The strongest proof is a cash-based acquisition cohort and project-estimate
roll-forward.

## Valuation and reverse-valuation questions

No price target is assigned. The market must prove that:

1. Signed backlog converts into revenue and cash with stable project margins.
2. The 92% mission-critical mix in E-Infrastructure remains profitable after
   labor, equipment, and customer-concentration risks.
3. CEC produces after-tax cash returns above the blended cost of capital after
   cash consideration, stock dilution, amortization, and earn-outs.
4. Contract liabilities and customer funding remain available after backlog
   growth normalizes.
5. The company can reduce transportation exposure without creating excessive
   dependence on one hyperscale, semiconductor, or manufacturing cycle.
6. OCF remains strong after capex, acquisition cadence, working capital, NCI,
   and share-related claims.

## Filing-based thesis breakers

The thesis weakens if future filings show:

- signed backlog fails to convert while combined backlog and future phases keep
  growing;
- gross margin declines as mission-critical projects are executed;
- contract assets, retainage, claims, or unbilled receivables rise faster than
  collected cash;
- OCF remains below net income while acquisitions and adjusted EBITDA rise;
- CEC contribution is low relative to purchase price, earn-out, goodwill, and
  intangible amortization;
- E-Infrastructure concentration creates customer or project dependence;
- transportation and building weakness become larger than the mix benefit; or
- RHB equity-method earnings and deconsolidation effects obscure comparable
  organic growth.

## Final judgment

Sterling controls a valuable physical relationship at the site-development and
electrical-integration layer of the AI and industrial buildout. Its best
economics may come from being one accountable operator across site and power
systems, while customers and project owners carry the larger facility capital
burden. Sterling itself carries the labor, equipment, estimate, working-capital,
acquisition, and financing burdens needed to make that relationship valuable.

The clean thesis is a durable shift toward mission-critical e-infrastructure
with higher-margin bundled execution. The skeptical thesis is that acquired
capability, layered backlog definitions, adjusted earnings, joint-venture
accounting, and customer-funded working capital make the current transition
look cleaner than eventual owner cash. The next filing should be read first for
backlog conversion, project-margin outcomes, CEC cash returns, contract assets,
and cash after acquisition capital—not for another headline visibility number.
