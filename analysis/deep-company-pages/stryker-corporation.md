# Stryker Corporation — procedure-platform medtech under forensic review

Research date: 2026-09-13
Evidence window: FY2025 and Q1/Q2 2026
Ticker: SYK
Evidence grade: A for reported financials, cash flows, and segment growth; B for the durability of acquisition returns, adjusted-EPS normalization, robotics/platform economics, and cyber/operational resilience.

## Bottom line

Stryker controls a valuable hospital and surgeon workflow through implants,
instruments, MedSurg equipment, neurotechnology, robotics, and enabling
technologies. Procedure-room presence and surgeon preference can create durable
switching costs, while a broad portfolio lets the company cross-sell into
capital budgets and recurring procedure demand.

The forensic catch is the scale of reinvestment required to keep compounding.
FY2025 operating cash flow was $5.044B, but acquisitions consumed $4.960B and
PP&E capex consumed $761M. Cash after both was negative before dividends,
interest, debt service, and other investing. Reported EPS was $8.40 versus
adjusted EPS of $13.63, with $170M of goodwill/other impairments, $173M of
inventory step-up expense, $243M of stock compensation, and substantial
amortization and deferred-tax movements in the cash bridge. This is not proof
of accounting abuse; it is a direct warning that “high-quality medtech” must be
underwritten through post-acquisition cash returns, not adjusted EPS alone.

Related first-principles explanation: [Procedure MedTech, clinical adoption, and owner cash](../first-principles/procedure-medtech-clinical-adoption-and-owner-cash.md).

## Control point and operating model

Stryker sells in approximately 61 countries and impacts more than 150 million
patients annually through products in MedSurg, Neurotechnology, and
Orthopaedics. Its economic control point is the procedure ecosystem: hospitals
buy equipment and instruments, surgeons develop preferences, and implants,
consumables, service, and software-like enabling tools reinforce the installed
workflow.

The moat is stronger than a single product patent but more operationally
dependent than a pure recurring subscription. Hospitals face switching costs in
training, inventory, sterilization, clinical familiarity, capital budgets, and
workflow disruption. Stryker still has to maintain quality, regulatory
compliance, product availability, cyber resilience, and a large sales/service
organization.

FY2025 net sales were $25.116B. The cash-flow series improved strongly, but the
cash result was largely redeployed into acquisitions. The 2025 acquisition
outlay was more than three times the 2024 level and was the dominant economic
variable in the year. The correct question is whether the acquired businesses
will generate incremental operating profit and cash sufficient to justify that
price, integration burden, and future amortization.

## Cash conversion and working capital

Reported OCF of $5.044B looked strong, but its bridge included $732M of
intangible amortization, $170M of goodwill/other impairments, $243M of stock
compensation, $173M of inventory stepped up to fair value at acquisition, and
$392M of deferred-income-tax expense. Those are valid GAAP cash-flow
adjustments, but they show why OCF is not automatically recurring owner cash.

The FY2025 investment bridge was:

- OCF: $5.044B
- Acquisitions: $4.960B
- PP&E capex: $761M
- Other investing, net: approximately $60M outflow, partly offset by $165M
  proceeds from the Spinal Implants business sale and $750M of short-term
  investment proceeds
- Approximate OCF less acquisitions and capex: negative $677M before the other
  investing and financing stack

Inventory increased $297M as a use of cash in 2025, after a $206M use in 2024
and $797M use in 2023. Accounts receivable supplied $127M of cash in 2025,
which may reflect collections/timing rather than a permanent improvement. The
next review should separate procedure growth from inventory build, acquisition
fair-value step-up, and deferred tax effects.

## Earnings quality and financial engineering

The $5.23 gap between reported and adjusted 2025 EPS deserves line-by-line
attention. Stryker’s adjusted presentation excludes or adjusts for selected
acquisition/integration costs, amortization and other items affecting
comparability. Some are genuinely noncash or transaction-specific; others can
recur whenever the company’s strategy depends on acquisitions.

Specific quality checks:

- Intangible amortization was $732M in the cash-flow reconciliation. It is
  noncash in the current period, but it represents the economic consumption of
  acquired customer relationships and technology.
- Inventory step-up at acquisition was $173M. This depresses post-acquisition
  GAAP gross margin as stepped-up inventory sells and should not be dismissed
  if acquisitions are a recurring growth engine.
- Goodwill and other impairments were $170M in 2025, versus $977M in 2024.
  The lower current charge does not prove that prior acquisition prices were
  sound; it makes future impairment testing a relevant falsifier.
- Stock compensation was $243M, up from $229M in 2024 and $205M in 2023. It
  is material enough to include in per-share and owner-cash analysis.
- Deferred tax expense was $392M in 2025 after a $370M benefit in 2024. Tax
  timing and acquisition-related basis differences materially affect the cash
  bridge.

No fraud conclusion is established. The risk is financial-engineering by
normalization: if the investor treats amortization, integration, inventory
step-up, recurring acquisition costs, and stock compensation as permanently
irrelevant, the economics become overstated.

## Balance sheet, capital allocation, and resilience

Stryker issued $2.979B of long-term debt and repaid $1.400B in 2025. It paid
$1.284B of dividends and $149M of taxes withheld from stock awards. The company
ended with $4.011B of cash, but this was alongside a large acquisition program
and significant debt financing. The capital structure is resilient enough to
support the platform, yet its resilience depends on acquisition cash returns,
not just procedure demand.

Cyber resilience is a genuine operating variable. The Q1 2026 cyber incident
affected operations and reported EPS, while Q2 showed recovery with sales of
about $6.6B, reported EPS of $3.30, and adjusted EPS of $3.69. Future filings
should disclose whether the incident caused lost sales, incremental remediation
costs, customer-service disruption, or lasting changes to system investment.

## Alden lens: healthcare durability and liquidity

Orthopaedic procedures, trauma, aging populations, hospital efficiency, and
replacement cycles create a more durable demand floor than discretionary
consumer spending. Robotics and enabling technologies can deepen the platform
relationship if they improve clinical workflow and hospital economics.

The macro caveat is that hospitals operate under labor, reimbursement, capital-
budget, and liquidity constraints. Procedure volume can be durable while
capital equipment purchases are delayed. The Alden question is whether Stryker
controls an unavoidable procedure bottleneck, or whether the company is
temporarily benefiting from post-pandemic procedure normalization and a strong
acquisition cycle.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers

Stryker’s story must be translated into:

1. Procedure-volume growth, price, mix, new-product adoption, and acquisition
   contribution separately.
2. Segment margins after amortization, inventory step-up, cyber remediation,
   quality/regulatory costs, and recurring integration expense.
3. Reinvestment in R&D, sales/service, PP&E, inventory, regulatory systems,
   cyber infrastructure, and acquisitions.
4. Incremental ROIC by acquisition cohort and platform, not only consolidated
   adjusted EPS growth.
5. A competitive-advantage period based on surgeon preference, installed
   workflow, clinical evidence, and hospital switching costs.

At the September 11, 2026 market snapshot, SYK was approximately $275.56 per
share with equity value of about $106.4B. Against FY2025 OCF less PP&E capex of
$4.283B, the equity value implies a substantial premium for future growth. If
acquisition spending is treated as recurring reinvestment, FY2025 owner cash
was negative after the major portfolio purchases. The valuation therefore
requires strong incremental returns, not merely continued adjusted-EPS growth.

### Valuation screen

The scenario register uses approximately `$106.4B` of equity value and applies
the following owner-cash cases:

| Case | Normalized owner-cash screen | Applied multiple | Implied equity value | What must be true |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Bear | `$2.5B` | `25x` | `$62.5B` | Procedure growth slows, acquisition spending remains high, and acquired cash returns do not offset amortization, integration, and debt claims |
| Base | `$4.0B` | `30x` | `$120.0B` | Organic procedure demand remains durable, acquisition cohorts earn acceptable returns, and cash after capex improves without excessive dilution |
| Bull | `$5.5B` | `35x` | `$192.5B` | Stryker sustains strong organic growth, robotics and platform adoption expand margins, and the acquisition engine compounds cash returns above its capital cost |

The market snapshot falls between the bear and base cases. That makes the
valuation less demanding than Intuitive Surgical's screen, but it still
requires a credible path from the current acquisition-heavy cash profile to
roughly `$4.0B` or more of recurring common-owner cash. The decisive evidence
is post-deal cash return, not adjusted EPS alone. This is a scenario screen,
not a price target.

## Incentives and thesis breakers

The proxy review should connect executive rewards to organic growth, operating
margin, ROIC, cash conversion, quality/regulatory performance, and acquisition
returns. A scorecard dominated by adjusted EPS or relative TSR could encourage
acquisition pace and buyback optics without sufficient post-deal cash proof.

The thesis weakens if:

- procedure growth slows while capital-equipment demand or hospital budgets
  weaken;
- acquisitions continue at high cash cost without rising incremental ROIC;
- amortization, inventory step-up, integration, or impairment charges recur;
- inventory grows faster than procedures and sales;
- cyber or quality events interrupt hospital workflow or increase remediation
  costs materially;
- stock compensation and debt-financed acquisitions dilute owner returns; or
- robotics/platform adoption increases service burden without improving
  retention, pricing, or procedure economics.

The next filing review should capture acquisition cohorts, contingent
consideration, segment-level organic growth, procedure volume, implant pricing,
inventory turns, cyber costs, quality/regulatory reserves, R&D capitalization,
stock dilution, and the exact incentive scorecard.
