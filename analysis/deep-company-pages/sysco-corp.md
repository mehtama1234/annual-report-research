# Sysco: food distribution density, receivables finance, and the Jetro balance-sheet test

## What the company does

Sysco sells, sources, warehouses, and delivers food and related products to
customers that prepare meals away from home. Its customers include independent
restaurants, restaurant chains, lodging, healthcare, education, government,
entertainment, and other institutional operators.

The control point is the local distribution relationship. Sysco buys product,
holds inventory, assembles orders, routes trucks, delivers to the customer, and
provides product selection, sourcing, and ordering support. The relationship is
valuable because a foodservice customer needs frequent reliable deliveries,
consistent product availability, credit, and a practical assortment. The
relationship is not equivalent to a consumer brand: customers can change
distributors, food costs are competitive, and the company operates at a thin
margin.

The central question is whether route density, purchasing scale, private-label
mix, and local customer service create enough gross profit to pay for warehouse
labor, drivers, fuel, inventory, fleet, technology, acquisitions, interest, and
credit risk.

## FY2025 and FY2026 financial starting point

| Measure | FY2024 | FY2025 | FY2026 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Sales | $78.8B | $81.4B | $84.6B |
| Net earnings | $2.0B | $1.8B | $1.8B |
| Operating cash flow | $2.989B | $2.510B | $2.638B |
| Net capital expenditures | $753M | $692M | $524M |
| Acquisitions, net of cash | $1.210B | $40M | $189M |
| Core residual: OCF less net capex and acquisitions | $1.026B | $1.778B | $1.925B |
| Stock-based compensation | $104M | $93M | $118M |

The core residual is a screening measure. It excludes dividends, debt service,
and share repurchases, and it uses net capital expenditures because the company
reports proceeds from asset sales as an offset. FY2026 free cash flow was about
$2.1B before acquisitions; subtracting the $189M acquisition cash payment gives
approximately $1.93B before discretionary shareholder returns.

FY2026 operating cash flow was not simply an earnings conversion story. The
company reported an unfavorable $263M year-over-year accounts-receivable
comparison, partly offset by favorable accounts-payable and inventory changes.
The working-capital movement was less favorable than the prior year, but the
business still generated more cash because of operating performance and lower
capital spending.

## Operating economics

### Revenue is not the economic output

Sysco sells products at a large dollar value but retains only a fraction after
product cost, freight, warehouse handling, delivery, and operating expenses.
FY2026 gross profit was $15.6B, or approximately 18.5% of sales. Operating
income was approximately $3.1B, or about 3.7% of sales.

The relevant operating units are cases, customer locations, deliveries, gross
profit per case, route stops, warehouse productivity, and local customer
retention. Sales growth from food-price inflation can increase reported revenue
without increasing cases or real economic value.

### U.S. Foodservice is the main scale engine

In FY2026, U.S. Foodservice sales were $58.8B. Total case volume grew 1.4% and
local case volume grew 1.7%. Gross profit grew 3.3% to $11.2B, while gross
margin was 19.1%. Operating income was approximately $3.5B.

The evidence supports modest volume improvement and better product mix, not a
high-growth conclusion. Local independent customers matter because they can
produce better growth and relationship depth, but they also have greater credit
and survival risk than large national accounts.

### International Foodservice has different economics

FY2026 International Foodservice sales increased 7.6% to $16.0B, helped by
currency. Constant-currency sales increased 4.1%. Gross profit grew 9.4% to
$3.4B, with gross margin of 21.2%. Adjusted operating income grew faster than
GAAP operating income.

This segment should be evaluated in constant currency and after separating
acquisitions, divestitures, and local market conditions. Currency can raise
reported sales and gross profit without improving local purchasing power or
returns on invested capital.

## Earnings-quality and financial-engineering review

### 1. Receivables sales are a real financing component

Sysco sells portions of trade receivables to third-party financial institutions
on a non-recourse basis. Receivables sold were $5.9B in FY2026 and $5.2B in
FY2025. The company records the proceeds as an operating cash inflow and
reduces accounts receivable. Derecognized receivables that remained outstanding
were $222M at FY2026 year-end.

The arrangement may satisfy the accounting requirements for a sale, but an
investor should still treat it as part of the working-capital funding model.
The company is converting customer invoices into cash before normal collection.
The analysis should track the volume sold, the discount paid, the receivables
balance, customer aging, and whether the arrangement expands as sales grow.

The relevant question is not whether the transaction is permitted under GAAP.
It is whether reported operating cash flow would remain strong without selling
more receivables or relying on favorable supplier terms.

### 2. Sale-leasebacks make capital spending look lower than total asset use

FY2026 cash capital expenditures were $700M gross and $524M net of $176M of
asset-sale proceeds. The company says the proceeds were primarily from
sale-leaseback transactions. Total net plant and equipment additions, including
assets acquired through financing programs and finance leases, were $733M.

For owner-cash analysis, the $524M net figure is not the full economic cost of
operating the distribution network. Sale-leasebacks provide immediate cash but
create or extend lease obligations. Future filings should show lease expense,
lease liabilities, facility ownership changes, and whether sale proceeds are
being used repeatedly to support distributions or acquisition financing.

### 3. Adjusted earnings remove recurring strategic costs

FY2026 adjusted results excluded $287M of restructuring and transformational
project costs and $232M of acquisition-related costs. The company also excluded
$30M of bridge-loan amortization and $54M of losses on deal-contingent rate-lock
transactions related to the planned Jetro acquisition.

These exclusions are not all the same. A goodwill impairment is non-cash and
not a current-period cash burden. Supply-chain transformation, technology
changes, restructuring, acquisition integration, and financing costs consume
cash or represent the cost of the strategy. They should not be treated as
irrelevant merely because they are labeled non-GAAP adjustments.

The repeated presence of these items means the company’s adjusted EPS growth
rate is not a sufficient measure of recurring earnings quality.

### 4. Acquisition growth has both operating and financing costs

Sysco paid $189M for FY2026 acquisitions, primarily Fairfax Meadow and
Ginsberg’s Foods. That cash is included in the residual above. Acquisitions can
add customers, local density, purchasing volume, and specialty products, but
they also create goodwill, integration costs, amortization, and additional
working-capital needs.

The acquisition return should be measured using incremental cases, gross profit,
operating income, working capital, capex, and debt—not only adjusted EBITDA.

### 5. Stock compensation is material to per-share analysis

Stock-based compensation was $118M in FY2026, up from $93M in FY2025. The
company repurchased $200M of stock in FY2026 and paid $1.0B of dividends. A
buyback does not automatically offset dilution. The correct test is diluted
shares outstanding, shares issued through compensation plans, and the cash paid
for repurchases.

The 2026 10-K reports $125M of unrecognized compensation cost expected to be
recognized over a weighted-average period of 1.8 years. Future per-share value
should deduct the recurring economic cost of that compensation before assigning
credit for repurchases.

## Jetro Restaurant Depot: the decisive capital-allocation issue

On March 30, 2026, Sysco agreed to acquire Jetro Restaurant Depot. The announced
transaction value was approximately $29.1B, including $21.6B of cash payable to
Jetro equity holders, subject to adjustments, plus 91.5M shares of Sysco Holdings
common stock. Sysco expected to finance the cash portion with approximately
$21B of new debt and hybrid debt and $1B of cash, equity, or equity-linked
securities.

The transaction was still subject to regulatory review at the FY2026 filing.
The FTC had issued a second request for additional information, extending the
HSR waiting period. The company’s financing commitment was reduced from a $22B
bridge facility to $19B after a $3B delayed-draw term loan facility was entered.

This is not a normal tuck-in acquisition. It can change:

- net debt and interest expense;
- diluted share count and ownership economics;
- leverage and credit ratings;
- customer overlap and antitrust exposure;
- purchasing and distribution density;
- integration and technology costs;
- capital available for dividends, buybacks, and ordinary fleet renewal.

The acquisition should therefore be analyzed in two separate cases:

1. standalone Sysco, using the pre-transaction cash bridge and balance sheet;
2. pro forma Sysco plus Jetro, with new interest, debt repayment, dilution,
   integration costs, working capital, and verified synergies.

Projected synergies should not be included in normalized owner cash until the
company shows their source, implementation cost, timing, and cash realization.

## Lyn Alden macro, liquidity, and durability lens

Sysco is exposed to food inflation, restaurant and hospitality activity,
consumer credit, labor availability, fuel, interest rates, and small-business
failures. Its demand is recurring in the sense that customers need food
frequently, but individual customers can fail or switch distributors.

The scale advantage is purchasing power, broad assortment, route density,
distribution infrastructure, and credit capability. The main liquidity risk is
that inventory, receivables, and facilities require funding before the customer
payment is received. A downturn can cause restaurant closures, slower payments,
inventory markdowns, and lower route density at the same time.

The $5.9B receivables-sale program and $13.5B of total debt at FY2026 year-end
make financing conditions relevant even though Sysco generates substantial cash.
The Jetro transaction would materially increase that sensitivity if completed
with the proposed debt structure.

The durable part of the business is local distribution density and customer
embeddedness. The fragile part is the thin margin: a small gross-margin decline,
labor cost increase, or delivery inefficiency can consume a large share of
operating profit.

## Damodaran narrative-to-numbers lens

Management’s current narrative is that Sysco can produce faster earnings growth
through local case growth, private-label penetration, sourcing efficiency,
supply-chain productivity, and AI-enabled process transformation.

That narrative requires the following numerical links:

1. Local case growth must remain above total market growth without higher credit
   losses or delivery cost per case.
2. Sysco Brand penetration must improve gross profit without increasing product
   quality claims, inventory risk, or customer churn.
3. Gross-margin expansion must survive food-price deflation and competitive
   pricing, not only food-cost inflation.
4. AI and routing investments must lower labor and delivery cost after including
   technology capital, implementation, training, and restructuring.
5. Receivables sales must remain a stable funding choice rather than a rising
   dependency needed to support dividends, buybacks, or acquisition debt.
6. Jetro synergies must exceed the incremental interest, dilution, integration,
   regulatory, and working-capital burden.

Sysco is a mature, low-margin distributor. Its lifecycle value comes from
moderate volume growth, small margin improvements, disciplined acquisitions,
and reliable capital returns. A high-growth valuation would require evidence
that the Jetro transaction and AI program change the long-term economics rather
than only increase the financial scale of the company.

## Valuation screen

The market feed showed an equity value of approximately $40.0B. The standalone
screen below uses normalized owner cash before the pending Jetro transaction.
It is not a recommendation and does not include pro forma Jetro debt or shares.

| Case | Normalized owner cash | Cash multiple | Implied equity value | Required interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Bear | $1.4B | 15x | $21.0B | Thin-margin pressure, working-capital burden, weak integration capacity |
| Base | $1.9B | 20x | $38.0B | FY2026 core residual broadly persists before Jetro effects |
| Bull | $2.4B | 25x | $60.0B | Sustained case growth, margin expansion, and credible AI productivity |

At the current equity value, the standalone price is close to the base case.
That leaves little room for execution failure in Jetro, a rise in interest cost,
or a deterioration in the receivables and inventory cycle. The pro forma equity
case must be rebuilt after the transaction closes and financing terms are known.

## Thesis breakers and future filing tests

| Question | Evidence that would support the thesis | Evidence that would weaken it |
|---|---|---|
| Is local distribution gaining value? | Local cases grow, retention holds, and gross profit per case rises | Cases grow only from inflation or customer additions with lower route economics |
| Is gross profit durable? | Gross margin holds through inflation and deflation with stable service | Margin depends on food inflation, vendor payments, or temporary mix |
| Is cash conversion clean? | OCF remains strong without increasing receivables sales or supplier stretching | Receivables sold rise faster than sales or discounts and liquidity needs increase |
| Is capital spending understated? | Lease liabilities and sale-leaseback proceeds remain stable relative to network needs | Net capex is low because owned assets are repeatedly sold and leased back |
| Are adjusted earnings credible? | Restructuring and transformation costs decline and cash savings are visible | The same strategic costs recur while GAAP profit remains weak |
| Does Jetro create value? | Post-close cases, gross profit, synergies, leverage, and interest coverage improve | Debt rises, shares dilute, integration costs recur, or synergies remain unverified |
| Are buybacks accretive? | Diluted shares fall after stock compensation and acquisition issuance | Buybacks offset awards or are funded by debt while leverage rises |

## Bottom line

Sysco controls a valuable distribution relationship, not a consumer-facing brand.
Its scale and route density can produce durable cash, but the business remains
labor-heavy, capital-intensive relative to its margin, and dependent on
receivables financing and customer credit.

FY2026 operating results show improving cases, gross profit, cash flow, and
international execution. They do not yet prove that the proposed Jetro
transaction creates value. The transaction is large enough to dominate the
balance-sheet and valuation analysis, and its projected synergies should remain
outside normalized owner cash until the company demonstrates them in filings.

**Evidence grade:** Strong for FY2025–FY2026 operating results, cash flow,
receivables sales, capex, stock compensation, debt, and Jetro financing plans;
medium for AI savings and route-density durability; unresolved for Jetro
approval, financing, integration, synergies, and pro forma per-share value.
