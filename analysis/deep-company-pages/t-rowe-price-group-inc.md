# T. Rowe Price Group, Inc. — public-market asset management under fee and flow pressure

Research date: 2026-09-14  
Evidence window: FY2025 and FY2026 Q2  
Ticker: TROW  
Evidence grade: A for reported AUM, flows, fees, expenses, and earnings; B for investment-performance persistence, ETF and SMA transition, alternative-product growth, and the durability of client relationships.

## Bottom line

T. Rowe Price controls investment research, portfolio management, distribution relationships, retirement-plan access, and client trust. It earns mainly from advisory fees tied to assets under management. That creates a strong cash model when clients retain assets and fees remain stable, but a weak operating model when clients move to lower-fee products, passive vehicles, ETFs, model portfolios, or competing managers.

FY2025 ending AUM was $1.776T, average AUM was $1.677T, net client outflows were $56.9B, net revenues were $7.315B, adjusted net income was $2.195B, and adjusted EPS was $9.72. The effective fee rate excluding performance fees fell to 39.4 basis points from 41.0 basis points. In Q2 FY2026, ending AUM reached $1.893T, but net client outflows were $6.5B and the fee rate fell to 38.1 basis points.

The company therefore illustrates an important distinction: market appreciation can lift AUM and earnings while the underlying client relationship weakens. The durable economics depend on net flows, investment performance, product mix, fees, distribution cost, and whether the firm can replace legacy active-equity outflows with products that preserve a good fee and margin.

## What T. Rowe Price controls

The operating chain is:

`client savings and retirement need -> investment product and advice -> AUM and fee rate -> investment performance -> retention and flows -> operating margin -> capital return`

T. Rowe Price controls research, portfolio construction, product design, client service, distribution partnerships, and capital allocation. Its brand and investment record can reduce customer search and switching costs, especially in retirement plans and institutional relationships.

It does not control market returns, client allocation decisions, intermediary shelf space, fee competition, passive substitution, investment performance persistence, or the price clients will accept. AUM can rise because markets rise even while clients withdraw capital.

In FY2025, market appreciation of $216.7B more than offset $56.9B of net cash outflows. That is a direct demonstration of why ending AUM is an incomplete health measure. The investor should monitor organic growth, average AUM, fee rate, and product-level flows together.

## Fee compression and product transition

More than 90% of net revenue comes from investment advisory fees. That makes the fee rate a central economic variable. A decline from 41.0 to 39.4 basis points looks small, but applied across roughly $1.7T of average AUM it represents a material annual revenue pressure before expenses.

The mix shift is not automatically bad. ETFs, SMAs, fixed income, retirement, alternatives, and partnerships may improve future growth or client retention. But lower-fee products can require technology, distribution, and service spending while producing less revenue per dollar. Alternatives may carry higher fees but also have different performance, liquidity, carried-interest, and valuation risks.

The company must prove that new products produce durable net inflows and acceptable contribution margins rather than simply replacing one outflowing vehicle with another lower-fee vehicle.

## Earnings quality and capital returns

T. Rowe Price reports GAAP and adjusted results. Adjusted measures exclude items such as acquisition-related amortization, impairments, accrued carried-interest effects, and other items. The investor should separate true nonrecurring costs from the expense of maintaining product breadth and distribution.

Capital allocation is a visible part of the model. T. Rowe Price returned $1.768B to stockholders in 2025, including $624.6M of repurchases, and returned $441M in Q2 2026. Buybacks can offset equity compensation and shrink the share count, but they cannot repair persistent client outflows. The right measure is adjusted earnings per share after recurring investment, compensation, distribution, technology, and product-development costs.

Compensation is not merely overhead in asset management. Investment professionals, distribution teams, and relationship managers are the production capacity. Cutting compensation may improve a quarter while damaging investment performance or client retention. The investor should compare compensation, operating expenses, net flows, performance outcomes, and fee rates over time.

## Lyn Alden lens: market cycle and liquidity durability

T. Rowe Price has a strong balance-sheet model because it does not finance a large loan book or own heavy physical assets. Its risk is the sensitivity of revenue and client behavior to markets and liquidity conditions. In a market decline, AUM falls, fee revenue falls, and clients may move toward cash or lower-cost products at the same time.

The resilience test is whether the firm can maintain research and distribution investment during a market downturn without sacrificing long-term competitiveness. Cash and investments provide flexibility, but a mature asset manager can still suffer permanent franchise erosion if outflows continue through several cycles.

## Management incentives and capital allocation

The proxy statement should establish whether annual and long-term awards reward
AUM, net revenue, adjusted EPS, operating margin, net client flows, investment
performance, return on capital, share price, cash flow, or cash per diluted
share. Record the exact definitions, weights, threshold/target/maximum levels,
peer adjustments, and exclusions for market movement, acquisitions,
restructuring, carried-interest effects, and other unusual items.

The incentive tests are:

- Does an AUM target distinguish market appreciation from net client additions
  and retention?
- Does an EPS or margin target charge compensation for investment talent,
  distribution, technology, product development, and client service?
- Does a product-growth target include the lower fee rate and higher delivery
  cost of ETFs, SMAs, fixed income, retirement, and alternatives?
- Are acquisitions evaluated on retained fee-paying AUM, fee rate, integration
  cost, and cash return rather than acquired assets alone?
- Are buybacks judged against stock compensation, diluted shares, product
  investment, and the value of retaining financial flexibility through a market
  decline?

The 2025 evidence is a useful governance test: ending AUM rose because market
appreciation more than offset $56.9B of net outflows, while the effective fee
rate declined. A compensation measure based mainly on AUM or EPS could therefore
look successful while the underlying client relationship and fee economics
weaken. A measure based on organic flows, investment performance, normalized
earnings, and cash per continuing share would test the franchise more directly;
the proxy must show which design is actually used.

For each major product launch or distribution partnership, compare management's
stated success with net flows, retention, fee rate, compensation, technology,
distribution cost, contribution margin, and cash per share. For each repurchase,
compare the price with normalized earnings after fee compression and the capital
needed to preserve investment capability.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers

The narrative is: T. Rowe Price can use its active-investment heritage, retirement relationships, ETFs, SMAs, fixed income, alternatives, and partnerships to restore organic growth while preserving high margins and returning capital.

That narrative must become measurable assumptions:

- net flows by asset class, channel, geography, and product;
- average AUM versus market appreciation and distributions;
- effective fee rate and fee rate by product;
- investment performance persistence and client retention;
- ETF, SMA, retirement, fixed-income, and alternatives contribution margins;
- compensation, distribution, technology, and product-development spending;
- performance-based and capital-allocation income versus recurring advisory fees;
- cash, investments, acquisitions, buybacks, dividends, and diluted shares;
- terminal earnings after fee compression and active-equity outflows normalize.

## Cash conversion after talent and product investment

T. Rowe Price has a light physical-asset model, but its owner cash is not costless. Start with operating cash flow, then account for compensation required to retain investment talent, distribution and technology spending, product development, taxes, acquisitions, and stock-based compensation. Repurchases and dividends are distributions after those needs; they should not be used to mask a continuing decline in fee-paying relationships.

The clean cash test is cash generated from advisory fees and related recurring services, less the recurring spending needed to preserve investment performance and client distribution, compared with diluted shares outstanding. In a market decline, AUM and fee revenue can fall quickly while the firm still needs to fund research and client service. The balance sheet provides time, but it does not turn persistent outflows into durable cash generation.

## Valuation

T. Rowe Price’s market value was approximately $22.673B on September 14, 2026. A simple adjusted-earnings screen is:

| Normalized annual adjusted-earnings screen | Multiple | Implied equity value |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $1.7B bear case | 9x | $15.3B |
| $2.2B base case | 11x | $24.2B |
| $2.7B strong case | 13x | $35.1B |

These are scenario outputs, not price targets. The base case requires new products and channels to offset legacy outflows, fee compression to slow, operating expenses to remain disciplined without damaging investment capability, and market appreciation not to be the only source of AUM growth.

## Thesis breakers and next evidence

The thesis weakens if net outflows remain negative after market appreciation is removed; if the fee rate continues to fall faster than expenses; if active investment performance deteriorates; if ETFs, SMAs, and alternatives grow but carry lower contribution margins; if distribution and technology costs rise without better flows; if performance-based or capital-allocation income is required to support earnings; if buybacks offset stock compensation without reducing diluted shares; or if a market decline exposes operating leverage.

The next filings should answer: Did flows turn positive by product and channel? Did fee compression slow? What share of AUM growth came from markets versus net subscriptions? Did ETF and SMA growth improve economics? Did compensation and distribution cost grow slower than advisory revenue? Did the share count fall after repurchases?
