# Coca-Cola — routine beverage brand, bottler system, and cash-flow distortion

As of 2026-09-13. FY2025 figures are U.S. GAAP figures in U.S. dollars; dollar
amounts are in millions unless otherwise stated.

## How to read this memo

Organic revenue growth removes the effect of acquisitions, divestitures, and
currency. Unit case volume measures physical beverage servings sold through the
system. Price/mix measures what the company earned from pricing and the mix of
packages and channels. Free cash flow is a non-GAAP measure based on operating
cash flow less capital spending, so unusual payments, bottler investments,
working capital, and acquisitions must remain visible.

## Investment question

Coca-Cola controls trademarks, beverage formulas, consumer demand, package and
channel architecture, and a global bottler and distribution system. It does not
operate every plant or route itself. The question is whether this division of
labor creates durable owner cash after marketing, bottler support, concentrate
costs, working capital, acquisitions, debt, and the changing health and
affordability preferences of consumers.

The three-year free-cash-flow comparison is $6.624B in 2023, $4.741B in 2024,
and $5.296B in 2025. The 2025 number is unusually affected by the $6.069B
fairlife contingent-consideration payment. A more useful bridge is therefore
reported FCF, the payment, capital spending, bottler investments, dividends,
buybacks, and any recurring restructuring or acquisition cash.

## What the annual and current-period record reveals

- The global beverage system remained resilient. FY2025 unit case volume was
  even, organic revenue growth was 5%, and Coca-Cola Zero Sugar volume grew
  14% for the year. The system still recruited demand, but reported growth was
  not simply a result of more physical cases.
- Price, package, and channel design are central. Mini-cans, single-serve
  packages, away-from-home availability, affordability options, and premium
  formats change the revenue and margin earned per occasion.
- The bottler structure transfers much of the physical work to partners, but it
  does not make the system costless. Coca-Cola must protect brand standards,
  support bottler execution, fund marketing, manage concentrate inputs, and
  preserve retail and food-service access.
- Q1 and Q2 2026 provide recent operating evidence. Organic revenue grew 10%
  and 6%, while unit case volume grew 3% and 5%. The gap between volume and
  revenue identifies pricing, mix, currency, and calendar effects that must be
  separated before calling the improvement demand-led.
- Q4 2025 operating income fell 32% partly because of a $960M non-cash BODYARMOR
  trademark impairment. The impairment is not a cash expense in the quarter,
  but it is evidence that a branded acquisition or growth asset did not support
  its previous carrying value.
- The $6.069B fairlife payment was cash, not an accounting add-back. Excluding
  it is useful for understanding the underlying period, but the payment shows
  that acquisition structures can create large future claims even when the
  purchase occurred years earlier.
- Coca-Cola's connected-packaging and FIFA activity may improve recruitment and
  first-party data, but the investment question is whether these activities
  increase repeat volume, price realization, or distribution efficiency after
  their cost.

## Forensic earnings-quality questions

### 1. Is revenue growth coming from demand or price architecture?

Separate unit case volume, concentrate sales, price/mix, currency, acquisitions,
and divestitures by geography and category. A 5% organic-revenue increase with
flat volume can be healthy if consumers accept the mix, but it can also weaken
future affordability and volume.

### 2. Is the bottler model transferring burden or creating resilience?

Track bottler ownership, equity-method income, bottler receivables, incentives,
marketing support, finished-beverage versus concentrate mix, and bottler
profitability. Parent economics are durable only if bottlers can fund vehicles,
labor, packaging, coolers, plant investment, and route execution.

### 3. What is recurring free cash flow after acquisition claims?

Show the $5.296B reported FCF and the $6.069B fairlife payment separately. Then
test recurring capex, acquisitions, restructuring, pension, litigation, and
working-capital needs. “Excluding” a large cash payment is an analytical bridge,
not proof that the payment was economically irrelevant.

### 4. Do impairments identify weak capital allocation?

Follow BODYARMOR and other acquired brands from purchase price through revenue,
volume, margins, marketing spending, impairment, and disposal. A non-cash
impairment does not prove wrongdoing; it shows that earlier expected cash flows
or brand values were revised downward.

### 5. Can price/mix continue without damaging the system?

Measure volume, affordability, consumer income, retailer response, package size,
promotions, and bottler margins. A price increase that raises parent revenue but
reduces cases, bottler health, or consumer recruitment may not create durable
owner cash.

### 6. Are buybacks and dividends covered after reinvestment?

Compare shareholder returns with normalized FCF after brand investment, bottler
support, PP&E, acquisitions, and working capital. A high payout can be rational
for a mature brand system, but it reduces flexibility if volume or bottler
economics weaken.

## Lyn Alden / Damodaran / forensic synthesis

- **Lyn Alden lens:** Coca-Cola has defensive demand and global distribution,
  but it remains exposed to inflation in ingredients and packaging, currency,
  rates, consumer affordability, retailer power, and regulation of sugar and
  health claims. Global scale improves resilience but does not eliminate local
  purchasing-power differences.
- **Damodaran lens:** model volume, price/mix, category mix, bottler economics,
  reinvestment, acquisitions, taxes, debt, and terminal growth separately. A
  brand deserves a long duration only if the system can preserve volume and
  returns while reinvesting enough to remain relevant.
- **Forensic lens:** the central risks are treating organic revenue as volume
  growth, treating bottler economics as irrelevant, excluding a large fairlife
  cash payment without a normalized bridge, and treating brand impairments as
  isolated events rather than evidence about acquisition underwriting.

## Valuation framing

The 2026-09-11 market snapshot was approximately $380.79B of equity value at
$88.29 per share. Against reported FY2025 FCF of $5.296B, the screen is about
71.9x. A normalized owner-cash range of $7B / $10B / $13B at 28x / 32x / 36x
implies $196B / $320B / $468B. The current value requires recurring cash closer
to the high end of the historical range, continued price/mix strength, stable
volume, and successful brand and bottler reinvestment.

## Thesis breakers and next checks

1. Organic revenue growth remains price-led while unit case volume declines.
2. Bottler profitability, route investment, or retailer relationships weaken.
3. Zero-sugar and newer categories fail to replace volume lost in legacy drinks.
4. Large contingent acquisition payments, restructurings, or brand impairments
   recur.
5. Marketing, bottler support, capital spending, and acquisitions consume the
   cash being labeled excess.
6. Regulations, health concerns, currency, or affordability pressure reduce
   repeat consumption and price acceptance.
