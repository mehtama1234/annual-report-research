# The Trade Desk — open-internet decisioning, stock compensation, and the test of platform control

Date baseline: 2026-09-13  
Primary annual filing: [The Trade Desk FY2025 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1671933/000167193326000014/ttd-20251231.htm)  
FY2025 results: [The Trade Desk FY2025 results](https://investors.thetradedesk.com/news-and-events/news/news-details/2026/The-Trade-Desk-Reports-Fourth-Quarter-and-Fiscal-Year-2025-Financial-Results/)  
Companion packet: [The Trade Desk company packet](/home/mehtama1/git-repo/annual-report-research/extracted/technology/internet-information-providers/the-trade-desk-inc/company-packet.md)

## Bottom line

The Trade Desk sells software that helps advertisers and agencies decide where
to buy advertising across websites, streaming television, audio, mobile, and
other digital channels. It generally does not own the content or most of the
advertising space. Its value depends on making fragmented inventory, audience
data, identity, measurement, and campaign optimization useful to buyers.

The control point is the repeated budget-allocation decision. A customer keeps
using the platform when it believes the software improves reach, measurement,
price, or return on advertising spend. That control point is real but exposed
to closed platforms, agencies, publishers, identity rules, data quality,
advertiser budgets, and the possibility that automated tools reduce the
platform’s differentiation.

FY2025 revenue was $2.896B, net income was $443.3M, and operating cash flow was
$992.7M. Capital spending included $197.0M of property and equipment and
$12.8M of capitalized software. Stock-based compensation was $490.6M, and the
company repurchased $1.380B of stock. These figures require two separate
questions: whether the platform generates strong cash before capital returns,
and how much of that cash remains for common owners after treating employee
equity, infrastructure investment, and dilution as real economic costs.

The main 2026 issue is growth deceleration. The packet reports Q2 2026 revenue
of $715.1M, up 3%, after Q1 revenue growth of 12%. The strategic platform may
remain valuable, but a lower growth rate changes the valuation substantially
when the market price assumes durable compounding.

## What The Trade Desk controls

`advertiser budget -> campaign rules and data -> bid decision -> open-internet inventory -> measurement -> budget renewal`

The Trade Desk controls the buyer-side workflow, bidding logic, campaign data,
optimization tools, integrations, and relationships with agencies, brands,
publishers, data providers, and identity partners. Advertisers control budgets
and can shift spending. Publishers control inventory access. Closed platforms
control their own data and supply. Regulators influence identity, privacy, and
data use.

The control point is stronger when campaign history, measurement, integrations,
identity, and optimization make the platform part of a customer’s daily
workflow. It is weaker when a buyer can move spend through another demand-side
platform, use a closed ecosystem directly, or obtain comparable optimization
from a large software or agency tool.

The reported free-cash figure here is operating cash less PP&E and capitalized

The 2025 cash flow contains a substantial working-capital component. Accounts
receivable used $432.7M of cash, while accounts payable provided $291.1M. This
is consistent with growth in transaction volume and payment timing, but it
means revenue growth does not immediately equal collected cash. Capitalized
software and high infrastructure spending should be compared with the service
capacity required to support AI, CTV, identity, and platform reliability.

## The platform’s economic chain

The Trade Desk is paid for helping a buyer execute advertising transactions.
The value is not simply the number of impressions. The buyer must believe that
the platform can identify useful audiences, access suitable inventory, avoid
fraud or low-quality supply, optimize bids, measure outcomes, and improve the
next campaign.

The durable chain has five tests:

1. advertising budgets continue to move to programmatic and connected television;
2. customers keep allocating a meaningful share through the platform;
3. measurement and identity remain credible under privacy restrictions;
4. data, inventory, and workflow integrations improve results; and
5. revenue and cash grow faster than hosting, data, AI, employee, and dilution costs.

Gross platform spend is not the same as company revenue or owner cash. It is
the value of advertising transacted through the platform. The company must
retain enough of the economics after payments to publishers, data providers,
and other partners while preserving customer value.

## Earnings quality and forensic tests

### 1. Stock-based compensation is a large economic cost

Stock-based compensation was $490.6M in 2025, compared with $494.7M in 2024
and $491.6M in 2023. The expense is added back in operating cash flow. That is
appropriate for reconciling accounting earnings to cash received and paid in
the period, but it does not mean the employee service was free. The company
must either issue shares or use cash to offset dilution.

Track award grants, vesting, diluted shares, repurchases, average repurchase
price, employee retention, and compensation per employee. The relevant output
is cash per continuing share after the economic cost of retaining the people
who build and operate the platform.

### 2. Receivables can grow with transaction volume

Accounts receivable used $432.7M of cash in 2025. An advertising intermediary
may record revenue and owe payments to suppliers before collecting from an
advertiser or agency. Growth can therefore require substantial financing even
when margins are high.

Review receivable days, customer and agency concentration, aging, credit-loss
provisions, disputed invoices, payment terms, and the relationship between
platform spend, billings, revenue, and cash collections. A rising receivable
balance is not automatically a problem, but it reduces the quality of growth
until collected.

### 3. Payables can temporarily support cash

Accounts payable provided $291.1M of cash in 2025. This may reflect ordinary
growth and the timing of payments to publishers, data providers, and other
partners. It can also make operating cash look stronger when the company is
paying suppliers later.

Compare payable days with contractual payment terms, platform volume, supplier
concentration, cash settled after year-end, and any disputes or changes in
settlement practice. A cash benefit from extending payment timing is not the
same as a permanent improvement in platform economics.

### 4. Revenue growth must be separated from gross spend growth

Gross spend reached $13.4B in 2025 while revenue was $2.896B. The spread
between those measures reflects the company’s economic share and the role of
payments to supply-side participants and other partners.

Track revenue as a percentage of gross spend, pricing, take rate, supply costs,
data costs, customer mix, and whether new CTV or retail-media activity carries
lower economics. If spend grows through lower-margin channels, revenue and
profit may not scale at the same rate.

### 5. AI can lower costs or weaken differentiation

The company is investing in Koa, Koa Agents, campaign automation, prediction,
optimization, and measurement. AI may improve results and reduce manual work.
It may also make bidding and campaign management more available through other
platforms, agencies, cloud providers, or advertisers’ internal systems.

The test is not whether AI features launch. Track incremental revenue, customer
adoption, retention by AI usage, gross margin, compute and hosting cost,
employee productivity, customer savings, pricing power, and whether customers
can reproduce the output elsewhere.

### 6. CTV and identity create both opportunity and dependency

Connected television, UID2/EUID identity tools, OpenPath supply access, and
publisher and retail-data partnerships may strengthen the independent buying
workflow. But the company relies on publishers, streaming services, device
owners, agencies, data providers, and privacy rules that it does not control.

Track CTV spend and take rate, inventory access, measurement quality, identity
match rates, opt-out rates, partner concentration, supply-path changes, and
whether major publishers or platforms favor their own buying systems.

### 7. Buybacks can obscure per-share economics

The company repurchased $1.380B of stock in 2025 and had $658.2M of cash at
year-end, alongside significant short-term investments. Buybacks can reduce
dilution or return surplus cash, but their value depends on the price paid and
whether the platform’s future growth supports that price.

Compare repurchase prices with normalized cash generation, diluted shares,
stock grants, liquidity needs, infrastructure investment, and the growth rate
actually achieved. A large buyback during a slowing-growth period deserves
explicit capital-allocation analysis.

## Alden macro and durability lens

The Trade Desk depends on advertiser budgets, which are sensitive to economic
growth, consumer demand, corporate confidence, and marketing return targets.
CTV and retail media may gain share while total advertising budgets weaken, but
share gain does not eliminate a cyclical budget base.

The liquidity profile is stronger than that of a leveraged media owner: cash
and short-term investments are material and the company reported no revolver
borrowings in the 2025 filing. The main balance-sheet risks are different:
receivables can absorb cash, AI and hosting investment can rise quickly, and
employee equity can dilute owners. Privacy regulation and closed-platform
decisions can change the economic access to data and inventory.

## Damodaran narrative-to-numbers lens

The narrative is “The Trade Desk is the independent decisioning layer for
fragmented open-internet and CTV advertising.” The model must specify:

1. total advertising growth versus share transferred to programmatic channels;
2. gross platform spend, revenue, take rate, and supply/data costs;
3. customer retention, wallet share, agency concentration, and budget renewal;
4. CTV, retail-media, international, and other channel mix;
5. identity match rates, measurement quality, and partner dependence;
6. AI adoption, compute cost, hosting cost, and employee productivity;
7. receivables, payables, credit losses, and cash collection timing;
8. PP&E, capitalized software, and infrastructure replacement needs;
9. stock compensation, dilution, buybacks, and cash per share; and
10. a growth fade rate that reflects competition, closed ecosystems, and macro cycles.

The company appears to be in a high-margin platform phase, but Q2 2026 growth
deceleration means the valuation must test lifecycle maturity and execution.
The key question is whether the decisioning workflow becomes more essential as
media fragments, or whether the same complexity attracts competing tools that
compress the take rate and growth rate.

## Valuation screen

The September 11, 2026 market snapshot showed equity value of approximately
$6.74B. The cases below use normalized common owner cash after PP&E and
software investment, an economic charge for stock compensation and dilution,
and a reserve for working-capital needs. They are a screening range, not a
complete discounted-cash-flow model.

| Case | Normalized common owner cash | Equity cash multiple | Implied common equity value |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Bear | $300M | 18x | $5.4B |
| Base | $450M | 22x | $9.9B |
| Bull | $600M | 26x | $15.6B |

The current value is below the base case but above the bear case. The base
case requires the platform to recover growth, maintain strong retention, keep
take rate and supply economics stable, and convert cash after infrastructure
investment and the economic cost of employee equity. The bear case reflects
lower growth, weaker CTV or identity economics, continued receivable funding,
or AI-driven pricing pressure. The bull case requires durable share gains and
meaningful operating leverage without a comparable rise in dilution.

## Thesis breakers and next filings

The thesis weakens if:

- revenue growth remains near the Q2 2026 pace while valuation assumes a higher rate;
- gross platform spend grows but revenue share or take rate falls;
- customer retention stays high while wallet share, pricing, or gross profit per customer declines;
- receivables grow faster than revenue or credit losses increase;
- payables temporarily support cash and later reverse;
- CTV inventory, identity access, or measurement quality deteriorates;
- closed platforms limit interoperability or route more budgets internally;
- AI features increase compute cost without improving retention, pricing, or productivity;
- stock compensation remains near $500M and dilution outpaces buybacks; or
- capital spending and working-capital funding rise faster than common owner cash.

The next filings should show gross spend and revenue by channel, take rate,
supply and data costs, customer wallet share, retention, receivable aging,
payable timing, credit losses, CTV economics, identity adoption, AI usage and
cost, diluted shares, stock awards, capital spending, buyback prices, and cash
per continuing share.

## Evidence grade

- Control point: **B+** — repeated buyer workflow, data, measurement, and integrations create switching friction, but supply and identity partners remain outside control.
- Operating economics: **A-** — the platform is highly profitable, but take rate, supply costs, and advertiser budgets matter.
- Cash conversion: **B** — FY2025 operating cash was strong, though receivables, payables, and large non-cash equity compensation require adjustment.
- Balance-sheet resilience: **A-** — substantial liquidity and low traditional debt reduce financial fragility.
- Capital allocation: **B-** — buybacks are large, but their value depends on price, dilution, and the durability of growth.
- Valuation: **B-** — current value is below the base screen but still requires a meaningful recovery from the 2026 slowdown.
