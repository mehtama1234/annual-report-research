## Bottom line

Union Pacific operates a large North American freight railroad. Its control
point is the rail network itself: right-of-way, terminals, locomotives, freight
cars, operating systems, customer connections, and the safety and regulatory
record required to move freight reliably. The network is difficult to reproduce
because it combines land, permits, infrastructure, terminals, trained labor,
and decades of customer and interchange relationships.

FY2025 operating revenue was `$24.5B`, net income was `$7.138B`, operating cash
flow was `$9.290B`, and capital investments were `$3.791B`. The cash residual
after capital investment was approximately `$5.499B` before dividends, share
repurchases, debt service, and other owner claims. That is strong cash
conversion, but it is not low-capital economics: the railroad must continually
replace and improve track, bridges, locomotives, freight cars, terminals,
technology, and safety systems.

Current judgment: Union Pacific has a durable physical network and meaningful
pricing power. The investment case depends on whether pricing and productivity
continue to exceed labor, fuel, maintenance, safety, and capacity costs, and
whether the proposed Norfolk Southern combination creates value without
bringing regulatory delay, integration cost, leverage, or service deterioration.

## What Union Pacific controls

`shipper demand -> origin terminal -> rail network -> interchange or destination -> delivered freight -> renewal and pricing`

Union Pacific controls the network path, terminal access, dispatching, service
reliability, locomotive and car utilization, labor deployment, and the customer
relationship for freight moving over its system. It does not control commodity
production, trade flows, customer inventories, fuel prices, labor negotiations,
regulatory approvals, or merger review.

Its freight mix includes agricultural products, automotive, chemicals and
plastics, coal, industrial products, and intermodal. Each has different volume,
price, fuel-surcharge, service, equipment, and cyclicality characteristics.

Rail economics are not equivalent to simple route density. The network's value
comes from the combination of long-haul capacity, terminal placement,
interchange connections, customer-specific facilities, and the high cost of
moving large freight volumes to another mode when alternatives are limited.

## Earnings quality and forensic tests

### 1. Operating ratio is useful but not the whole return

The operating ratio measures operating expenses as a percentage of operating
revenue. A lower ratio generally means more revenue remains after operating
costs, but the measure excludes the capital spending required to maintain and
improve the railroad.

Union Pacific improved its reported operating ratio to `59.8%` in 2025. The
analyst should connect that improvement to carloads, price, mix, fuel surcharge,
labor, equipment utilization, maintenance, safety spending, and capital
investment. A lower ratio produced by deferring maintenance or reducing
service quality would not represent durable economics.

### 2. Price and fuel surcharge must be separated

Rail revenue can rise because of core pricing, fuel surcharge, volume, mix, and
foreign or cross-border effects. Fuel-surcharge revenue may rise when fuel
prices rise without producing the same economic benefit because fuel expense
also rises.

The correct bridge separates core price, fuel surcharge, volume, mix, fuel cost,
and customer contract terms. The durable pricing test is whether core price
stays above labor, materials, maintenance, and capital inflation after fuel
effects are removed.

### 3. Network utilization is constrained by service quality

Railroads can improve short-term productivity by running more cars through the
same network. If congestion, crew shortages, locomotive availability, terminal
delays, or service failures increase, the apparent efficiency can damage future
volume and customer retention.

Track velocity, dwell, terminal performance, locomotive availability, car
utilization, on-time performance, safety, customer complaints, and service
recovery. A railroad earns the right to raise price only when it delivers a
reliable service that customers cannot readily replace.

### 4. Capitalized costs require judgment

The 2025 filing describes significant judgment in deciding which expenditures
meet property-accounting criteria, extend useful life, improve safety, or
improve operating efficiency. Capitalized costs added to property were about
`$3.9B` in 2025.

Capitalization is normal for a railroad, but it moves cost from the current
period into future depreciation. The analyst should compare capital additions
with depreciation, maintenance needs, asset age, safety outcomes, service
metrics, and cash returns. A high capital program can be productive; it can
also lower current expense while increasing the future replacement burden.

### 5. The Norfolk Southern proposal changes the risk profile

The proposed combination with Norfolk Southern could expand network reach and
create operating or commercial benefits. It also introduces merger review,
regulatory conditions, integration cost, financing needs, labor coordination,
service risk, and potential dilution or leverage.

The standalone Union Pacific valuation should remain separate from merger
synergies until approvals, funding, integration plans, and operating results
make them observable. Management projections for unlevered cash are not the
same as realized common cash.

## Balance sheet and liquidity

Union Pacific generated large operating cash and had access to debt markets,
but its network requires continuous capital and carries substantial debt,
lease, labor, environmental, safety, and legal claims. In 2025 it issued about
`$1.995B` of debt and repaid about `$1.428B`.

The liquidity stress case is not only a recession. It is lower freight volume,
higher labor or fuel cost, a major safety event, regulatory restrictions,
service investment, merger financing, and a need to maintain dividends while
capital spending remains high. A resilient railroad should preserve service
quality and capital renewal without requiring repeated balance-sheet expansion.

## Alden lens: liquidity and durability

Railroad durability comes from scarce right-of-way, network scale, customer
connections, high fixed costs, and the limited availability of equivalent
routes. Macro exposure remains meaningful because freight volume follows
industrial activity, agriculture, automotive production, trade, construction,
energy, and consumer imports.

The railroad is a durable asset system, not a low-risk cash stream. Higher
interest rates affect debt and merger financing. Labor agreements can alter
cost and service. Safety or environmental events can create large cash claims.
The strongest evidence of durability is stable service and cash after capital
renewal across a full freight cycle.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers

The narrative is: “Union Pacific controls a scarce freight network and can
increase owner cash through pricing, utilization, mix, and operating efficiency.”
The valuation model must specify:

1. carloads, revenue per car, core price, fuel surcharge, and mix;
2. freight category economics and cyclicality;
3. velocity, dwell, terminal productivity, crew, and equipment utilization;
4. labor, fuel, maintenance, safety, environmental, and service costs;
5. maintenance versus growth capital, lease buyouts, and asset age;
6. debt, interest, pensions or labor claims, and liquidity;
7. Norfolk Southern approval, funding, integration, and synergy probability;
8. dividends, repurchases, share count, and cash per diluted share.

Union Pacific is a mature network operator with pricing power and a large
reinvestment requirement. A premium valuation is justified only if pricing and
productivity continue to create cash after the capital required to preserve
service and safety, and if merger optionality does not become a balance-sheet
or regulatory liability.

the capital and regulatory burden.

## Thesis breakers and next filings

The thesis weakens if:

- core pricing falls below labor, fuel, maintenance, and capital inflation;
- operating-ratio improvement comes from deferred maintenance or weaker service;
- carloads, velocity, terminal performance, or customer retention deteriorate;
- capital additions rise without safety, capacity, service, or cash returns;
- fuel-surcharge revenue obscures weak core pricing or fuel-cost recovery;
- safety, environmental, labor, or regulatory claims produce recurring cash
  charges;
- Norfolk Southern financing or approval terms increase leverage or dilution;
  or
- dividends and repurchases consume cash needed for network renewal.

The next filings should provide:

1. carloads, revenue per car, core price, fuel surcharge, and mix;
2. operating ratio, labor, fuel, maintenance, and service metrics;
3. velocity, dwell, terminal performance, locomotive and car utilization;
4. capital investment by maintenance, growth, safety, and lease buyout;
5. debt, interest, labor, environmental, and safety claims;
6. Norfolk Southern approval, funding, synergy, and integration updates; and
7. cash per diluted share after capital renewal and capital returns.

## Evidence grade

- **High confidence:** FY2025 revenue, net income, operating cash flow, capital
  investments, operating ratio, dividends, repurchases, 2026 quarterly revenue
  and EPS, and capital plan from filed or company-issued materials.
- **Medium confidence:** normalized owner-cash cases and the maintenance versus
  growth split within capital investment.
- **Open:** long-run freight-cycle normalization, realized pricing after fuel,
  service quality under higher utilization, and the value and cost of any
