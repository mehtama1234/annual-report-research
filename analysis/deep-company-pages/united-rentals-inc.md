Primary annual filing: [United Rentals 2025 Form 10-K](/home/mehtama1/git-repo/annual-report-research/raw/sec/industrial-goods/rental-leasing-services/united-rentals-inc/2025-10k.html)

## Bottom line

United Rentals owns the access layer behind physical infrastructure. Customers
rent equipment, specialty capability, logistics, telematics, maintenance, and
availability instead of owning every machine themselves. That is a real
workflow control point, but it is not asset-light: the platform must purchase,
maintain, reposition, finance, insure, and eventually sell a very large fleet.

The FY2025 filing shows attractive but burdened economics. Operating cash flow
was $5.190B and management-defined free cash flow was $2.181B after $4.149B of
rental-equipment purchases, $379M of non-rental equipment and intangible
spending, equipment-sale proceeds, and insurance proceeds. Acquisitions consumed
$357M, leaving only about $1.824B before the $1.969B repurchase program and
$464M of dividends. The business generated cash, but the capital-return policy
was not covered by the simple residual after acquisition spending.

Current judgment: United Rentals is a high-quality physical access platform
with scale, density, specialty breadth, and customer workflow relevance. The
forensic question is whether utilization, rental rates, resale recovery, and
fleet productivity can keep returns above the cost of fleet capital through a
cycle. The thesis should be valued on through-cycle free cash flow after fleet
refresh and acquisitions, not adjusted EBITDA or peak project demand.

## What the company controls

The economic chain is:

`customer project / maintenance need -> equipment selection and availability -> delivery / service -> utilization and rental rate -> fleet renewal / resale`

United Rentals' control point is not merely the equipment itself. It combines a
large catalog, branch density, specialty businesses, digital tools, delivery,
service, and the ability to move fleet toward large projects. This helps
customers manage construction, power, utilities, industrial maintenance, and
other work without carrying the full fixed cost and utilization risk of an
internal fleet.

The customer receives flexibility and labor/productivity support. United
Rentals carries equipment ownership, depreciation, maintenance, damage,
insurance, idle-time, logistics, residual-value, financing, and environmental
risks. That is the control-point-versus-burden-carrier pattern in unusually pure
form.

\*Net rental capex is rental-equipment purchases less rental-equipment sale

The important pattern is not just revenue growth. From 2023 to 2025, revenue
rose while management FCF declined modestly from $2.306B to $2.181B, even as
operating cash flow improved. More fleet investment and acquisition activity
absorbed much of the operating improvement. That is consistent with a growing
platform, but it means the return on new fleet and specialty capacity must be
measured rather than assumed.

The residual after returns is not a claim that United Rentals was insolvent or
that buybacks were improper. It is a stress signal: the company returned more
capital than the simple cash left after fleet investment and acquisitions. That
can be rational if fleet growth produces high future returns, but it increases
the importance of leverage, resale values, utilization, and capital-market
access during a downturn.

The FCF definition is more transparent than many adjusted metrics because it
shows equipment purchases and proceeds directly. It still requires judgment:
fleet purchases can be growth or replacement, sale proceeds can be favorable or
forced, and a fleet that is underinvested can temporarily produce superior cash
at the cost of future availability and competitiveness.

## Earnings quality and possible financial engineering

### 1. Utilization and rate can mask fleet aging

Rental revenue is a function of fleet size, time utilization, rental rate,
productivity, mix, and ancillary/re-rent economics. Strong revenue growth can
come from adding more equipment rather than earning better returns on existing
fleet. The next filing should separate fleet productivity from fleet growth,
owned-equipment economics from re-rent, and rental rate from inflationary
pass-through.

The 2025 and Q2 2026 materials show the right contradiction: specialty revenue
grew strongly, but lower-margin ancillary and re-rent mix pressured specialty
margin. A larger specialty footprint may increase customer control while
lowering current unit economics.

### 2. Resale gains and fleet age matter

Equipment-sale proceeds are part of the FCF bridge, but proceeds are not pure
operating income. High resale values can support cash in a tight equipment
market; falling values can expose the residual-value assumption embedded in
depreciation and fleet replacement economics. The forensic test is to compare
realized sale proceeds and gains/losses with book value, equipment age, and
replacement spending across a cycle.

### 3. Adjusted EPS and EBITDA exclude the capital burden

Adjusted EBITDA is useful for operating comparison, but it excludes the
depreciation and capital renewal that make the rental platform possible. The
company's management-defined FCF is more decision-useful, though it still does
not automatically deduct acquisition capital, debt principal, or the economic
cost of keeping fleet availability ahead of demand.

Stock compensation was $134M in 2025. It is smaller than fleet capex but remains
a real per-share claim when buybacks are presented as shareholder returns.
Repurchase analysis must show whether the $1.969B buyback reduced diluted shares
after SBC or mainly offset dilution while leverage remained elevated.

### 4. Restructuring is a recurring normalization question

The Q1 2026 packet included $45M of restructuring charges tied to branch and
function consolidation. Branch optimization may create real future savings,
but repeated “one-time” restructuring should be treated as a cost of operating
the network. The valuation case should not capitalize the savings without
tracking cash implementation costs, service levels, fleet repositioning, and
whether revenue is lost from local coverage reductions.

### 5. Acquisitions add density but also integration and leverage

The FY2025 acquisition cash outflow was $357M, while the company continues to
use acquisitions and specialty expansion to deepen its offering. Acquisitions
can improve branch density, customer access, and fleet utilization. They can
also purchase peak rental rates, add integration costs, and raise depreciation
and debt. Each cohort needs post-close revenue, margin, utilization, capex,
synergy, and retention evidence.

## Capital structure and Lyn Alden durability lens

United Rentals benefits from a secular outsourcing and access trend. Customers
with seasonal, specialized, expensive, or labor-intensive equipment needs may
prefer variable access to ownership. Infrastructure, power, utilities, and
industrial projects can create a multi-year demand floor.

The macro burden is operating leverage plus financial leverage. Higher rates
raise fleet financing costs; recession reduces utilization and rental rates;
contractor failures increase credit risk; and a broad used-equipment selloff
reduces resale recovery just when customers need less capacity. The company
ended 2025 with roughly $14.3B of long-term debt and only $459M of cash, while
the packet reported 1.8x net leverage and nearly $3B of liquidity in Q2 2026.
Liquidity is adequate in the disclosed operating plan, but the asset base must
keep earning and refinancing remains part of the thesis.

The Alden questions are:

- Does access-over-ownership behavior persist through a tighter liquidity and
  construction cycle?
- Can scale and specialty density protect utilization and rental rates when
  industry supply rises?
- How much of FCF depends on favorable used-equipment prices and sale timing?
- Can the company fund fleet renewal, acquisitions, and shareholder returns
  without relying on peak utilization or cheap debt?

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers

The bullish narrative is that United Rentals coordinates equipment access for
physical projects: scale, branch density, specialty breadth, and digital service
raise utilization and customer switching costs while customers avoid owning a
full internal fleet.

The skeptical narrative is that competitive protection still depends on a
capital-intensive fleet with cyclical utilization. The market may capitalize record revenue and adjusted
EBITDA while treating replacement capex, fleet sales, debt, and restructuring as
temporary or optional.

The valuation case requires management FCF to remain positive after a
through-cycle fleet refresh, acquisition returns to exceed the cost of capital,
resale proceeds to normalize without a damaging equipment-price decline,
leverage to remain controllable, and per-share cash to grow after SBC. A
reasonable scenario range should be built from $1.5B bear, $2.2B base, and
$3.0B bull normalized owner cash—not from peak EBITDA—and should show the
multiple and capital-intensity assumptions separately.

## Scenario and valuation expectation

The September 11, 2026 market snapshot recorded approximately $61.6B of equity
value for United Rentals. Against FY2025 management FCF of $2.181B, the market
was screening at roughly 28x listed FCF before acquisition spending and other
owner claims. The scenario register therefore uses:

| Case | Normalized owner cash | Equity-cash multiple | Implied equity value |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Bear | $1.5B | 18x | $27.0B |
| Base | $2.2B | 24x | $52.8B |
| Bull | $3.0B | 30x | $90.0B |

The base case does not clear the current equity value. The bull case requires
both materially higher normalized cash and a premium multiple, which means the
market is underwriting durable fleet productivity, specialty growth, and
capital discipline rather than merely another strong project year.

## Q4 2025 earnings-call evidence

The preserved Q4 2025 call adds useful operating detail behind the fleet
numbers. Management said that more than 95% of the fleet remained fungible,
meaning it could generally be moved among customer uses rather than being tied
to one project. It also said that high utilization of certain aerial and
telehandler equipment caused the company to sell about $2.73B of used equipment
instead of the roughly $2.8B originally expected. Holding productive equipment
for customers supports rental revenue, but it reduces near-term sale proceeds.

Management described 2025 total capital expenditure as approximately $4.2B,
including about $3.4B of maintenance capital and roughly $0.8B–$0.9B of growth
capital. For 2026, it discussed approximately $1.1B of growth capital focused
on specialty equipment and large projects. These are management definitions,
not independent accounting categories, so the next filing should reconcile
fleet additions, disposals, depreciation, utilization, and actual replacement
needs.

The call also set a 1.5% fleet-productivity hurdle to offset inflation and said
transportation and repositioning costs were pressuring margins. Management
expects positive full-year fleet productivity but acknowledged that quarterly
results can vary materially with large-project timing. That admission directly
supports the dossier's central test: rental rate and time utilization must be
measured together with repositioning cost, fleet age, used-equipment prices,
maintenance capital, and debt. A strong quarterly utilization figure without
the cost of moving and renewing equipment would overstate the economic return.

## Thesis breakers and next filing tests

The thesis weakens if:

- rental rates or utilization fall while fleet additions continue;
- specialty growth increasingly comes from lower-margin ancillary or re-rent
  revenue;
- used-equipment sale proceeds or residual values weaken materially;
- FCF depends on underinvesting in fleet maintenance or branch coverage;
- acquisitions add revenue but not utilization, margin, or cash return;
- restructuring charges recur without measurable cash savings;
- net leverage rises while buybacks and dividends exceed post-fleet cash; or
- contractor, industrial, or infrastructure customers experience credit stress.

The next annual filing should capture fleet age, utilization, rental rate,
productivity, owned versus re-rent mix, gross and net rental capex, sale gains,
maintenance versus growth purchases, acquisition cohorts, debt maturities,
interest cost, SBC-adjusted share count, customer concentration, and cash after
all capital returns.
