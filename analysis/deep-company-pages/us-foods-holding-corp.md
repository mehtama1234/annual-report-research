# US Foods Holding Corp.: Route Density, Vendor Rebates, and the Cash Cost of Feeding Institutions

Date baseline: `2026-09-13`

Evidence base: US Foods fiscal `2025` Form 10-K, the filed Q4 `2025`, Q1 `2026`, and Q2 `2026` results materials and 10-Qs, and the company packet and source ledger at [US Foods company packet](../../extracted/services/retail-grocery-stores/us-foods-holding-corp/company-packet.md). No earnings-call transcript is saved locally, and the company IR host was unavailable when the packet was collected. The quarter evidence therefore comes from filed SEC exhibits and 10-Qs.

## Bottom line

US Foods is a scaled foodservice distributor whose valuable asset is the customer relationship and delivery network, not the food itself. It supplies roughly `250,000` locations through more than `70` distribution facilities, over `6,500` trucks, and more than `90` cash-and-carry locations. Its customers include independent restaurants, chains, hospitals, hotels, schools, government organizations, and other institutional kitchens.

The business improved in fiscal `2025` and the first half of `2026`. But the quality of that improvement must be read carefully. Fiscal `2025` sales increased `4.1%`, while total case volume increased only `1.0%`; food-cost inflation supplied part of the sales growth. Gross-profit dollars grew faster than sales, but the gross margin moved only from `17.3%` to `17.4%`. The company is therefore proving operating discipline and share gains, not proving a structurally high-margin business.

The central investment question is:

> Can US Foods turn route density, procurement scale, private label, and ordering tools into durable owner cash after inventory, fleet, distribution-center, debt, and customer-credit requirements?

My current answer is: the operating model is real and improving, but the equity is not obviously cheap. The September 11, 2026 market snapshot was approximately `$21.1B` of equity value. Against fiscal `2025` reported free cash flow of `$965M`, the market was valuing the company at roughly `21.8x` reported free cash flow. A stricter normalized owner-cash range of `$650M–$1.05B` produces illustrative equity values of `$11.7B–$27.3B` at `18x–26x`. The base case is close to the current market value and requires continued margin discipline, case growth, and working-capital control.

## 1. What the company controls

US Foods controls access, replenishment, and execution for customers that do not want to purchase and manage a broad food inventory themselves. It creates value through five connected activities:

1. It aggregates purchasing demand from many customers.
2. It holds and manages inventory across a broad assortment.
3. It routes products through distribution facilities and a delivery fleet.
4. It provides sales support, chefs, menu assistance, private-label products, and business tools.
5. It uses MOXē and related digital tools to connect ordering and inventory decisions to the customer relationship.

This is a one-segment business, but “one segment” does not mean one simple economic activity. The company has a physical distribution layer, a purchasing and vendor-rebate layer, a customer-service layer, and a digital workflow layer. The physical layer carries most of the cost. The other layers determine whether that cost earns an adequate return.

The control point is the repeated order relationship. A restaurant or hospital can theoretically change suppliers, but changing suppliers requires new price files, product substitutions, delivery schedules, credit arrangements, ordering habits, and kitchen processes. The switching cost is operational rather than contractual. It is meaningful, but it is not absolute.

## 2. What fiscal 2025 actually showed

The filed annual report gives a useful three-year comparison:

| Metric | FY2023 | FY2024 | FY2025 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Net sales | `$35.597B` | `$37.877B` | `$39.424B` |
| Gross profit | `$6.148B` | `$6.534B` | `$6.864B` |
| Operating income | `$1.017B` | `$1.099B` | `$1.199B` |
| Net income | `$506M` | `$494M` | `$676M` |
| Operating cash flow | `$1.140B` | `$1.174B` | `$1.369B` |
| Capital expenditures | `$309M` | `$341M` | `$410M` |
| Reported free cash flow | `$841M` | `$836M` | `$965M` |
| Adjusted EBITDA | `$1.559B` | `$1.741B` | `$1.932B` |

The three-year cash record is better than the net-income record. Operating cash flow remained above `$1.1B` each year, and reported free cash flow stayed between `$836M` and `$965M`. That supports the view that this is a cash-generative operating network rather than an earnings-only story.

It does not remove the main forensic issue: working capital can materially affect cash in a distributor. Inventory, accounts receivable, vendor receivables, accounts payable, food-cost inflation, and payment timing all move around the cash result. A single strong cash-flow year is not enough; the correct test is whether cash remains strong after normalizing inventory and vendor-rebate timing across several years.

## 3. Recent quarter sequence

The latest three reported quarters reinforce the operating improvement:

| Period | Sales | Adjusted EBITDA | Relevant operating signal |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Q4 FY2025 | `$9.8B` | `$490M` | Independent restaurant case volume `+4.1%`; healthcare `+2.9%`; hospitality `+3.1%`; chain volume `-3.5%` |
| Q1 FY2026 | `$9.6B` | `$413M` | Independent restaurant case volume `+4.6%`; healthcare `+3.7%`; hospitality `+5.0%` |
| Q2 FY2026 | `$10.5B` | `$604M` | Independent restaurant case volume `+5.1%`; healthcare `+3.5%`; hospitality `+4.4%`; inventory about `$1.703B` versus `$1.711B` at FY2025 year-end |

The important point is customer mix. Growth is strongest in independent restaurants, healthcare, and hospitality, while chain volume is weaker. This can mean share gains and better local execution. It can also mean the company is more exposed to smaller customers with greater credit and failure risk. The next filings need to show whether case growth remains positive after separating price, customer reclassification, acquisitions, and food-cost inflation.

## 4. Earnings quality and possible financial engineering

There is no evidence here of fraud or misconduct. There are, however, several accounting and financial-engineering areas that deserve repeated reconciliation.

### Vendor consideration

Supplier rebates and incentives reduce inventory cost and then reduce cost of goods sold when the inventory is sold. The company estimates some of this consideration from purchasing activity and vendor agreements. The 2025 Form 10-K identifies vendor consideration as a critical audit matter. Deloitte described testing vendor agreements, subsequent receipts, inventory samples, payment deductions, and independent expectations based on historical ratios.

This accounting is economically understandable, but it creates a timing question. If purchasing accelerates, or if expected rebate rates change, gross margin and inventory values can move before cash is collected. The test is not whether rebates exist; it is whether reported gross-profit improvement is supported by realized vendor consideration and cash receipts rather than increasingly optimistic estimates.

### LIFO and food-cost effects

US Foods includes LIFO reserve adjustments in its adjusted EBITDA reconciliation. LIFO is an inventory-cost method in which the cost assigned to goods sold can reflect more recent purchase costs. In a changing food-cost environment, the reported gross margin can be affected by the timing of price changes, inventory turns, and reserve adjustments.

The company also reported that fiscal 2025 sales growth was driven partly by `2.6%` food-cost inflation. That means a portion of nominal revenue growth does not represent more meals delivered or more customer relationships. Volume, price, mix, and vendor funding must remain separate in the analysis.

### Adjusted EBITDA exclusions

US Foods excludes restructuring and impairment charges, share-based compensation, LIFO reserve adjustments, debt-extinguishment losses, business-transformation costs, pension settlement effects, and other specified items from adjusted EBITDA. Some exclusions may be genuinely unusual. Others can recur in a distribution network that is continuously changing facilities, systems, routes, and administrative structure.

The proper rule is simple: an item is not economically one-time merely because management labels it one-time. The next filing should show whether restructuring, transformation, impairment, and share-based compensation remain small relative to the cash generated by the business.

### Cash flow quality

Fiscal 2025 operating cash flow was `$1.369B`, and capital expenditures were `$410M`, producing reported free cash flow of `$965M`. That is a strong conversion of adjusted EBITDA, but it is not the same as cash available for shareholders without qualification. The company still has debt service, fleet and facility renewal, systems spending, working-capital needs, leases, and possible acquisition spending.

The 2025 balance sheet had approximately `$2.026B` of accounts receivable, `$173M` of vendor receivables, `$1.711B` of inventory, and `$5.2B` of total debt. Cash was only about `$41M`. This is a cash-generative but leveraged operating model, not a cash-rich balance sheet.

## 5. Capital requirements and balance-sheet resilience

The physical network requires continuing investment. Fiscal 2025 capital expenditures were `$410M`, compared with `$341M` in 2024 and `$309M` in 2023. The annual report describes spending on information technology, property and equipment, and distribution-center construction and improvements.

Leases are also material. The company disclosed approximately `$201M` of leased assets obtained in exchange for financing lease liabilities during fiscal 2025 and `$109M` obtained through operating lease liabilities. Those non-cash additions do not appear as ordinary capital expenditures in the same way as cash purchases, but they represent access to assets that the business must economically pay for.

Debt was approximately `$5.2B` at fiscal year-end, and net interest expense was `$305M` in fiscal 2025. Interest expense declined from `$315M` in 2024, but the debt burden remains important because the company operates on a low operating margin. A one-percentage-point change in a margin on `$39.4B` of sales is approximately `$394M` before tax; the same scale shows why small operating changes can matter more than the revenue growth headline.

Liquidity is therefore dependent on three things staying aligned:

- customers paying on time;
- inventory and vendor terms remaining controlled; and
- adjusted EBITDA remaining high enough to cover interest, maintenance spending, and debt reduction.

## 6. Alden lens: macro, liquidity, and durability

The macro exposure is indirect but substantial. US Foods does not primarily sell discretionary consumer goods to households. It sells inputs to businesses whose own demand depends on restaurant traffic, hotel occupancy, healthcare utilization, education budgets, and government meal programs.

The relevant macro questions are:

- Do consumers continue purchasing food away from home, even if they trade down in menu choice?
- Can independent restaurants survive higher labor, occupancy, insurance, and financing costs?
- Do hospitals and institutions preserve meal-service spending during budget pressure?
- Does food inflation increase nominal revenue while reducing customer affordability and case volume?
- Does a higher-rate environment increase US Foods’ interest burden or reduce acquisition flexibility?

The durability is strongest where US Foods is embedded in recurring institutional demand and weakest where the customer is a financially fragile independent operator. Independent restaurant case growth is positive, but that metric should not be treated as proof that the underlying customer base is healthy. Customer closures, credit losses, delivery density, and route profitability matter more than aggregate cases alone.

## 7. Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers

The narrative is that national scale plus local execution, private label, digital ordering, and procurement discipline will produce durable margin expansion. To make that narrative investable, it must become a small number of measurable assumptions:

1. **Revenue growth:** case growth separated from food-cost inflation, price, mix, customer reclassification, and acquisitions.
2. **Margin:** gross profit and operating income growth that survives vendor-rebate normalization and LIFO effects.
3. **Reinvestment:** capital expenditures, lease additions, distribution-center spending, fleet replacement, and technology spending required to sustain the network.
4. **Working capital:** normalized inventory, receivable, vendor-rebate, and payable requirements as sales and food costs change.
5. **Risk:** customer failure, chain-volume weakness, labor and fuel costs, interest expense, food-cost volatility, and debt refinancing.
6. **Lifecycle:** a mature broadline distributor should not receive a high-growth multiple merely because adjusted EBITDA is improving from a prior efficiency program.

The base case should therefore use a normalized cash range, not the highest recent adjusted EBITDA. The company needs to demonstrate that higher margin is structural and not mainly the result of favorable food-cost timing, vendor rebates, temporary working-capital release, or cost cuts that cannot be repeated.

## 8. Valuation and market-implied expectations

The September 11, 2026 market snapshot showed approximately `$21.07B` of equity value. The following is an illustrative equity-value screen, not a complete discounted-cash-flow valuation:

| Case | Normalized owner cash | Equity-cash multiple | Implied equity value | What must be true |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Bear | `$650M` | `18x` | `$11.7B` | Case growth weakens, debt remains meaningful, and margin improvement proves partly cyclical or timing-related |
| Base | `$850M` | `22x` | `$18.7B` | Independent, healthcare, and hospitality growth continues; working capital stays controlled; margin gains persist |
| Bull | `$1.05B` | `26x` | `$27.3B` | US Foods gains share, private label and digital tools deepen retention, and debt falls without sacrificing network investment |

At the market snapshot, the company was valued at about `21.8x` fiscal 2025 reported free cash flow. That is not an extreme multiple for a durable compounder, but US Foods still has a low operating margin, meaningful debt, and a physically intensive network. The market is paying for continued execution rather than merely for the existence of food demand.

The base case does not create a large margin of safety. The bull case requires durable improvements in customer retention, mix, procurement, and cash conversion. The bear case shows that a modest normalization of owner cash and multiple can leave substantial downside even when the company remains profitable.

## 9. Management incentives and capital allocation

Management has incentives to emphasize adjusted EBITDA, adjusted EPS, case growth, private-label penetration, and margin expansion. Those metrics are useful, but each can improve without creating equal value for common shareholders:

- adjusted EBITDA can exclude recurring restructuring, transformation, and share-based compensation;
- case growth can rise while food-cost inflation or customer mix hides weak real demand;
- private-label penetration can improve margins but increase inventory and product-liability exposure;
- share repurchases can reduce share count but also consume cash that could reduce `$5.2B` of debt;
- acquisitions can add sales and cases while weakening returns if integration and working-capital needs are understated.

Fiscal 2025 included approximately `$926M` of common-stock repurchases plus `$50M` of unsettled accelerated repurchase activity, while the company ended the year with only about `$41M` of cash and approximately `$5.2B` of debt. That allocation can be rational if cash generation is durable and leverage is controlled. It deserves scrutiny because repurchasing shares at a high market value while carrying substantial debt can transfer risk to remaining shareholders.

## 10. Who captures the economics and who carries the burden?

| Layer | Captures value | Carries the burden |
|---|---|---|
| Purchasing scale and vendor programs | US Foods and, indirectly, customers through better sourcing | Vendor-rebate estimation, inventory valuation, and supplier dependence |
| Route density and facilities | US Foods through delivery efficiency and customer retention | Fleet, labor, fuel, facilities, maintenance, and utilization risk |
| Independent restaurant relationship | US Foods through share gains and repeat orders | Customer credit risk and closures; route density can deteriorate if customers fail |
| Private label | US Foods through potentially better margin and retention | Product quality, recalls, inventory, brand investment, and substitution risk |
| MOXē and business tools | US Foods through workflow embedment and data-assisted retention | Technology spending, adoption risk, cybersecurity, and uncertain incremental return |
| Debt-funded capital returns | Existing shareholders when repurchases are value-accretive | Remaining shareholders if cash generation weakens while debt remains |

The cleaner economics sit in recurring customer relationships, procurement density, private label, and workflow tools. The heavy burdens sit in inventory, delivery labor, facilities, fleet, customer credit, and debt. US Foods is attractive only if the relationship layer earns enough to cover the physical layer through a full cycle.

## 11. Next-filing watchlist and thesis breakers

The next filings should answer these questions:

1. Does case growth remain positive after separating food-cost inflation, price, mix, acquisitions, and customer reclassification?
2. Does independent-restaurant growth continue without a rise in bad-debt expense, customer closures, or delivery-cost pressure?
3. Do gross-profit dollars and gross margin improve without unusually favorable vendor-rebate estimates or LIFO effects?
4. Does inventory grow more slowly than sales, and do vendor receivables and accounts receivable convert to cash without a later reversal?
5. Does operating cash flow remain close to or above reported free cash flow after normalizing working capital?
6. Are capital expenditures and lease additions sufficient to maintain fleet, facilities, technology, safety, and delivery reliability?
7. Does debt decline while the company continues repurchases, or are capital returns being funded by leverage and temporary cash releases?
8. Do restructuring, transformation, impairment, and share-based compensation exclusions remain recurring?
9. Does MOXē increase customer retention, order frequency, or margin in a way that is visible in financial results rather than only in adoption statistics?

The thesis breaks if case growth weakens, independent-customer credit losses rise, gross margin reverses, inventory and receivables consume cash, or debt remains high while management continues repurchases. A second break would be evidence that digital tools and private label improve reported metrics without improving return on the capital required to operate the network.

## 12. Cohort conclusion

US Foods strengthens the foodservice and institutional-replenishment cohort because it shows the same economic structure as healthcare distributors but with a different demand source. The company owns a repeated operating relationship, absorbs procurement and inventory complexity, and charges a narrow spread on very large product flows. It can improve its position through route density, local service, private label, and digital workflow. It cannot escape the need to fund trucks, warehouses, labor, working capital, quality control, and debt.

The key distinction from a cleaner asset-light intermediary is that US Foods must continuously renew and operate a physical network. The company deserves credit for stable three-year free cash flow and recent case growth. It does not deserve a high-quality multiple unless that cash survives normal working-capital conditions, required fleet and facility spending, customer failures, and debt service.
