# Ventas Inc.: Senior-Housing Scarcity, Operating NOI, and the Cost of Funding Growth

Date baseline: `2026-09-14`

Evidence base: Ventas fiscal `2025` Form 10-K, filed Q4 `2025`, Q1 `2026`, and Q2 `2026` materials, and the [Ventas company packet](../../extracted/real-estate/reit-healthcare-facilities/ventas-inc/company-packet.md). No earnings-call transcript is saved locally. The latest quarter evidence is anchored to the filed Q2 `2026` Form 10-Q and results exhibit.

## Bottom line

Ventas owns and finances healthcare-related real estate, with particular emphasis on senior housing. Its economic control point is not healthcare treatment itself. It is access to scarce, well-located communities and outpatient properties, combined with the capital and operating relationships needed to keep those properties occupied and productive.

The strongest part of the thesis is real: senior-housing demand is improving, new supply is limited, and Ventas’s senior-housing operating portfolio produced strong same-store cash NOI growth. In Q2 `2026`, normalized FFO per share was about `$0.97`, total company NOI grew about `17%`, total same-store cash NOI grew about `10%`, and SHOP same-store cash NOI grew about `16%`. Year-to-date investments reached about `$3.4B`, and management raised 2026 investment expectations to about `$4.5B`.

The main risk is that growth requires capital, operating execution, and new equity. Ventas had approximately `$12.7B` of debt at June 30, 2026, net debt to further adjusted EBITDA of about `4.7x`, and about `$4.9B` of liquidity. During the first half of 2026 it entered into equity forward sales for roughly `34.7M` shares and gross proceeds of about `$3.0B`. The investment program can create value, but existing shareholders do not receive the full benefit if the required capital is raised at unattractive prices or if property-level returns fail to exceed the financing and dilution cost.

This is a high-quality demographic and operating recovery case, not a simple defensive-yield case.

## 1. Define the business before valuing it

Ventas operates through three main property and operating models:

- **SHOP:** senior housing operating portfolio. Ventas owns the communities and engages operators to run them. The company is exposed to occupancy, rates, labor, food, insurance, resident demand, and operator execution.
- **OM&R:** outpatient medical and research properties. The company owns, develops, acquires, leases, and manages outpatient medical buildings and research centers. The cash flow is closer to landlord rent, although tenant quality and medical demand still matter.
- **NNN:** triple-net properties. Tenants generally pay property-level operating costs and capital expenditures under the leases. This reduces direct operating burden but increases dependence on tenant credit and lease economics.

Normalized FFO is a common REIT performance measure. It starts with net income, adds back real-estate depreciation and amortization, removes gains or losses on real-estate sales, and then excludes additional items defined by management. It is useful for comparing property operations, but it is not the same as cash available to common owners. It does not automatically deduct every recurring property renewal cost, investment requirement, debt need, or dilution effect.

That distinction is central to Ventas. The company is partly a landlord, partly an operating platform, and partly a capital allocator. Each part has a different cash and risk profile.

## 2. Three-year financial record

The filed annual materials report the following normalized FFO attributable to common stockholders:

| Metric | FY2023 | FY2024 | FY2025 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Net income attributable to common stockholders | `($41M)` | `$81M` | `$251M` |
| Normalized FFO attributable to common stockholders | `$1.212B` | `$1.327B` | `$1.610B` |
| Normalized FFO per diluted share | `$2.99` | `$3.19` | `$3.48` |
| Normalized FFO per-share growth | — | `6.7%` | `9.1%` |

The increase is meaningful, but the bridge must be read correctly. Real-estate depreciation makes GAAP net income a poor measure of recurring property operations, so FFO is useful. At the same time, FFO adds back non-cash depreciation while the properties still require maintenance, redevelopment, and investment. The correct question is whether FFO growth remains after recurring capital requirements and after the equity used to fund acquisitions and developments is included.

| Period | Normalized FFO per share | Same-store / operating signal | Capital signal |
|---|---:|---|---|

The quarter sequence supports a real operating improvement. It does not prove that every dollar of investment is accretive. It shows that current properties are producing stronger operating income while management is deploying substantial capital into additional senior-housing exposure.

## 4. What controls the relationship?

Ventas controls the property, the capital structure, and in SHOP the economic platform around the community. It does not control every part of resident demand or care delivery.

The customer relationship is divided:

- residents and families choose the community and pay or arrange payment;
- operators provide staffing, programming, food, care coordination, and daily service;
- Ventas owns or finances the physical setting and determines capital allocation;
- referral sources, local supply, and family affordability influence occupancy;
- lenders and equity investors determine the cost and availability of growth capital.

This division matters because a strong property owner can still suffer if operators cannot hire enough staff, if rates rise faster than resident affordability, or if the community requires capital that does not earn an adequate return.

## 5. Operating economics: SHOP is the key contradiction

SHOP provides the most growth and the most operating risk. Strong same-store cash NOI can result from several sources:

- higher occupancy;
- higher resident fees;
- higher acuity or service mix;
- improved staffing and operating productivity;
- lower temporary expenses;
- acquisitions or conversions that later enter the same-store pool.

These sources have different durability. Higher occupancy in a supply-constrained market can be durable. Higher rates may be limited by family affordability. Higher acuity can increase revenue and cost at the same time. Staffing improvement can raise service quality but may require higher wages.

The same-store definition also matters. Ventas excludes or delays properties affected by acquisitions, redevelopment, operator transitions, or significant disruptions until they meet its comparability rules. Same-store results are useful, but they do not show the full capital required to produce growth across the whole portfolio.

The next filings must separate occupancy, average monthly revenue per occupied room, labor expense, agency labor, food and insurance costs, and resident turnover. A high same-store NOI percentage without this bridge is incomplete evidence.

## 6. OM&R and NNN provide different quality, not simply lower risk

OM&R and NNN can make the portfolio look more stable because they rely more on rent and less on direct operating performance. But the risk moves rather than disappears.

For OM&R, the questions are tenant credit, medical-practice demand, research funding, lease rollover, development costs, and property obsolescence.

For NNN, tenants generally pay property expenses and capital expenditures. That lowers Ventas’s direct operating cost, but tenants may seek rent relief or fail to fund obligations if their own operating economics weaken. Ventas may then have to provide loans or advances for capital expenditures, increasing its effective exposure.

The cleanest economics occur when a strong tenant or operator pays rent and funds property needs while the asset remains scarce and useful. The burden is heavier when the landlord must fund capital to preserve the tenant’s ability to pay.

## 7. Earnings quality and normalization questions

Ventas defines normalized FFO by excluding gains and losses on derivatives, non-cash tax effects, debt-extinguishment costs, transaction and restructuring costs, amortization of other intangibles, non-cash stock compensation, disruptive events, impairments, contingent consideration, and selected items related to noncontrolling interests and unconsolidated entities.

These exclusions are not automatically improper. They require classification:

- Real-estate depreciation is a legitimate non-cash charge for a property portfolio, but it does not mean buildings require no replacement capital.
- Gains on property sales are not recurring rent and should not support a recurring FFO conclusion.
- Transaction costs can be unusual in one year but recurring in a capital allocator that continuously acquires, develops, and restructures assets.
- Stock compensation is non-cash at grant but dilutes common owners or consumes value when new shares are issued.
- Disruptive events and impairments may be irregular, but recurring operational disruption or asset weakness is not economically one-time.

The strongest forensic question is whether normalized FFO per share rises after considering property renewal, development, acquisitions, financing costs, and share issuance. Total FFO can grow while per-share value stagnates if new capital is raised too frequently or at too low a return.

## Cash conversion and owner-cash bridge

For Ventas, the useful bridge is:

`property rent and SHOP operating cash -> property operating costs and operator support -> recurring renewal capital -> interest and debt requirements -> acquisitions and development -> equity issuance and dilution -> common-owner residual`

Normalized FFO is a property-operations denominator, not the final owner-cash
number. The Q2 2026 operating improvement is meaningful, but the first-half
investment program reached about `$3.4B`, 2026 investment expectations rose to
about `$4.5B`, and the company used forward equity structures for roughly
`34.7M` shares. Those uses may create value when stabilized property returns
exceed the cost of capital; they reduce the cash available per existing share
when they do not.

The next filing bridge should reconcile normalized FFO to cash after recurring
property capital, operator or tenant support, interest, debt repayment, and
dilution. A higher FFO figure is not enough if the company must continually
raise capital to preserve the portfolio or fund growth.

## 8. Capital requirements and balance-sheet resilience

Ventas’s growth model requires several forms of capital:

- acquisitions of senior-housing communities;
- development and redevelopment;
- property improvements and tenant advances;
- working capital and loans receivable;
- debt refinancing;
- equity issuance and forward equity structures.

At June 30, 2026, total debt was about `$12.7B`. Total assets were about `$29.7B`. Liquidity was about `$4.9B`, and net debt to further adjusted EBITDA was about `4.7x` according to the packet.

The company also disclosed that during the first half of 2026 it entered into equity forward sales for approximately `34.7M` common shares for gross proceeds of about `$3.0B`, at an average price near `$86.02` per share. It settled approximately `31.4M` shares under outstanding forward contracts for net proceeds of about `$2.5B`.

This financing can be sensible when the acquired assets produce returns above the cost of equity. It becomes value-destructive when the company issues equity to buy assets whose stabilized returns are below the return demanded by existing shareholders.

## 9. Alden lens: demographic durability versus funding liquidity

The demographic demand is durable, but the equity return is still sensitive to funding conditions.

Senior housing can benefit from aging populations, limited new supply, and family demand for safety and support. These forces help occupancy and pricing.

The macro risks are:

- interest rates that raise the cost of debt and reduce property values;
- equity markets that make forward issuance more dilutive;
- labor inflation that reduces SHOP margins;
- recession or housing weakness that reduces family ability to pay;
- insurance and property-tax inflation;
- operator stress that transfers capital needs back to Ventas;
- policy or reimbursement changes affecting residents and care operators.

Ventas can have strong properties and still experience weak equity returns if capital access tightens while investment opportunities require funding. Liquidity and debt maturity management therefore belong in the core thesis, not in a separate risk section.

## 10. Damodaran lens: translate the demographic story into returns

The narrative is “aging plus low supply creates long-term senior-housing growth.” The numbers that must follow are:

1. occupancy and move-in rates;
2. revenue per occupied room and pricing power;
3. labor, food, insurance, and property-level operating costs;
4. same-store cash NOI after normal operating expenses;
5. acquisition and development yields after integration and stabilization costs;
6. recurring capital expenditure and tenant advances;
7. cost of debt and cost of equity;
8. FFO per share after dilution;
9. net asset value relative to the share price;
10. dividend coverage after recurring property needs.

The growth rate is valuable only if the return on incremental invested capital exceeds the blended cost of the debt and equity used to fund it. A low-supply market can support attractive growth while a poorly priced acquisition destroys value. These are separate questions.

## 11. Valuation: use FFO per share and NAV, not a generic owner-cash multiple

The September 11, 2026 market snapshot showed a share price near `$89.99` and equity value near `$46.2B`. The 2025 normalized FFO per share was `$3.48`, so the market was valuing the company at approximately `25.9x` 2025 normalized FFO. The company’s 2026 guidance midpoint was approximately `$3.83` per share, which implies roughly `23.5x` that forward measure.

Illustrative per-share cases:

| Case | Normalized FFO per share | FFO multiple | Implied share value | Required condition |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Bear | `$3.25` | `20x` | `$65` | SHOP growth slows, labor costs rise, capital becomes more expensive, and dilution offsets part of FFO growth |
| Base | `$3.80` | `24x` | `$91` | Occupancy and SHOP NOI remain strong, investment returns are acceptable, leverage stays controlled, and per-share growth continues |
| Bull | `$4.35` | `27x` | `$117` | Low supply persists, investment returns remain high, operator execution improves, and equity issuance is accretive |

This screen is not a complete NAV valuation. A serious underwrite must separately value owned real estate, unconsolidated interests, loans, debt, noncontrolling interests, and the operating platform. The screen shows the market expectation: the current price already assumes sustained FFO growth and a relatively strong multiple.

## 12. Management incentives and capital allocation

Management is evaluated through FFO, normalized FFO, same-store NOI, investment volume, leverage, liquidity, and per-share growth. These are relevant measures, but they can encourage behavior that requires careful review:

- investment volume can rise even when marginal returns decline;
- normalized FFO can exclude transaction and stock-compensation costs;
- same-store metrics can improve while new properties require significant capital;
- equity issuance can fund growth while reducing each existing share’s claim;
- asset sales can support liquidity but reduce future income;
- dividends can satisfy REIT distribution requirements while limiting retained capital.

The correct capital-allocation test is per-share value after the entire financing chain. More properties and more total FFO are not enough.

## 13. Who controls the value and who carries the burden?

| Layer | Cleaner value capture | Main burden |
|---|---|---|
| Senior-housing scarcity | Ventas and operators with available, well-located communities | Construction scarcity, property maintenance, local competition, and financing |
| SHOP operating platform | Ventas when occupancy, rates, and labor productivity improve together | Staffing, food, insurance, resident service, operator quality, and operating volatility |
| OM&R medical properties | Landlord rent and tenant retention | Lease rollover, medical demand, tenant credit, and building obsolescence |
| NNN properties | Contracted rent with limited direct property expense | Tenant health, rent relief, capital advances, and refinancing |
| Acquisitions and development | Potentially higher FFO and portfolio scale | Purchase price, integration, stabilization, debt, equity issuance, and execution |
| Capital markets access | Liquidity and ability to fund growth | Interest cost, dilution, property values, and market dependence |

The real burden carrier is not only the property owner. Operators carry labor and service risk; residents and families carry affordability risk; lenders and shareholders carry financing risk. Ventas captures the property and capital-allocation economics only if the operating system remains healthy.

## 14. Next-filing watchlist and thesis breakers

1. Does SHOP same-store cash NOI remain strong after separating occupancy, rates, labor, food, insurance, and operator changes?
2. Does occupancy improve without a material increase in concessions, bad debt, or resident turnover?
3. Do new investments produce returns above the cost of debt and equity after stabilization and recurring capital spending?
4. Does normalized FFO per share grow faster than the diluted share count?
5. Does net debt to further adjusted EBITDA remain controlled as the investment program expands?
6. Do tenant advances, loans receivable, and capital funding obligations increase faster than rent and NOI?
7. Does the company continue excluding transaction, restructuring, stock compensation, and disruptive costs that recur each year?
8. Do OM&R and NNN tenants remain able to pay contractual rent and fund required capital?
9. Does the dividend remain covered by recurring cash after property renewal and financing costs?
10. Does NAV per share support the market price after debt, noncontrolling interests, and dilution are included?

The thesis breaks if same-store NOI weakens because of labor or occupancy pressure, if operators require repeated support, if acquisition yields fall below funding costs, or if per-share FFO stops growing despite higher total investment and total FFO.

## Conclusion

Ventas is a strong example of how an aging population can create an investable property and operating system. The demand is not automatically a moat. The moat depends on scarce supply, location, operator quality, occupancy, service execution, and capital access.

The cleanest conclusion is narrower than “healthcare real estate wins.” Ventas can create durable value when it owns scarce senior-housing capacity and funds investments at returns above its financing cost. The company becomes vulnerable when growth depends on continual equity issuance, when operators cannot carry labor and service costs, or when normalized FFO hides recurring property and financing requirements.
