# Vistra Corp. — merchant reliability, hedge books, and the owner-cash test

As of 2026-09-13. FY2025 figures are filed GAAP figures unless explicitly identified otherwise; dollar amounts are in millions.

## How to read this memo

In this memo, a control point means the specific customer relationship, asset,
or process that gives a company the ability to earn money. GAAP means the accounting rules used in the filed financial statements.
Operating cash flow is cash produced by day-to-day operations. Capital
expenditure is cash spent on productive assets such as power plants and
equipment. A hedge is a contract intended to reduce future price risk; its
reported value can change before the related cash is received or paid. The
residual below is a screening calculation, not Vistra’s management-defined
adjusted free-cash-flow measure.

## Investment question

Vistra is the merchant-power comparison case for regulated utilities and for
Constellation’s nuclear-focused platform. It combines retail load,
dispatchable generation, nuclear assets, hedges, PPAs, and acquisitions. The
question is whether that combination converts power scarcity into durable
common-equity cash after fuel, nuclear obligations, fleet maintenance,
acquisition spending, collateral, debt, and capital returns.

## What the filing reveals

- Vistra owns a real control point—dispatchable generation in constrained power
  markets—but monetizes it through market prices, hedges, retail load, PPAs,
  and asset transactions rather than approved rate-base returns.
- The filing’s capital-expenditure definition is economically important. Nuclear
  fuel and long-term service prepayments are included with PP&E spending, which
  makes a superficial “capex-light” adjusted-FCF claim especially dangerous.
- Management’s 2025 materials cite approximately 100% generation hedged for
  2026, 84% for 2027, and 58% for 2028. Coverage reduces spot-price risk but
  does not eliminate basis, collateral, counterparty, volume, outage, or
  reinvestment risk.
- The annual filing describes Energy Harbor’s nuclear generation and the 2025
  acquisition of 2,557 MW of natural-gas facilities. The platform is therefore
  expanding through acquisition while simultaneously carrying nuclear and gas
  fleet obligations.
- The company’s large-load and nuclear PPA narrative can improve visibility,
  but the customer contract must be tested against full fleet maintenance,
  fuel, outage, reliability, and financing costs.

## Forensic earnings-quality questions

### 1. Is adjusted FCFbG being mistaken for common owner cash?

The $3.592B 2025 Ongoing Operations Adjusted FCFbG figure is useful as a
management operating measure, but it is not interchangeable with cash after
all growth acquisitions and capital commitments. The filed bridge leaves $178M
after capex and Lotus, versus $1.336B returned to shareholders. Reconcile the
adjusted measure to GAAP OCF, nuclear fuel, LTSA prepayments, acquisitions,
collateral, debt service, and common cash before assigning it a valuation
multiple.

### 2. Do hedge marks and settlements tell the same story?

The 2025 materials identify an unrealized hedge loss expected to settle in
future years of approximately $808M. A hedge can protect future cash while
making current GAAP earnings look worse, or it can create a mark that has not
yet become cash. Track realized settlements, collateral postings, contracted
volumes, and physical generation together.

### 3. Does acquisition growth create incremental cash?

Lotus consumed $1.140B in 2025 and Energy Harbor consumed $3.065B in 2024.
The proper cohort test is post-integration cash after maintenance capital,
fuel, hedging, debt, and lost or transferred generation—not EBITDA acquired at
closing. Cogentrix and future digital-infrastructure transactions increase the
need for this discipline.

### 4. Who carries reliability and retirement costs?

The $4.216B asset-retirement obligation is a reminder that a fleet is not only
a dispatchable revenue machine. Nuclear decommissioning, environmental
compliance, coal retirement, fuel, safety, and site remediation can absorb
cash long after a generation asset is described as “existing.”

### 5. Are capital returns funded by the business or by market confidence?

2025 dividends and repurchases exceeded the simple post-capex/acquisition
residual by more than $1.1B. Test whether the gap was funded by debt, asset
sales, working-capital release, hedge collateral movements, or genuinely
recurring cash. A buyback is not evidence of owner cash if the balance sheet is
funding it during an expansion cohort.

## Incentives, hedge quality, and cohort position

Vistra's adjusted FCFbG and capital-return narrative can reward a larger
reported cash figure even when the company is using cash for nuclear fuel,
long-term service agreements, acquisitions, collateral, or fleet renewal. The
proper incentive test is whether normalized cash after all of those claims can
fund debt reduction and capital returns without relying on asset sales or a
new financing cycle.

Hedge coverage should be evaluated by realized cash and risk transfer, not by
the percentage of generation labeled hedged. A hedge can protect price while
still requiring collateral, leaving basis and volume risk, and delaying the
cash benefit until a future settlement. The next filings should connect hedge
marks to settlement cash, physical output, counterparty exposure, and the
maintenance and fuel cost of the fleet supporting the contract.

Vistra belongs in the [utility rate-base, merchant-power, and large-load
first-principles essay](../first-principles/utility-rate-base-merchant-power-and-large-load.md)
and the [power cohort comparison](../cross-sector/nuclear-scarcity-versus-regulated-and-merchant-power-forensic-comparison-2026-09-13.md).
Compared with Alliant, it has more market-price and hedge exposure; compared
with NRG, it has a heavier generation and nuclear-obligation denominator;
compared with Constellation, its portfolio is less centered on nuclear scarcity
and more dependent on fleet mix, retail, and acquisition execution.

## Lyn Alden / Damodaran / forensic synthesis

- **Lyn Alden lens:** Vistra owns scarce physical capacity and benefits from
  tighter reliability conditions, but it is exposed to rates, collateral,
  leverage, commodity cycles, and the capital intensity of keeping the fleet
  available.
- **Damodaran lens:** separate retail customer economics, nuclear scarcity,
  merchant gas, contracted PPAs, hedge-protected forward cash, and acquisition
  options. Do not capitalize all adjusted EBITDA as one mature annuity.
- **Forensic lens:** the central category errors are treating adjusted FCFbG as
  common owner cash, hedge marks as settled economics, acquisition EBITDA as
  organic growth, and capital returns as proof that the cash denominator is
  durable.

## Valuation framing

The 2026-09-11 market snapshot was approximately $50.3B of equity value at
$148.38 per share. That is roughly 283x the $178M mechanical post-capex and
Lotus-acquisition residual. A normalized range of $1.0B / $2.0B / $3.5B at
20x / 25x / 30x implies $20B / $50B / $105B. The market therefore requires
normalized cash near or above $2B, sustained reliability pricing, successful
acquisition integration, and a capital structure that can support the fleet.

## Thesis breakers and next checks

1. Hedge coverage protects price but not cash because settlements, collateral,
   outages, or basis movements consume liquidity.
2. Lotus, Energy Harbor, or Cogentrix fails to earn returns after maintenance,
   fuel, integration, and debt costs.
3. Power and capacity prices normalize while the market continues to value
   peak-cycle adjusted EBITDA.
4. Nuclear, gas, coal-retirement, or environmental obligations rise faster than
   contracted pricing and fleet availability.
5. Retail affordability, customer churn, or counterparty losses weaken the
   load-side hedge against generation exposure.
6. Repurchases continue while filed residual cash remains below dividends and
   buybacks, increasing leverage or reducing resilience.

Next filing checks: realized hedge cash versus marks; collateral; generation
availability; nuclear fuel and LTSA spending; post-Lotus cash; Cogentrix
purchase price and financing; retail receivable aging; debt maturities; asset
retirement obligations; and capital returns after all investment spending.
