# Waystar Holding Corp. — healthcare payment workflow, transaction volume, and owner cash

Date baseline: 2026-09-14  
Primary evidence: [Waystar company packet](../../extracted/technology/healthcare-information-services/waystar-holding-corp/company-packet.md), [Waystar FY2025 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1990354/000199035426000011/way-20251231.htm), and [Waystar source ledger](../../extracted/technology/healthcare-information-services/waystar-holding-corp/source-ledger.md)

## Investment question

Waystar provides software and payment workflows used by healthcare providers,
payers, and patients. It connects claims, payment, denial, eligibility, and
patient-balance processes across healthcare organizations. The important
question is not whether the software is used. It is whether the company can
keep a meaningful share of the value after payment-processing costs,
implementation, support, regulation, acquisitions, debt, stock compensation,
and the possibility that healthcare transaction volume grows more slowly than
the installed customer base.

The evidence supports a valuable and expanding workflow position. It does not
yet support treating all revenue as equally recurring, all adjusted EBITDA as
owner cash, or acquisition spending as optional.

## What Waystar controls

The operating sequence is:

`provider or patient event -> eligibility, claim, payment, or denial workflow -> transaction and data processing -> collection or reimbursement -> subscription and volume fee -> cash`

Waystar controls software connections, workflow rules, payment coordination,
and the data path between healthcare participants. It does not control the
patient's ability to pay, the payer's reimbursement policy, the provider's
financial health, or the underlying medical service. Those parties carry much
of the economic and regulatory burden.

The 2025 filing describes provider solutions as approximately 70% of total
revenue. Subscription revenue reflects recurring provider-count fees and
minimum amounts owed. Volume-based revenue reflects fees tied to transaction
count or dollar volume above minimums. Patient-payment solutions have higher
third-party costs: the filing says those costs are approximately 60% of
associated revenue, versus approximately 6% to 8% for provider solutions.
This mix difference is essential when interpreting revenue growth and gross
margin.

## Definitions

- **Subscription revenue** is the recurring fee for access, provider counts,
  or contractual minimums. It is recurring, but renewal and implementation
  still have to be tested.
- **Volume-based revenue** is linked to claims, transactions, or patient
  payment dollars. It can grow with customer activity without an equivalent
  increase in customer count.
- **Net revenue retention (NRR)** measures revenue from an existing customer
  cohort after expansion, contraction, and churn. It is useful, but it does
  not show cash margin, acquisition cost, or customer concentration.
- **Adjusted EBITDA** excludes interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization,
  stock compensation, acquisition and integration costs, debt-amendment costs,
  IPO costs, and other items. It is a management measure, not a cash-flow
  statement.
- **Owner cash** here means operating cash after recurring capital spending,
  before discretionary acquisitions and capital returns. Acquisition cash is
  shown separately because the company uses acquisitions to expand its
  product and customer base.

in claim and payment activity can weaken the volume component while customer
counts remain stable.

The recurring-capital residual is useful for describing the software business,

debt paydowns, and working-capital changes. Those benefits should be separated
from durable unit economics.

## Earnings quality and financial-engineering risks

### Adjusted EBITDA excludes real claims on the business

FY2025 GAAP net income was `$112.1M` while adjusted EBITDA was `$462.1M`.
The reconciliation included `$140.5M` of depreciation and amortization,
`$42.1M` of stock compensation, `$21.1M` of acquisition and integration costs,
and other exclusions. Depreciation and amortization partly reflects acquired
intangible assets, but acquisition accounting does not make the underlying
replacement cost disappear. Stock compensation is a recurring transfer of
value to employees if the company continues to use equity to pay for labor.

The test is not whether each adjustment is permitted. The test is whether the
cash earnings after employee dilution, recurring integration work, debt
service, and replacement acquisitions justify the valuation.

### Subscription growth can hide mix and margin changes

Subscription revenue is the cleaner recurring component. Volume-based revenue
can be economically attractive for provider workflows, but patient-payment
revenue carries much higher third-party costs. Track revenue by provider versus
patient solutions, subscription versus volume, gross profit by solution, and
NRR by customer type. A rising NRR with declining contribution margin is not
the same outcome as rising NRR with stable cash margin.

### Acquisitions are part of the strategy, not an exception

Waystar acquired Iodine in 2025 for `$629.5M` of cash and issued about `$620.8M`
of stock in the transaction. Iodine contributed only `$31.0M` of 2025 revenue
and was excluded from the internal-control assessment for the acquisition year.
The acquisition may create a valuable product and cross-selling opportunity,
but the return must be measured against the cash, shares, integration cost,
new amortization, and control risk.

### Working capital deserves a direct reconciliation

Accounts receivable rose to `$177.0M` from `$145.2M` while revenue rose 16.5%.
Deferred revenue rose sharply to `$67.9M` current plus `$5.5M` long term from
about `$16.3M` in total. That can provide cash and may reflect contract timing,
but the next filings should show whether collections, billings, implementation,
and renewal support the increase. The company also had an `$80M` receivables
facility, so cash conversion should be reviewed together with the financing
of receivables.

## Balance-sheet resilience and capital requirements

At December 31, 2025, Waystar had `$61.4M` of cash, `$24.9M` of investment
securities, and `$1.481B` of outstanding debt before debt issuance costs. The
first-lien facility had a 5.69% effective rate and most principal was due at
maturity in 2029. Debt was lower than the pre-IPO level, but it remains a
material claim on cash generation and limits the margin for acquisition error.

The company also carried `$4.017B` of goodwill and `$1.293B` of intangible
assets after Iodine. These balances are not proof of overpayment. They do mean
that an acquisition-led thesis requires impairment tests, organic growth
disclosure, retention of acquired customers, and incremental cash returns—not
just consolidated revenue growth.

## Lyn Alden lens

Healthcare demand is relatively durable, but Waystar's revenue is connected to
the financial administration of care. Provider liquidity, payer reimbursement,
patient employment and coverage, claim volume, collection rates, and public
healthcare policy affect the amount and timing of money moving through the
platform.

The company may be resilient in a slowdown because providers still need to
submit claims, collect balances, prevent denials, and reconcile payments. It
can still face pressure if providers consolidate and negotiate price, if
patient-pay volumes weaken, if payers change transaction standards, or if a
customer's financial stress slows collections. Its debt and acquisition model
make interest rates and credit access relevant even if healthcare utilization
remains stable.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers

The narrative becomes:

`healthcare customers × workflows per customer × subscription price + transaction and payment volume × fee rate -> revenue -> payment processing, support, implementation, sales, R&D, interest, taxes, and acquisitions -> owner cash`

The valuation should separately model:

1. provider subscription retention and price;
2. provider transaction volume and minimum commitments;
3. patient-payment volume after third-party processing cost;
4. NRR and customer concentration;
5. Iodine and future acquisition returns;
6. debt paydown versus acquisition spending; and
7. stock compensation and diluted shares.

At the September 14, 2026 market snapshot, Waystar's equity value was about
`$4.6B`. That is roughly 16 times FY2025 reported recurring-capital residual,
before acquisition cash, and roughly 10 times FY2025 adjusted EBITDA. The
multiple is not obviously excessive for a durable healthcare workflow, but the
equity value should be analyzed with net debt, acquisition needs, and dilution
included. The relevant question is what portion of future cash can be
distributed after maintaining the platform and replacing acquired growth.

## Management incentives and thesis breakers

Management is incentivized to emphasize revenue growth, subscription growth,
NRR, adjusted EBITDA, adjusted EBITDA margin, transaction volume, and cross-
selling. Pair each with provider/patient mix, gross profit, cash collection,
GAAP earnings, SBC, diluted shares, acquisition return, and debt reduction.

The thesis breaks if:

- NRR remains high only because new products or acquisitions are added while
  organic retention weakens;
- volume revenue grows through low-margin patient-payment activity;
- accounts receivable or the receivables facility grows faster than collections;
- adjusted EBITDA expands while GAAP earnings, cash per diluted share, or debt
  reduction do not improve;
- Iodine revenue and customer retention do not justify its cash and stock cost;
- future acquisitions are required to maintain reported growth;
- healthcare providers or payers reduce pricing or consolidate purchasing; or
- security, regulatory, payment, or patient-data failures raise support,
  litigation, or customer-loss costs.

## Next-filing evidence ladder

| Question | Confirming evidence | Falsifying evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Is recurring revenue durable? | Organic subscription growth, stable gross retention, NRR above 100%, and cash collection move together | Growth depends on acquisitions, pricing concessions, or volume spikes while organic retention weakens |
| Is volume revenue high quality? | Provider volume grows with contribution margin and stable patient-payment costs | Patient-payment revenue grows faster but third-party costs consume the gross profit |
| Did Iodine earn its cost? | Acquired revenue, retention, cross-sell, and incremental cash returns are disclosed and improve | Consolidated growth rises while acquired revenue, integration cost, amortization, or impairment risk remain opaque |
| Is cash clean? | Operating cash remains close to earnings after working-capital normalization and capex | Receivables, deferred revenue, or receivables financing explain most of the improvement |
| Is leverage falling? | Debt declines while the company still funds product investment and avoids excessive dilution | Acquisitions require new debt or equity before prior acquisition returns are proven |
| Is the adjustment gap narrowing? | GAAP profit and cash per diluted share catch up with adjusted EBITDA | SBC, amortization, acquisition costs, and other exclusions remain recurring and material |

## Bottom line

Waystar appears to control an important healthcare administration workflow with
high customer embedment, recurring subscription revenue, and strong reported
cash generation. Its economic quality is better than a simple transaction
processor because provider workflows, integrations, data, and payment
coordination can create switching friction.

The main risk is not that the product is irrelevant. It is that the owner-cash
claim is overstated if volume mix, acquisitions, debt, stock compensation,
receivables financing, and acquired intangible replacement are treated as
secondary. The durable thesis requires organic retention, stable contribution
margin, disciplined acquisition returns, falling leverage, and rising cash per
diluted share.
