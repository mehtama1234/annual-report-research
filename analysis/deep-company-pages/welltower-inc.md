# Welltower Inc. — healthcare property through occupancy, operator performance, and capital markets

Research date: 2026-09-14  
Evidence window: FY2025, Q4 2025, Q1 2026, and Q2 2026  
Ticker: WELL  
Evidence grade: A for normalized FFO, same-store NOI, senior-housing operating data, leverage, and investment activity; B for resident affordability, operator-level cash, maintenance capital, lease economics, property valuation, and the durability of current growth rates.

## Investment question

Welltower is a healthcare real-estate investment trust whose economics are
increasingly tied to senior-housing operating performance. It owns or finances
healthcare and senior-living properties, but its results are not only passive
rent collection. Occupancy, resident rates, operator quality, staffing,
property investment, acquisitions, debt, and access to equity capital all
affect the cash available to common owners.

The investment question is whether the aging-related demand and current senior-
housing operating improvement can produce durable per-share cash after property
maintenance, operator support, acquisitions, development, debt, dilution, and
the cost of maintaining a safe and useful property system.

## What the customer and counterparty buy

The resident or patient receives access to a physical community or healthcare
facility. The operator receives a location from which to provide housing or
care. Welltower receives rent, operating participation, or property-level
economic exposure, depending on the arrangement.

This creates several relationships that must not be combined:

- residents and families care about location, affordability, service, and care;
- operators need occupancy, staffing, reimbursement or resident payments, and
  viable community economics;
- Welltower needs rent or operating cash, asset quality, financing access, and
  returns on property investment.

Welltower controls property selection, capital allocation, financing, asset
management, operator relationships, and parts of the operating platform. It
does not control labor availability, resident income, government reimbursement,
operator execution, interest rates, property insurance, or the timing of
healthcare demand.

## Operating sequence and denominator

The operating sequence is:

`aging or care need -> resident or patient demand -> occupancy and service revenue -> operator margin -> property NOI or operating participation -> maintenance and growth capital -> financing and common-owner cash`

The correct denominators are:

- occupancy by property type and market;
- revenue per occupied unit or available unit;
- same-store NOI and same-store revenue;
- seniors-housing operating portfolio occupancy, revenue, and NOI;
- operator coverage, rent coverage, and tenant or operator health;
- property-level cash after maintenance and recurring capital;
- acquisition yield, development cost, lease-up, and stabilization period;
- debt, fixed-charge coverage, maturities, interest rates, secured debt, and
  net debt to adjusted EBITDA;
- normalized FFO per share, AFFO or cash available after recurring capital, NAV,
  and diluted share count.

NOI growth is not automatically common-owner cash. It must be reduced by
property capital, corporate costs, interest, dilution, and the capital required
to support the operator and portfolio. A high FFO growth rate can also reflect
acquisitions, asset sales, fair-value changes, or a recovery from a depressed
occupancy base.

## Senior-housing operating portfolio

The central current fact is the combination of occupancy improvement, revenue
growth, and NOI growth. Occupancy gives fixed property costs more revenue to
absorb. Revenue growth can come from occupancy, rates, care mix, or portfolio
changes. NOI growth can come from genuine operating leverage, but it can also
reflect a low prior base, temporary cost timing, or acquisition mix.

The next test is the quality of the incremental NOI. A stronger result would
show occupancy gains across markets, sustainable resident pricing, stable
operator staffing, healthy rent coverage, and maintenance spending that keeps
properties competitive. A weaker result would show rate-led growth with
resident affordability stress, higher labor cost, weaker service, or capital
deferral.

## Operator health and burden transfer

Welltower’s property economics depend on operators. If the operator cannot
recruit staff, pay wages, maintain quality, or collect resident fees, the
property owner may face lower rent coverage, concessions, restructurings,
capital support, or replacement-operator work.

The property structure can make reported rent look stable while the operating
system weakens. The investor should follow operator revenue, occupancy,
coverage, staffing, service quality, and capital needs rather than evaluating
the landlord only through contractual rent.

This is a burden-transfer question, not a burden-elimination question. A REIT
can avoid directly employing all care staff while still carrying the economic
cost when operator health, occupancy, or resident satisfaction deteriorates.

## Property capital and acquisitions

Healthcare and senior-living properties require recurring capital for life-
safety systems, roofs, common areas, rooms, accessibility, technology, and
service capacity. New investment also requires due diligence on location,
operator capability, resident affordability, local competition, construction,
lease-up, and financing.

The roughly `$11B` of 2025 net investment activity is therefore a central
valuation question. The right test is:

`investment cost + financing + lease-up and integration burden -> stabilized NOI -> recurring property capital -> return on invested capital`

Acquisition growth can increase FFO while lowering per-dollar returns if asset
prices rise, cap rates compress, debt costs increase, or management accepts
weaker operators. Development can create long-term supply but consumes capital
before occupancy and stabilized cash arrive.

## Earnings quality and financial-engineering tests

### 1. Normalized FFO is necessary but incomplete

Normalized FFO is a useful REIT measure because depreciation does not represent
the full current-period property economics. It is not a final owner-cash
measure. The investor must subtract recurring maintenance and replacement
capital, recurring leasing or operating support, interest, preferred claims,
and dilution where applicable.

### 2. Same-store NOI must be separated from acquisitions

Same-store growth is the better read on the existing portfolio, but the base
must be checked for occupancy recovery, property transfers, operator changes,
and abnormal prior-period conditions. Total FFO growth that comes mainly from
acquisitions should be tested against acquisition yields and per-share cash.

### 3. Occupancy and rate growth can strain affordability

Higher rates and occupancy can improve revenue, but resident and family budgets
are finite. If price increases outpace affordability, move-ins, length of stay,
or collections can weaken. A senior-housing thesis must include the payer’s
ability to pay, not only the demographic need.

### 4. Property NOI can exclude costs borne by operators

An operator may carry labor, marketing, service, and resident-acquisition cost
that does not appear as the landlord’s direct expense. If operator margins
compress, future rent growth, occupancy, and asset value can suffer. The
investor should reconcile property NOI to operator health and rent coverage.

### 5. Net debt to adjusted EBITDA is not the whole capital test

The reported year-end ratio of about `3.0x` provides a useful leverage screen.
It does not capture secured debt, maturities, fixed-rate versus floating-rate
exposure, development commitments, preferred capital, joint ventures, or the
effect of equity issuance on per-share value.

### 6. Asset values and FFO can move in opposite directions

FFO can grow while property values fall if capitalization rates rise or market
rent expectations weaken. Conversely, fair-value gains can increase reported
net income without producing distributable cash. NAV, replacement cost, debt,
rent coverage, and property-level cash should be analyzed separately.

## Lyn Alden lens: demographic demand and real-estate liquidity

The demographic force is favorable, but the cash path is financial and
operational. Welltower depends on resident and family liquidity, operator
liquidity, labor supply, property financing, interest rates, cap rates, and
access to equity and debt markets.

The strongest durability case is a property platform with rising occupancy,
operator health, disciplined capital allocation, manageable leverage, and
assets that remain useful through a credit cycle. The risk case is a period in
which higher rates reduce property values and financing capacity while labor,
insurance, and resident affordability weaken operating cash at the same time.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers and lifecycle

The bullish narrative is that aging demand, limited senior-housing supply,
occupancy recovery, operating data, and scale allow Welltower to compound NOI,
FFO, and asset value through disciplined investment.

That narrative requires measurable assumptions:

- normalized occupancy and revenue growth by property type;
- resident affordability, pricing, care intensity, and operator coverage;
- same-store NOI after sustainable labor and service costs;
- maintenance and recurring replacement capital;
- acquisition and development yields after lease-up, financing, and integration;
- debt cost, maturities, fixed-charge coverage, and equity issuance;
- NAV after realistic cap rates, property condition, operator risk, and debt;
- normalized FFO or AFFO per diluted share through a full property cycle.

Welltower is in a high-investment and operating-recovery phase. The valuation
should not capitalize current same-store NOI growth as a permanent rate before
separating recovery from normalized growth and charging the capital required to
keep the properties and operators viable.

## Valuation expectations

The September 14, 2026 market snapshot was approximately `$172.837B` of equity
value and a quoted trailing P/E ratio above `100x`. That P/E is not a useful
primary REIT measure because depreciation and property marks distort GAAP
earnings. Q2 2026 normalized FFO of `$1.60` per diluted share annualizes to
about `$6.40`; the equity snapshot is therefore roughly `37x` that simple
annualized FFO screen.

| Normalized FFO per share screen | Applied multiple | Implied share value |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| `$5.50` bear case | `20x` | `$110` |
| `$6.40` base case | `30x` | `$192` |
| `$7.25` strong case | `34x` | `$247` |

These scenarios are not a price target or NAV estimate. The base case requires
senior-housing operating growth to remain strong after recovery effects,
operator health to hold, and capital investment to earn returns above debt and
equity costs. A defensible REIT valuation must also test NAV using property
cash flow, cap rates, debt, preferred claims, recurring capital, and dilution.
The current screen indicates that the market already expects substantial
durability and growth; it does not establish that those expectations are
wrong or right.

The current market value is close to the strong-case equity value implied by
the per-share screen. That comparison is not a conclusion about margin of
safety: the strong case assumes durable senior-housing growth after recovery
effects, healthy operators, and capital investment that earns more than debt
and equity costs.

## Management incentives and thesis breakers

Management has reasons to emphasize normalized FFO growth, same-store NOI,
investment volume, operating portfolio growth, and leverage. These measures are
useful, but they can reward expansion before the full return and maintenance
burden is visible.

The thesis weakens if occupancy growth stalls, rate growth reduces move-ins or
collections, operator coverage deteriorates, labor or insurance costs absorb
NOI growth, maintenance capital rises, acquisition yields compress, leverage
or equity issuance increases, cap rates rise faster than NOI, or normalized
FFO grows without comparable per-share cash and asset value.

## Next-filing tests

The next filing and earnings release should answer:

- Did senior-housing occupancy and same-store NOI growth continue after the
  recovery comparisons became less favorable?
- What portion of revenue growth came from occupancy, rates, care mix,
  acquisitions, or development?
- Did operator coverage, staffing, concessions, bad debt, or resident
  affordability change?
- What was recurring maintenance and replacement capital by property type?
- What were acquisition yields, development costs, lease-up periods, and
  stabilized returns?
- Did debt, fixed-charge coverage, maturities, interest expense, or equity
  issuance change?
- Did normalized FFO per diluted share grow after capital raising and asset
  transactions?
- Did property values, cap rates, fair-value marks, and NAV change in a way
  consistent with the operating results?
