## Bottom line

Wingstop is a high-royalty, highly franchised restaurant system with a
valuable brand and a growing digital operating interface. The parent company
reported strong 2025 revenue and net-income growth, but the quality of that
headline growth is mixed: a $92.5 million gain on the sale of an investment
was the principal reason reported net income jumped to $174.3 million, while
operating cash flow fell to $153.1 million from $157.6 million.

The clean version of the thesis is that Wingstop owns the flavor brand,
franchise standards, royalty stream, digital ordering relationship, and
system-level data while franchisees carry most restaurant labor, food,
occupancy, maintenance, and financing burden. The hard question is whether
the franchisees still earn enough after that burden to keep opening and
remodeling stores. Fiscal 2025 provides a useful stress test: domestic
same-store sales declined 3.3% even as system-wide sales rose 12.1% and
digital sales exceeded 60%.

This is a quality-and-investigation memo, not a recommendation. The annual
report does not disclose a complete franchisee cash-return bridge.

## What the company actually is

Wingstop is not primarily a company-owned restaurant operator. At December 27,
2025 it had 3,056 restaurants, of which 2,999 were franchised and 57 were
company-owned—approximately 98% franchised. The parent records royalty,
franchise-fee, advertising-fee, and limited company-restaurant revenue. The
system-wide sales metric captures customer activity across the network, but is
not the same as Wingstop's consolidated revenue.

The economic system is:

`consumer order -> franchisee restaurant execution -> system sales -> royalty and advertising fees -> Wingstop corporate cash`

That structure creates operating leverage for the parent, but also creates an
information problem. The most important costs may sit outside the parent’s
income statement, while the parent’s revenue can remain healthy until
franchisee economics have already deteriorated.

## Three-year reported financial picture

report's consolidated statements.

| Fiscal year | Revenue | Operating income | Net income | Operating cash flow | PP&E purchases | Restaurant acquisitions |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2023 | 460.1 | 112.6 | 70.2 | 121.6 | 40.8 | 10.8 |
| 2024 | 625.8 | 165.6 | 108.7 | 157.6 | 51.9 | 14.0 |
| 2025 | 696.9 | 179.3 | 174.3 | 153.1 | 47.4 | 18.5 |

Several points matter more than the growth percentages:

- 2025 revenue grew 11.4%, but operating income grew only 8.3%.
- 2025 operating cash flow declined 2.9% despite higher operating income.
- Cash spent on PP&E and restaurant acquisitions was $66.0 million in 2025,
  leaving approximately $87.1 million before dividends, buybacks, and debt
  principal considerations.
- Reported 2025 net income includes a $92.5 million gain on the sale of the
  company's U.K. master-franchisee investment. The report's adjusted net
  income was $114.5 million versus GAAP net income of $174.3 million.
- Stock-based compensation was $24.9 million in 2025, up from $22.1 million
  in 2024 and $15.6 million in 2023. It is non-cash in the period but dilutive
  or economically relevant when shares are used to compensate employees.

The initial quality read is therefore: operating performance improved, but
reported earnings growth substantially overstates recurring operating growth.

## Earnings quality and possible financial engineering

### 1. The investment gain is clearly non-recurring, but it changes the headline

Wingstop sold an unconsolidated equity-method investment in Lemon Pepper
Holdings, its U.K. master franchisee, and recorded a $92.5 million gain. This
is not evidence of misconduct—the company discloses it and removes it in its
adjusted earnings reconciliation—but it is a major reason the 2025 GAAP
earnings number looks unusually strong.

The correct recurring comparison is closer to operating income, operating cash
flow, or adjusted net income than to 2025 GAAP EPS. The gain also deserves a
second question: did the sale and subsequent reinvestment improve the future
royalty stream, or did it mainly create cash available for repurchases?

### 2. Adjusted EBITDA has a meaningful add-back stack

2025 EBITDA was $298.0 million. Adjusted EBITDA was $244.2 million after
subtracting the $92.5 million investment gain but adding back stock-based
compensation of $24.9 million, system implementation costs and amortization,
transaction costs, and the loss on an office-building sale. The company
appropriately warns that EBITDA does not reflect working capital, taxes,
interest, or asset replacement.

The unusual feature is not that management reports adjusted EBITDA; it is that
the investor must avoid using it as discretionary cash. 2025 cash from
operations was $153.1 million, far below adjusted EBITDA, and $66.0 million
went to PP&E and acquired restaurants. The gap is not automatically a red
flag, but it requires reconciliation rather than a margin shortcut.

### 3. Advertising-fund accounting needs a separate cash test

Advertising fees were $247.6 million and advertising expenses were $261.5
million in 2025. Advertising expenses are recognized when related revenue is
recognized, which the company says does not necessarily match the actual
timing of advertising spend. Operating cash flow was also affected by changes
in advertising-fund cash and liabilities.

The advertising fund is not ordinary corporate profit, but its size and timing
can affect reported working capital and cash. The next review should reconcile
advertising-fee revenue, advertising expense, restricted cash, fund assets and
liabilities, and actual cash disbursements. A cash build caused by delayed
advertising payments should not be mistaken for permanent free cash flow.

### 4. Buybacks are material relative to recurring cash

Wingstop repurchased $221.9 million of stock in 2025, paid $32.4 million of
dividends, and spent $66.0 million on PP&E and acquired restaurants. Those
uses exceed 2025 operating cash flow by approximately $167 million. The 2024
securitized financing transaction added $500 million of debt, and the company
states that proceeds were used partly for general corporate purposes including
share repurchases.

This is not necessarily imprudent: the company may have had cash, investment
proceeds, and borrowing capacity. But it is a clear capital-allocation test.
Buybacks funded partly by new debt should be evaluated against recurring
post-maintenance cash flow, not adjusted EPS accretion alone.

## Cash conversion and capital requirements

### Owner-cash bridge

| 2025 item | $ millions |
|---|---:|
| Operating cash flow | 153.1 |
| PP&E purchases | (47.4) |
| Restaurant acquisitions from franchisees | (18.5) |
| Approximate cash after these operating investments | 87.1 |
| Dividends paid | (32.4) |
| Share repurchases | (221.9) |

The $87.1 million figure is not a definitive owner earnings measure. It
excludes taxes already paid through operating cash flow, debt principal
structure, potential maintenance versus growth capex, and cash held for the
advertising fund. It is a useful warning against describing all $153.1 million
of operating cash flow as distributable.

Capital needs include technology investments, company-owned restaurants,
acquisitions of restaurants from franchisees, debt service, dividends, and
share repurchases. Although the franchise model is asset-light at the parent
level, the network still requires restaurant-level capital. The parent can
acquire underperforming franchisee restaurants, as it did for five restaurants
in 2025 at a total purchase price of $18.5 million. That creates a possible
countercyclical opportunity, but it also means the parent may absorb burden
when franchisees cannot or will not fund the system.

## Balance-sheet resilience and debt

At year-end, reported cash, cash equivalents, and restricted cash were $228.5
million. The securitized financing structure included approximately:

- $472.8 million carrying value of 2020 Class A-2 notes;
- $248.1 million carrying value of 2022 Class A-2 notes; and
- $500.0 million carrying value of 2024 Class A-2 notes.

That is approximately $1.22 billion of securitized note carrying value before
considering other balance-sheet details. The 2024 notes bear a 5.858% fixed
rate. 2025 net interest expense rose 68.1% to $35.8 million, primarily because
of the 2024 financing.

The company reported leverage below 5.0x and compliance with financial
covenants. Principal payments can be suspended while the leverage ratio stays
below the contractual threshold. The debt also has rapid-amortization events
tied to debt-service coverage, specified global gross sales, change of control,
management termination, default, or failure to refinance.

The macro/liquidity question is not merely whether Wingstop can pay interest
today. It is whether system-wide sales and royalty receipts remain resilient
enough across a consumer slowdown for the securitized cash-flow structure to
avoid accelerated amortization just when franchisees are least able to fund
stores.

## Operating performance: what changed in 2025

Royalty, franchise-fee, and other revenue rose 11.6% to $321.8 million. The
company attributes $22.6 million to net new franchise development and $19.1
million to increased royalty fees, partly offset by an $8.3 million reduction
from the 3.3% domestic same-store-sales decline.

Advertising fees rose 13.8% to $247.6 million, including a higher national
advertising-fund contribution rate of 5.5%. Company-owned restaurant sales
rose 6.4%, mainly from restaurants opened or acquired during the year; the
company-owned same-store-sales increase was 2.6%, driven primarily by more
transactions.

This produces an important split:

- New development and higher fee rates helped parent revenue.
- Domestic same-store performance was a drag.
- Company-owned transaction growth was positive, but the company-owned scope
  is only 57 restaurants and cannot stand in for the 2,999 franchised stores.
- Food, beverage, and packaging costs rose to 36.8% of company-owned sales
  from 36.2%; company-owned labor improved to 23.2% from 23.6%, partly because
  a corporate restaurant sale shifted scope to a franchisee.

The apparent labor improvement therefore contains a scope effect. It is not
clean evidence that the full system became more labor efficient.

## 2026 quarterly update: the contradiction intensified

The 2026 second-quarter 10-Q adds the missing quarterly evidence. For the
thirteen weeks ended June 27, 2026, system-wide sales increased 5.3% to $1.411B
and total revenue increased 6.4% to $185.6M, but domestic same-store sales
declined 7.5%. The decline was attributed to lower transaction volumes and
reduced royalty revenue relative to what new franchise development contributed.
Domestic AUV fell to $1.893M from $2.112M in the comparable quarter. This is
not a clean demand recovery hidden by the growing unit count.

The year-to-date picture is similar: system-wide sales rose 5.6% to $2.788B,
but domestic same-store sales fell 8.1% and net income fell 48.6% to $61.2M.
Adjusted net income rose 14.8% to $64.6M, and adjusted EBITDA rose 11.2% to
$132.0M. The gap is partly explained by the prior-year $93.8M investment gain
from the Lemon Pepper Holdings sale, but it also reinforces why adjusted
metrics cannot replace the GAAP and cash bridge.

The 26-week cash-flow statement provides a useful capital check. Operating cash
flow was $68.3M versus $31.9M in the prior-year period; purchases of property
and equipment were $35.9M, with no restaurant acquisition cash in the current
period. Financing cash outflow was $103.4M, including $78.5M of repurchases and
$8.5M of taxes on vested restricted stock. Management attributes much of the
operating-cash increase to advertising-fund timing and working-capital changes,
so the improvement should not be annualized without a fund-cash reconciliation.

The balance sheet also makes the burden visible. At June 27, 2026, cash and
restricted cash were $153.4M before considering the separately restricted
advertising-fund assets, long-term debt was $1.211B, and common shares issued
and outstanding were 27.240M. Subsequent to quarter-end, Wingstop agreed to
purchase 13 restaurants for $32M, expected to be funded with cash on hand.
That transaction is strategically consistent with control of the network, but
it is another use of parent cash while mature-store demand is weak.

The new loyalty program, Club Wingstop, launched May 27, 2026. The filing says
the loyalty points create a separate performance obligation and that the
contract liability was immaterial for the quarter. The program is therefore a
promising relationship-control experiment, not yet evidence of a material
prepaid demand pool or incremental frequency.

### Updated forensic judgment

The quarterly evidence lowers confidence in a quick return to the bull case:
new units and system-wide sales are growing, but domestic same-store sales,
AUV, and transactions are under pressure. Parent OCF improved, yet management
explicitly ties much of the change to advertising-fund timing and working
capital. The current base case should therefore require proof of traffic and
franchisee economics, not simply another quarter of adjusted EBITDA growth.

## Lyn Alden lens: durability, liquidity, and burden transfer

Wingstop has several durability features: a focused product identity, repeat
franchise royalties, a large and expanding store network, digital ordering,
weekly royalty collection, and a model that limits parent-level restaurant
labor exposure.

The vulnerabilities are equally concrete:

- Consumer trade-down can reduce restaurant frequency or push customers toward
  value offerings.
- Bone-in chicken and other commodity inputs can pressure franchisee margins.
- Wage and benefit rules raise local operating costs.
- Franchisee financing conditions affect store openings and remodels.
- A distributor and supplier disruption can impair availability or cost.
- Securitized debt makes system sales and debt-service coverage relevant to
  liquidity, not just brand perception.

The key Alden-style conclusion is that the parent is more resilient than a
single restaurant, but not independent of the restaurant system. It has
transferred much of the operating burden; it has not eliminated it.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers and lifecycle

The growth narrative has four components: international expansion, domestic
new-store development, digital penetration, and brand-led same-store demand.
Each must be modeled separately.

### Growth components

1. **New units:** number of openings, closure rate, maturation curve, required
   franchisee capex, and royalty ramp.
2. **Same-store sales:** traffic, price, mix, promotions, and transaction
   frequency—not just the combined percentage.
3. **Digital:** order frequency, retention, average order value, delivery cost,
   labor effect, and whether digital customers are incremental.
4. **International:** local unit economics, currency, master-franchisee health,
   and capital returned to the parent.

### Reinvestment and lifecycle

Wingstop is in an expansion phase, but the parent has begun acquiring
restaurants from franchisees and investing heavily in technology. The right
reinvestment question is not whether the brand can open many restaurants; it
is whether the next restaurant produces an adequate return after franchisee
capex, labor, food, rent, technology, and financing.

The proxy-linked compensation disclosures say performance stock units can vest
based on net-new-restaurant targets, return on incremental investment targets,
or sales targets, with awards ranging from 0% to 250% of target depending on
performance. This is a positive design feature because return on incremental
investment is included, but it creates a verification obligation: future
filings should show which goals drove vesting and whether return metrics are
measured after the full burden stack.

### Reverse-valuation questions

The dated scenario register preserves a September 14, 2026 equity-value input
for this screen. It is an expectations screen, not a live quote or price
target. A valuation model should reverse-engineer what the price expects for:

- sustainable royalty growth after the domestic same-store-sales decline;
- long-run net-new restaurant additions;
- franchisee health sufficient to fund openings and remodels;
- recurring operating cash flow after technology and restaurant acquisitions;
- debt refinancing and interest costs;
- stock-based compensation and dilution; and
- buyback-funded EPS growth.

If the implied assumptions require perpetual high unit growth, no franchisee
stress, stable commodity/labor costs, and uninterrupted debt access at modest
rates, the margin of safety may be smaller than the brand narrative suggests.

## Management incentives and governance questions

The annual report discloses three economically relevant incentive channels:

- performance stock units tied to net-new restaurants, return on incremental
  investment, or sales targets;
- share repurchases of $221.9 million in 2025, which can support per-share
  metrics but may increase leverage when funded with debt or investment
  proceeds; and
- a concentrated franchise-development model where growth in the store count
  supports future royalty revenue but may also increase system stress.

The investigation questions are:

1. Are performance targets based on reported revenue and store count, or on
   cash returns after the full capital and burden stack?
2. Did 2025 buybacks occur at prices justified by recurring cash flow rather
   than the non-recurring investment gain?
3. Does management acquire restaurants from franchisees because it sees
   attractive returns, or because the franchise network is under stress?
4. Does higher digital activity improve restaurant profitability, or does it
   mainly provide better data while delivery and fulfillment costs rise?

These are forensic questions, not accusations of manipulation.

## Who controls the relationship and who carries the burden

| Layer | Wingstop parent | Franchisee / local system |
|---|---|---|
| Demand | Brand, menu identity, loyalty and digital interface | Local service execution and customer recovery |
| Workflow | POS, restaurant-management systems, Smart Kitchen, standards | Staffing, scheduling, food prep, equipment uptime |
| Economics | Royalties, franchise fees, advertising coordination | Store sales after food, labor, rent, delivery, and financing |
| Capital | Corporate technology, selective restaurant acquisitions, debt | Store build-outs, remodels, working capital, local borrowing |
| Risk | Brand, technology, debt-service, franchisee health | Commodity, wage, labor, occupancy, supply, and operating volatility |

The parent controls the valuable relationship layer more than it controls the
physical work. That explains the cleaner corporate margins, but also means
franchisee health is a leading indicator that may not appear immediately in
Wingstop's reported earnings.

## Thesis breakers and next-filing watchlist

### Thesis breakers

- Domestic same-store sales remain negative because transactions and traffic
  deteriorate, not merely because of temporary mix.
- Franchisee openings slow, closures rise, or financing availability weakens.
- Royalty growth depends on fee-rate changes and new-store count while mature
  store economics deteriorate.
- Digital sales grow but do not improve order frequency, throughput, labor
  productivity, or franchisee returns.
- Operating cash flow remains below adjusted EBITDA and recurring post-capex
  cash cannot support dividends, buybacks, and debt service without new debt.
- Commodity, labor, or distributor pressure forces franchisee relief that
  transfers economics back to the parent.
- Debt-service coverage or global-sales covenants approach the rapid-
  amortization thresholds.

### Next filing fields

1. Domestic comparable sales split into traffic, price, and mix.
2. Franchise openings, closures, transfers, acquisitions, and development
   pipeline.
3. Franchisee financial-health indicators and any incentives or relief.
4. Royalty revenue growth versus system-wide sales growth.
5. Company-owned food and labor costs, clearly separated from system-wide
   economics.
6. Advertising-fund cash, assets, liabilities, and timing effects.
7. Operating cash flow less PP&E and acquired-restaurant spending.
8. Debt balance, interest paid, leverage, debt-service coverage, and covenant
   headroom.
9. Stock compensation, share count, repurchase price, and remaining
   authorization.
10. PSU performance outcomes and the definition of incremental return.
11. 2026 10-Q cash flow, advertising-fund movements, and the cash funding of
    restaurant purchases.
12. Club Wingstop enrollment, redemption, frequency, and incremental gross
    profit after rewards.

## Final judgment

Wingstop is a useful contradiction to a simplistic asset-light success story.
The parent has a genuinely attractive control point—brand, franchise system,
royalty collection, and digital infrastructure—and its 2025 filing shows real
operating scale. But 2025 also shows why the full system must be analyzed:
reported net income was lifted by a large investment gain, operating cash flow
declined, same-store domestic demand weakened, debt-funded capital returns were
material, and company-owned labor data benefited from a scope change.

The 2026 second-quarter filing strengthens rather than resolves that caution:
unit growth and system-wide sales remain positive, but domestic same-store
sales fell 7.5% in the quarter and 8.1% year to date, while cash improvement
was materially connected to advertising-fund and working-capital timing. The
parent now has a more explicit growth-versus-mature-demand conflict to resolve.

The strongest current conclusion is therefore conditional:

> Wingstop's parent-level economics may be cleaner than restaurant ownership,
> but the investment case remains dependent on franchisee returns, mature-store
> traffic, recurring cash conversion, and debt resilience.

## Skeptical Reader Test

- Are system-wide sales, parent revenue, company-owned sales, and advertising
  fees kept on separate denominators?
- Is the $92.5 million investment gain excluded from recurring earnings?
- Does the cash bridge include PP&E, acquired restaurants, debt service, and
  stock-based compensation rather than relying on adjusted EBITDA?
- Are company-owned labor trends prevented from standing in for franchisee
  economics?
- Can the next-filing watchlist actually falsify the asset-light thesis?

## Evidence grade and unresolved questions

- **Filing fact:** 2025 revenue, investment gain, cash flow, PP&E, debt,
  buybacks, advertising-fund flows, and company-owned labor disclosures are
  anchored to the annual filing.
- **Quarterly/IR fact:** Q2 2026 domestic same-store sales, unit growth,
  systemwide sales, and advertising-fund timing are preserved in the quarterly
  filings and IR releases.
- **Inference:** Wingstop controls a valuable brand and royalty relationship,
  but its economic durability depends on franchisee store-level returns rather
  than parent revenue alone.
- **Unresolved:** the next filing must reconcile mature-store traffic, franchisee
  openings and closures, advertising-fund cash, debt service, and recurring
  owner cash. Continued negative domestic traffic or distributions above cash
  after required investment would weaken the thesis.
