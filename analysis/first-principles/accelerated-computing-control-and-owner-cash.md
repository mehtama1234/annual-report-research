# AI computing: why better chips do not guarantee lasting profits

Date baseline: 2026-09-13

## What the customer is paying for

Accelerated computing uses specialized processors and software to perform certain calculations faster or with less energy than general-purpose processors. In AI, those calculations include training models and running them when users make requests. Customers buy computing equipment because they expect to earn revenue, reduce costs or develop a useful capability.

The purchase price is only part of the customer's cost. Equipment needs a place to run, electricity, cooling, network connections and staff. It also becomes outdated. A faster chip is economically useful when its performance improves the result enough to justify these costs. A comparison based only on processing speed leaves out much of the purchase decision.

The supplier can record a sale before the customer has demonstrated a satisfactory return on the equipment. Current sales therefore do not prove that customers will keep buying at the same rate. Examine both the supplier's accounts and what customers achieve with the installed equipment. Where customer returns are not disclosed, that uncertainty belongs in the conclusion.

## Why customers may stay with one supplier

Customers use processors, memory, network equipment and software together. Engineers learn particular tools and write programs that work with them. Changing suppliers may require rewriting software, checking its results, retraining engineers and testing reliability before the replacement can serve customers.

These costs can help a supplier retain business. They do not make replacement impossible. Competing products become more attractive if their savings exceed the cost of switching, or if software becomes easier to move between processors. Large customers may also develop their own chips for tasks they perform repeatedly.

## Revenue concentration changes the risk

If most revenue comes from data-center customers, gaming or visualization
cannot offset a data-center spending pause. Large customers may be financially
strong but still have bargaining power and can change orders quickly.

Track customer concentration, order timing, payment terms, deployment rate,
customer revenue per deployed system, and the share of demand that is expansion
versus replacement.

## Product transitions create real costs

Semiconductor products have short transition windows. The company must commit
to wafers, packaging, memory, boards, and systems before final demand is fully
known. A new architecture can improve performance while making the prior
generation less valuable.

Inventory reserves and purchase-commitment charges are therefore central
operating risks. They are not merely accounting noise.

## Outsourcing production does not eliminate investment needs

Direct factory capex may be modest because manufacturing is outsourced. The
company can still fund inventory, receivables, supplier commitments, advanced
packaging, engineering compute, software development, partner investments, and
customer support.

Distinguish costs already paid from future commitments. Inventory purchases and ordinary operating payments affect operating cash flow. A contract to buy future production can create exposure before payment occurs. Subtracting both a current cash payment and the full related commitment would overstate the cash spent in the period.

## Alden and Damodaran applied

Lyn Alden’s lens asks how AI capital spending, interest rates, power, data-center
availability, export rules, and liquidity affect the buildout. Strong demand
does not remove the risk of an overbuilt or delayed capacity cycle.

Aswath Damodaran’s lens converts “AI platform control” into customer returns,
architecture life, software switching cost, gross margin, inventory, purchase
commitments, R&D, SBC, working capital, export access, and normalized cash per
share over the lifecycle.

## Research conclusion

Accelerated computing has become a major physical infrastructure layer. The
best evidence of durability is not another record quarter; it is stable
customer returns, controlled inventory and commitments, continued software
dependence, cash per share after stock compensation, and successful product
transitions through a weaker demand period.

## Calculate cash without counting a cost twice

**Accelerated computing** uses specialized processors and software to perform
some workloads faster or with less energy than general-purpose processors. The
customer buys the result: lower computing time or cost, higher service
capacity, or a new product. Technical performance matters only if it improves
the customer's economics.

**Platform control** means that production code, developer skills, libraries,
deployment tools, data paths, and measured performance are organized around a
supplier's architecture. It can increase retention and pricing power, but it
does not prevent internal chips or competing platforms.

**Owner cash** is an analyst's estimate of what can remain available to shareholders after the business funds its needs. It is not a standard reported measure, and the calculation must specify its starting point.

Start with reported operating cash flow. Under the indirect method, it reconciles net income with noncash items and changes in operating assets and liabilities. Do not subtract the same operating working-capital movements again. Ordinary cash operating costs already reflected in that figure should not be deducted a second time. The [SEC's explanation of cash-flow statements](https://www.sec.gov/about/reports-publications/investorpubsbegfinstmtguide) describes this reconciliation.

A useful first calculation is operating cash flow less capital expenditure, meaning spending on long-lived assets such as equipment. Then show acquisitions and other investing payments separately, identifying which spending supports ongoing operations and which expands the business. This subtotal is not automatically distributable: debt payments, minimum cash needs and other obligations still matter.

Forecasts require a separate judgment about what will recur. If unusually slow supplier payments temporarily increased cash flow, explain the adjustment needed for a normal payment schedule. For purchase commitments, record payment dates and cancellation terms, then include the relevant future payments in the forecast. Do not deduct all future commitments from one year's historical cash flow.

Share compensation needs its own treatment. Paying employees with shares may increase the share count without an immediate cash payment. A valuation can model that dilution explicitly or make a clearly explained adjustment for its economic cost. It should not charge the same dilution twice, and share repurchases should be shown separately from the operating cash calculation. The reader must be able to reproduce each adjustment.

## The workload-to-cash sequence

| Stage | Evidence to inspect | Main risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Workload choice | Customer use case, deployment, expected return | Customer cannot earn enough from capacity |
| Architecture selection | Benchmarks, software compatibility, total cost | Alternative chips or internal design |
| Supply commitment | Wafers, packaging, memory, boards, systems | Product transition and excess inventory |
| Shipment and acceptance | Orders, terms, delivery, returns, receivables | Revenue before cash or deployment |
| Production deployment | Utilization, energy, performance, customer revenue | Capacity sits idle or economics weaken |
| Software and support | Libraries, updates, tools, service cost | Engineering burden remains high |
| Replacement cycle | Product life, depreciation, upgrade timing | New architecture makes old assets less valuable |
| Next budget | Customer return on deployed capacity | Spending pause despite technical demand |

This sequence prevents a record order, backlog, or benchmark from being treated
as proof of recurring owner cash. The investor must follow the customer's
return and the supplier's cash obligations together.

## Product transitions and supply-chain economics

An architecture transition can increase system revenue because the new product
contains more memory, networking, packaging, and integration. It can also lower
gross margin or create a charge on the prior generation. Separate chip, board,
complete-system, networking, and software revenue; product-generation mix and
margin; wafer, packaging, memory, substrate, and system cost; inventory by
generation; non-cancelable purchase commitments; customer deposits; returns;
acceptance; warranties; and engineering cost for the next architecture.

Outsourcing manufacturing reduces factory ownership but does not remove supply
risk. A supplier reservation can be economically similar to a capacity
commitment, and an inventory reserve is evidence that a prior commitment has
lost value.

## Earnings-quality and possible financial-engineering tests

The central forensic risk is allowing platform growth, non-GAAP cash, customer
orders, and investment gains to obscure the cost of maintaining the platform.
Test:

- revenue against shipments, acceptance, receivables, payment terms, deposits,
  and cash collected;
- direct cloud and hyperscaler demand against indirect distributors, system
  integrators, and end-user deployment;
- customer concentration in revenue, receivables, backlog, and commitments;
- inventory by generation against reserves, sell-through, deployment, and
  cancellation rights;
- non-cancelable supplier obligations and exposure to packaging, memory, and
  foundry capacity;
- core operating profit against investment gains, foreign exchange,
  restructuring, and other non-operating items;
- stock compensation, diluted shares, repurchases, and employee retention cost;
  and
- cash after working capital and platform investment against cash after only
  property and equipment spending.

The H20 charge in NVIDIA's dossier is an example of real exposure to export
restrictions and product commitments. A one-time classification does not make
the underlying geopolitical or transition risk nonrecurring.

## Management incentives and capital allocation

The proxy should establish whether management is rewarded for revenue, data-
center growth, gross margin, operating income, free cash flow, market value,
return on invested capital, product launches, customer adoption, or per-share
cash. Record definitions, weights, threshold/target/maximum levels, and
exclusions for stock compensation, product-transition charges, investment
gains, acquisitions, and export-related costs.

A revenue target can encourage shipments before end-user deployment and return
are known. A non-GAAP cash target can exclude stock compensation or working
capital. A launch target can reward a transition before prior inventory clears.
A return-on-capital or cash-per-share measure is more complete only if it
charges commitments, engineering, inventory risk, and dilution.

For each major launch, compare management's target with inventory, margin,
customer acceptance, receivable collection, software adoption, support cost,
and cash per diluted share. For each capital return, compare the repurchase
price with normalized owner cash and the funding needed to preserve the
platform lead.

## Lyn Alden and Damodaran application

The Alden lens asks how data-center capital spending, rates, power, export
rules, supply-chain liquidity, and customer financing affect demand. Customers
can have strong strategic interest in AI while reducing orders if their own
returns weaken or capital markets tighten. A supplier with little debt can
still be exposed through inventory and purchase commitments.

The Damodaran lens separates the mature platform, new architecture, networking,
software, systems, and investment portfolio. The forecast should specify unit
volume, price, product mix, gross margin, customer return, architecture life,
R&D, supply commitments, inventory, support cost, taxes, stock compensation,
diluted shares, and terminal cash after growth normalizes.

The valuation question is how much future cash per share remains after
customers, suppliers, and the company all fund the next computing cycle.

## Cohort comparison and filing-based falsifiers

NVIDIA controls architecture, software compatibility, networking, systems,
roadmap, and deployment knowledge. Foundries, packaging providers, memory
suppliers, cloud customers, governments, and data-center owners control other
critical inputs. KLA controls process-control tools and yield information but
is exposed to semiconductor capital cycles. Micron controls memory capacity
but carries commodity pricing, manufacturing, and capex burden. Vertiv and
Cummins receive equipment revenue while carrying manufacturing, inventory,
warranty, labor, and service obligations.

Cloud providers fund more physical capacity and carry utilization, power,
facility, debt, and replacement risk. NVIDIA captures a more technical
control point with less direct factory capex, but carries product-transition,
inventory, export, customer-concentration, and software-maintenance risk. The
cleaner economics are not determined by gross margin alone; they depend on
which party funds the next investment and absorbs the loss if demand changes.

The thesis weakens if customer orders grow while deployment and customer
returns weaken; margin falls without a credible mix or cost explanation;
inventory and commitments rise faster than cash; export restrictions create
repeated product charges; large customers shift to internal or competing
chips; software compatibility becomes more portable; support consumes
increasing cost; investment gains support reported earnings; or stock
compensation and repurchases fail to improve cash per diluted share.

The next filings should provide revenue and margin by product generation,
customer concentration, receivable terms, inventory aging and reserves,
purchase commitments, customer deposits, export exposure, software and
support cost, R&D, stock compensation, diluted shares, repurchases, and cash
after full platform reinvestment.
