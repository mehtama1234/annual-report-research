# First Principles: Aging Demand, Property Ownership, and Owner Cash

Date: `2026-09-14`

## The question

An aging population creates demand for care, housing, medical services, equipment, and distribution. The demand does not tell us which company receives the best economics. The value may accrue to the direct operator, the property owner, the payer, the equipment supplier, or the distributor.

This essay separates those roles using Brookdale, Ventas, Addus, AdaptHealth, DaVita, and Cencora.

## 1. Aging demand has several economic layers

The same person can create revenue for multiple companies:

1. A property owner supplies the building or community.
2. An operator supplies staff and daily services.
3. A payer or insurer coordinates and funds eligible care.
4. A device or equipment provider supplies clinical tools and consumables.
5. A distributor moves medicines and supplies through the system.

These layers do not have equal economics. The layer with the highest revenue may have the lowest margin. The layer with the most recurring need may carry the greatest labor burden. The layer with the best margin may depend on other parties to fund the physical system.

## 2. Direct operator versus property owner

Brookdale operates senior-living communities. Its economics depend on occupancy, resident fees, staffing, food, insurance, maintenance, and local operating execution. It carries direct labor and service risk.

Ventas owns healthcare-related properties and also operates or manages senior-housing exposure through SHOP. Its economics depend on property scarcity, occupancy, operator quality, rent or resident fees, capital spending, financing, and portfolio allocation.

The owner has a different burden from the operator. A property can be scarce and valuable, but it does not produce cash without a healthy operator and enough residents able to pay. An operator can have strong demand, but it may not create high returns if wages, insurance, occupancy costs, and property requirements consume the revenue.

## 3. Home care shifts the burden

Addus provides personal care and home-based services. Its value comes from recurring care relationships and scheduling capability, but its main constraint is labor. The company must recruit, retain, schedule, and supervise caregivers while managing reimbursement and state-level funding.

AdaptHealth provides home medical equipment and supply replenishment. It can earn recurring revenue from resupply and equipment support, but it carries reimbursement, referral, field-service, supplier, and working-capital risk.

The home setting may reduce property costs, but it does not eliminate operating complexity. It shifts the burden toward labor, reimbursement, logistics, and patient-service execution.

## 4. Treatment and product flow

DaVita shows recurring treatment frequency. Dialysis patients require repeated treatment, so the company has a regular operating base. It still carries clinic labor, reimbursement, facility, medical quality, and regulatory risk.

Cencora shows product-flow infrastructure. Pharmacies and care sites need continuous medicine delivery. Cencora carries inventory, thin-spread, customer concentration, policy, and working-capital risk.

These companies benefit from recurring demand without owning the same property burden as Ventas or the same direct-care labor burden as Brookdale and Addus. Their margins can be thinner, and the need to move very large volumes can make cash conversion more sensitive to payment and inventory timing.

## 5. The control point is not the same as the burden carrier

| Layer | Control point | Main burden |
|---|---|---|
| Brookdale | Community relationship and daily resident service | Labor, occupancy, food, insurance, and property operating costs |
| Ventas | Scarce property, capital allocation, and senior-housing platform | Debt, equity dilution, property capital, operator health, and tenant support |
| Addus | Caregiver network and recurring home-service scheduling | Labor availability, reimbursement, supervision, and service quality |
| AdaptHealth | Equipment access, referral, payer, and resupply workflow | Reimbursement, field service, supplier pricing, and working capital |
| DaVita | Repeated treatment network and clinic workflow | Medical labor, regulation, reimbursement, facility capital, and quality |
| Cencora | Pharmaceutical access and distribution scale | Inventory, thin spreads, vendor/customer terms, policy, and concentration |

The investment mistake is to describe all six as “aging beneficiaries.” The more useful question is which layer captures value after funding its specific burden.

## 6. Property scarcity is not enough

Ventas’s senior-housing portfolio benefited from limited new supply and strong SHOP same-store cash NOI growth. That supports the value of existing communities.

But a scarce building can still underperform if:

- residents cannot afford rate increases;
- labor costs rise faster than fees;
- the operator cannot maintain service quality;
- insurance and property taxes rise;
- the owner overpays for acquisitions;
- debt or equity funding becomes expensive;
- the property requires capital that is not recovered through rent or resident fees.

Scarcity improves the owner’s negotiating position. It does not replace operating discipline.

## 7. Why FFO requires a second bridge

REITs use funds from operations because GAAP depreciation assumes property values decline on a systematic schedule. FFO removes much of that accounting depreciation to better show recurring property operations.

FFO is not the final owner-cash measure because properties still require:

- maintenance and redevelopment capital;
- tenant advances;
- development and acquisition funding;
- debt service;
- leasing and transaction costs;
- equity issuance and dilution.

The correct chain is:

`normalized FFO -> recurring property renewal -> tenant/operator support -> debt and preferred claims -> equity issuance -> cash and value per share`

The final output is not total FFO. It is durable value per diluted share.

## 8. Macro and liquidity test

The demand for senior care may be durable while the equity returns remain cyclical. Interest rates change property values, refinancing costs, and the relative attractiveness of REIT equity. A stressed credit market can make acquisitions unattractive even when properties are operationally strong.

The direct-care operators face another macro issue: wages, labor availability, and reimbursement may move in opposite directions. Demand can rise while margins fall if the cost of care rises faster than payment rates.

Distributors face working-capital and policy pressure. More drug volume can produce more revenue while percentage margins decline. A large recurring flow is not the same as high-quality cash.

## 9. Damodaran test: growth must earn its funding

Every aging-related growth claim needs four numbers:

1. demand or utilization growth;
2. price or reimbursement growth;
3. incremental operating and capital costs;
4. return on the new capital deployed.

Ventas’s senior-housing investments must earn more than the cost of debt and equity. Brookdale’s occupancy gains must cover labor and property costs. Addus’s patient growth must cover caregiver wages and reimbursement risk. AdaptHealth’s equipment growth must cover working capital and supplier costs. DaVita’s treatment growth must cover clinics, staff, and quality requirements. Cencora’s volume growth must cover inventory and financing needs.

## Valuation: demand after property and care obligations

Valuation should separate property cash flow, direct-care operating cash, equipment resupply, treatment cash, and pharmaceutical distribution. The model must state occupancy or utilization, reimbursement or pricing, labor and operating cost, maintenance and redevelopment capital, tenant or operator support, inventory and working capital, debt, preferred claims, acquisitions, and diluted shares.

For a REIT, start with normalized FFO and subtract recurring property renewal, tenant advances, debt and preferred claims, and dilution. For an operator, start with collected revenue and subtract labor, quality, facilities, equipment, working capital, and required capital. A demographic growth rate deserves a premium only when value per diluted share rises after the full burden is funded.

## 10. Risks, accounting tests, and what to check next

The aging-demand thesis weakens if:

- occupancy rises only through concessions or temporary pricing;
- senior-housing operators require repeated landlord support;
- direct-care labor cost outpaces reimbursement;
- medical-equipment resupply grows while cash conversion worsens;
- treatment volume rises but clinic returns decline;
- distribution revenue grows while gross-profit percentage and cash conversion deteriorate;
- property investment grows faster than FFO per diluted share;
- FFO grows but maintenance capital, tenant advances, debt, or dilution absorb the benefit.

At each filing, compare occupancy and utilization with collected revenue; resident or patient growth with labor and reimbursement; FFO with maintenance capital and tenant support; equipment and drug volume with inventory and receivables; and acquisitions with returns after debt and dilution. The next filing should show which layer is retaining cash rather than merely receiving more aging-related activity.

## Conclusion

Aging is a demand source, not an investment conclusion. The strongest economics belong to the layer that controls a necessary relationship while carrying a manageable share of labor, property, regulatory, and funding costs.

Ventas adds the property-owner case to the archive. It shows that demographic scarcity can support both operating growth and capital-allocation opportunity, but only if the property owner funds the system at returns above its financing and dilution cost. The clean analysis follows the cash and burden through every layer.
