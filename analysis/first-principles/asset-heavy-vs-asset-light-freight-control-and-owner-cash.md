# Asset-heavy versus asset-light freight control and owner cash

## The question

Freight companies can earn money by owning transportation capacity or by
coordinating capacity owned by others. Those models should not be compared only
by revenue, operating margin, or reported free cash flow.

An asset-heavy operator funds vehicles, aircraft, buildings, sorting equipment,
maintenance, labor, and network renewal. An asset-light broker funds software,
employees, credit, working capital, and customer acquisition. The broker has a
smaller physical capital bill, but it must manage the difference between when a
customer pays and when a carrier must be paid. The correct comparison is the
cash left after the costs required to preserve the relevant control point.

## Three control points

| Company | Control point | Main source of bargaining power | Main burden |
|---|---|---|---|
| UPS | Pickup, sorting, air, ground, delivery, and tracking network | Density, service reliability, integrated execution, customer systems | Labor, aircraft, vehicles, buildings, automation, pensions, transformation |
| C.H. Robinson | Shipper-carrier coordination and pricing workflow | Customer access, carrier network, data, pricing, credit, execution | Receivables, carrier-rate changes, staffing, software, credit, service claims |
| Union Pacific | Regulated rail right-of-way, terminals, locomotives, and freight cars | Route ownership, terminal access, network density, long-lived infrastructure | Track, bridges, locomotives, safety, labor, environmental and regulatory obligations |

The control point determines who can charge for scarcity. It does not by itself
determine who earns the cleanest economics. A broker may have lower capital
requirements but weaker customer and carrier switching costs. A physical network
may have stronger barriers to entry but larger renewal and labor claims.

## Cash bridges

| Company | FY2025 operating cash flow | Capital and acquisition cash | Residual used in this comparison |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| UPS | $8.450B | $3.685B capex + $1.968B acquisitions | $2.797B |
| C.H. Robinson | $914.5M | $70.5M capex + $11.9M acquisitions | $832.1M |
| Union Pacific | $9.290B | $3.791B capital investment | $5.499B |

The residuals are not directly comparable in scale. They are useful because they
make the burden visible. C.H. Robinson’s low capital spending does not mean it
has no reinvestment requirement. Its continuing requirements include software,
employee capability, customer and carrier retention, working-capital funding,
credit losses, and restructuring needed to keep the cost base aligned with
freight demand.

UPS generated much more operating cash, but capital expenditures and
acquisitions consumed a larger share. Its 2025 cash outcome must be read with
the Amazon volume reduction and network transformation. The company has to
restore package density while funding labor and automation. Union Pacific’s
residual is higher after capital investment, but service quality and safety
depend on continuing physical renewal.

## Revenue quality

Revenue is a weak common measure in freight.

C.H. Robinson records amounts billed to shippers, including purchased
transportation costs. A large revenue change can therefore reflect a change in
freight rates that passes through to carriers. Gross profit per shipment is more
informative.

UPS records the price of moving packages through its network. Price, package
mix, volume, route density, and fuel surcharges affect revenue, but the company
must still pay labor and maintain the network. Revenue growth without better
package density or route productivity can increase the cost burden.

Union Pacific’s revenue is tied to carloads, mix, core pricing, fuel surcharge,
and service. Core pricing that exceeds cost inflation is more valuable than a
fuel surcharge that passes through to customers.

## Working capital and capacity risk

C.H. Robinson’s 2025 operating cash flow benefited from a $95.4M receivables
release and a $44.3M contract-asset release. In 2024, receivables used $164.3M
of cash; in 2023, they supplied $607.3M. This is normal for a transaction and
credit intermediary, but it means a cash-flow screen must normalize the working
capital cycle.

UPS and Union Pacific also have working-capital movements, but their primary
risk is physical capacity and labor. UPS can lose margin if package density
falls while buildings, aircraft, vehicles, and labor remain in place. Union
Pacific can lose service quality if it cuts operating resources or defers
renewal while attempting to improve the operating ratio.

For C.H. Robinson, the equivalent failure occurs when carrier prices rise
before shipper prices reset, customers pay slowly, or contracted rates force the
company to accept negative spreads. The broker’s capacity is flexible in a
longer period, but cash and pricing exposure can be immediate.

## Productivity claims

The companies use different productivity language, so the evidence must be
translated into operating measures.

| Claim | Required evidence |
|---|---|
| UPS automation improves economics | Labor hours per package, package density, service quality, capex return, and cash per diluted share improve after transformation spending |
| C.H. Robinson Lean AI improves economics | Gross profit per employee and shipment improve after software, training, restructuring, service, and stock compensation |
| Union Pacific operating efficiency improves economics | Operating ratio falls without weaker velocity, dwell, safety, service, or maintenance renewal |

An adjusted margin increase is not sufficient. It must survive the costs that
management has excluded or classified as investment.

## First-principles valuation drivers

| Driver | UPS | C.H. Robinson | Union Pacific |
|---|---|---|---|
| Demand | Packages and package mix | Shipments, freight market share, trade flows | Carloads and commodity mix |
| Price | Base rates, surcharges, mix | Customer price less carrier cost | Core pricing less inflation |
| Utilization | Route, facility, aircraft, vehicle density | Employee productivity and carrier availability | Train, terminal, and equipment utilization |
| Reinvestment | Aircraft, vehicles, automation, buildings | Software, employees, working capital, customer and carrier systems | Track, bridges, locomotives, terminals |
| Burden | Labor, pensions, transformation, claims | Credit, working capital, restructuring, service and rate-spread risk | Labor, safety, environmental, regulation |
| Falsifier | Adjusted margin rises but density and cash per share fall | Gross profit rises but cash depends on receivable releases | Operating ratio improves while service or renewal deteriorates |

## Conclusion

Asset-heavy and asset-light freight companies can both have durable value, but
they carry different claims on future cash. UPS and Union Pacific own physical
systems that can be difficult to replace; C.H. Robinson owns a customer,
carrier, pricing, and execution workflow that must remain useful to both sides.

The cleanest economics belong to the company that can raise realized price or
productivity without transferring the cost into a larger unrecognized burden.
The next filings should therefore be read for cash per shipment, cash per
employee, working-capital normalization, full capital spending, customer and
carrier retention, service outcomes, and diluted share count.
