# AUM growth versus client relationship

T. Rowe Price shows why assets under management are not the same as a healthy asset-management franchise.

**Assets under management (AUM)** are client assets managed under an investment
mandate. They belong to clients, not to the asset manager. **Owner cash** is the
cash that remains for common shareholders after paying investment professionals,
distribution, technology, administration, product development, taxes, and any
acquisition or capital-return claims.

## The control point

An asset manager controls investment research, product design, distribution, and client service. Its relationship is valuable when clients keep capital through market cycles and accept a fee that covers the people, systems, and distribution required to manage it.

The manager does not own the client assets. AUM is a measure of capital entrusted to the firm, not an owned asset. Client flows and fee rates determine how much economic value the relationship produces.

## The four-part AUM bridge

The useful sequence is:

`starting AUM + market movement + distributions + net client flows = ending AUM`

Then add:

`average AUM x effective fee rate - compensation - distribution - technology - operating costs = advisory economics`

If markets rise while flows are negative, ending AUM can reach a record even as the franchise loses clients. If flows move into lower-fee products, revenue can grow more slowly than AUM. If new products require more service or distribution cost, margin can decline even when AUM grows.

## Who controls the relationship and who carries the burden?

| Participant | Value captured | Burden carried |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Asset manager | Research, portfolio management, product design, and client service fees | Investment talent, systems, regulation, distribution, and retention |
| Adviser, retirement plan, or institution | Product selection and client access | Fiduciary duty, fee pressure, and performance review |
| Fund investor | Portfolio exposure and professional management | Market loss, fees, taxes, and liquidity terms |
| Distribution partner | Shelf access and customer relationship | Sales effort, compliance, and client retention |
| Common shareholder | Residual advisory profit | Flows, fee compression, compensation, and market sensitivity |

The manager controls the service and distribution relationship, but does not own
the capital being managed. A record AUM number therefore proves little unless it
is supported by net inflows, client retention, acceptable fee rates, and a cost
structure that produces cash per diluted share.

## The product-transition test

ETFs, SMAs, fixed income, alternatives, and retirement products may be strategically useful. They must be judged by net flows, fee rate, retention, operating cost, and contribution margin. A product can improve AUM and still reduce owner economics if it carries a lower fee or higher delivery cost.

Investment performance also has two effects. It supports client retention and market appreciation, but it requires investment professionals and research spending. Cutting those costs can improve near-term margins while weakening the control point.

## Earnings quality and financial-engineering questions

1. **Market return versus client demand:** Reconcile ending AUM into market
   movement, currency, distributions, gross subscriptions, and redemptions.
2. **Effective fee rate:** Calculate fee revenue divided by average fee-paying
   AUM, by product and channel where possible. A small rate decline applied to a
   large asset base can outweigh considerable AUM growth.
3. **Product substitution:** Determine whether ETFs, SMAs, passive products,
   alternatives, and retirement products replace outflows while reducing fee or
   margin per dollar of AUM.
4. **Performance fees and marks:** Separate recurring advisory fees from
   performance-based income, carried interest, realized gains, and private-asset
   valuation changes. Do not use an unrealized mark as a permanent fee stream.
5. **Compensation as production capacity:** Investment professionals and client
   teams create the service. Repeated cuts can raise reported margin while
   weakening future investment results and retention.
6. **Acquisition returns:** Track acquired AUM, fee rate, integration cost,
   goodwill, contingent consideration, retention, and incremental cash rather
   than counting acquired AUM as organic growth.
7. **Buybacks and dilution:** Compare repurchases with stock compensation and
   diluted shares. Higher EPS is not enough if the share count does not fall or
   if buybacks replace necessary product investment.
8. **Management incentives:** Read the proxy for weights on AUM, revenue,
   adjusted EPS, net flows, operating margin, investment performance, cash per
   share, and return on invested capital. AUM targets can reward market exposure
   or acquisitions even when client economics weaken.

## Lyn Alden lens: market levels and liquidity conditions

Asset managers usually require little physical capital, but their revenue is
highly sensitive to market prices, client risk appetite, and liquidity. When
markets fall, AUM falls and fee revenue often falls with it. Clients may also
move toward cash, lower-fee products, or competitors while compensation and
research costs remain difficult to reduce.

The resilience test is whether the manager can maintain investment capability,
distribution, technology, and client service through a market decline without
damaging the relationship or relying on acquisitions to replace lost flows.
Cash and investments provide flexibility, but they do not prevent permanent
franchise erosion from sustained outflows.

## Damodaran lens: narrative to numbers

The narrative may be that a manager has a trusted brand, strong performance,
retirement access, product breadth, and a long runway for alternatives or
technology. Translate that narrative into:

- net flows by asset class, channel, geography, and product;
- market movement and currency separated from client demand;
- average AUM and effective fee rate;
- investment-performance persistence and retention;
- compensation, distribution, technology, and administration cost;
- product contribution margin and required development spending;
- performance fees, carried interest, and private-asset marks separately;
- acquisition cash, integration, goodwill, and contingent consideration;
- dividends, buybacks, diluted shares, and cash per share; and
- normalized earnings after a market decline.

Value recurring advisory fees after recurring operating costs. Treat performance
income as uncertain until realized and durable. The correct valuation multiple
depends on whether client flows and fee rates are stable, not merely whether
ending AUM is large.

## Cohort comparison

| Business type | Cleaner economic feature | Main uncertainty |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Active public-market manager | Research-based fees and long client relationships | Performance, outflows, compensation, and fee compression |
| Passive ETF or index manager | Scaled distribution and low incremental service cost | Very low fees, market levels, and concentration of flows |
| Alternative asset manager | Higher fees and longer commitments | Realizations, marks, partner claims, leverage, and liquidity |
| Insurance-linked manager | Fee income plus access to permanent capital | Statutory capital, spread, claims, and asset-liability risk |
| Fee-only broker or adviser | Client relationship without investment-balance-sheet risk | Producer retention, acquisitions, and service cost |

The cleanest economics generally belong to recurring fees on retained client
capital with low incremental service cost. The highest reported fee rate is not
necessarily the best economics if it depends on performance marks, realizations,
or capital claims outside the parent company.

## Filing-based falsifiers

The conclusion should be revised if AUM growth comes mainly from market
appreciation, net flows remain negative, fee rates continue to fall, new products
have lower contribution margins, investment performance weakens, compensation
cuts damage retention, acquisitions replace organic growth, performance income
supports ordinary dividends, or buybacks fail to reduce diluted shares.

The next filing should show market movement, net flows, average AUM, fee rate,
product mix, retention, investment performance, compensation, technology cost,
acquisition cash, diluted shares, and owner cash after normal product investment.

## The valuation consequence

An asset manager should be valued from recurring advisory fees after compensation and distribution costs, not from ending AUM alone. The cleanest economics are durable client relationships with stable fees and low incremental service cost. The burden carrier is the product mix that requires continuing investment while clients demand lower prices or leave.
