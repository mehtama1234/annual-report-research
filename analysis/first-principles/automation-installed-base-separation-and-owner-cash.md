# Automation economics: installed base, separation, and owner cash

## The first principle

Industrial automation earns its value by helping a customer operate a process
more safely, reliably, and productively. The system may include sensors,
controls, software, engineering, installation, maintenance, and replacement.
The customer does not buy a software subscription in isolation; it buys a
working process with acceptable downtime and risk.

`customer process -> qualified control interface -> equipment and software -> operation and data -> service, upgrade, replacement, and renewal cash`

The key questions are which part of that chain the company controls, how hard it
is to replace, and who funds the cost of keeping the system reliable.

## Control point versus demand exposure

An automation supplier may control an approved interface, a proprietary control
architecture, field-service expertise, or data embedded in a customer process.
That can create switching cost. It does not control the customer’s capital
budget, project schedule, energy price, construction financing, or final demand.

Backlog is a promise to deliver work under contracts. It is not the same as
cash, and it does not prove that the work will earn the expected margin. A
backlog conversion analysis needs delivery timing, cancellation, change orders,
customer advances, working capital, and margin by project.

## How an installed base becomes recurring cash

The installed base is economically useful only when four conditions hold:

1. The product is important enough that failure or replacement is costly.
2. The supplier remains qualified and trusted after the original sale.
3. The supplier earns recurring service, software, replacement, or upgrade
   revenue.
4. The recurring revenue has a return above engineering, support, inventory,
   cybersecurity, warranty, and product-renewal costs.

Installed units, users, or connected assets are therefore leading indicators.
The cash proof is service attachment, renewal, retention, gross margin, and
cash after the costs needed to protect the installed base.

## Portfolio separation changes the evidence

When a conglomerate separates businesses, investors receive a clearer business
description but also a new accounting problem. Historical revenue, margin,
cash, debt, pensions, taxes, shared services, and corporate costs may no longer
belong to the same entity. A higher multiple after separation is justified only
if the new company’s economics are better after all costs are allocated.

The required bridge is:

`reported conglomerate results -> discontinued operations -> pro forma continuing business -> stranded costs and allocated obligations -> standalone capex, R&D, service, debt, and tax -> owner cash`

Honeywell’s 2025 results illustrate this requirement. Continuing OCF was
$6.075B, but it included a $1.590B indemnification receipt and a $1.428B
asbestos-liability payment. The future automation company must show cash from
customers rather than rely on transaction-year cash movements.

## Who carries the physical and execution burden?

The automation supplier carries engineering, inventory, warranty, field
service, cybersecurity, compliance, and product-renewal costs. The customer
carries facility construction, process downtime, operating labor, and project
financing. Contractors may carry installation and working-capital risk.

This differs from a pure software company. Physical automation has a recurring
relationship, but the supplier must maintain hardware compatibility and field
execution. It also differs from an equipment rental company, which carries a
fleet and utilization risk. The correct comparison charges each model for the
cost it actually funds.

## Forensic cash framework

For an automation company, use this sequence:

1. Start with GAAP operating cash flow from continuing operations.
2. Remove indemnity receipts, litigation settlements, divestiture proceeds,
   unusual tax reversals, and other clearly non-operating cash events.
3. Subtract maintenance and growth capex separately.
4. Charge R&D and software investment needed to preserve the product position.
5. Add acquisitions only after measuring the acquired cohort’s incremental cash.
6. Include separation costs, pensions, debt service, warranties, and recurring
   restructuring when they are required to operate the business.
7. Compare the result with dividends, repurchases, and diluted shares.

The result is not one universal “free cash flow” number. It is a transparent
range showing what must be spent to preserve current economics and what is
being spent to create new economics.

## Alden and Damodaran conclusions

From a durability and liquidity perspective, mission-critical automation can
hold up better than discretionary industrial projects when customers need
productivity, safety, or uptime. It remains exposed to rates, construction,
industrial production, energy markets, and customer financing.

From a narrative-to-numbers perspective, “automation to autonomy” must become
specific revenue, margin, renewal, capital, and return assumptions. A separation
can improve the story, but the valuation must use standalone cash and a
standalone reinvestment rate. The growth period ends when new automation sales
require proportionally more capacity, working capital, R&D, or acquisitions.

## Cohort conclusion

Honeywell, Eaton, Caterpillar, Cummins, Vertiv, and KLA all sit near physical
control points, but they do not have the same economics. Eaton carries
electrical architecture and acquisition burden. Caterpillar carries dealer,
finance, inventory, and equipment-cycle exposure. Cummins carries emissions,
warranty, and power-system cycle risk. Vertiv carries data-center project and
capacity risk. KLA has a more concentrated process-control and service model.
Honeywell adds portfolio-separation risk and a broad installed-base claim.

The useful cohort question is not which company mentions AI or automation most
often. It is which company converts a qualified installed relationship into
recurring cash after service, R&D, capex, working capital, acquisitions, and
balance-sheet obligations.

## Next evidence

Future filings should make service and software attachment, backlog conversion,
maintenance versus growth capex, recurring customer retention, acquisition
returns, stranded costs, and standalone cash visible. If those fields improve
while growth remains organic, the installed-base thesis strengthens. If backlog
and adjusted EPS rise while cash requires transactions and balance-sheet
funding, the thesis weakens.
