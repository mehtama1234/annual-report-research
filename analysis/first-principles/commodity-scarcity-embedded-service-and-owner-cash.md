# Commodity scarcity, embedded service, and owner cash

## Business economics: the first principle

Physical businesses do not all monetize scarcity in the same way. A producer can own a scarce molecule or ore body; a midstream company can own the corridor that moves it; a processor can own conversion capacity; an embedded service company can own the workflow that prevents failure; and a distributor can own the customer relationship that makes replenishment easy.

The investment mistake is to call all of these durable competitive advantages without asking who funds the system and who absorbs the cycle. Scarcity can produce pricing power, but capital intensity, depletion, regulation, working capital, and financing can transfer much of that value away from common shareholders.

The cohort’s economic chain is:

`resource or risk -> extraction / conversion / transport / workflow -> customer dependency -> reinvestment -> owner cash`

## Four different forms of control

### 1. Resource and reserve control: ExxonMobil and ConocoPhillips

ExxonMobil controls an integrated energy system across reserves, refining, chemicals, and specialty products. ConocoPhillips controls a lower-complexity upstream portfolio with resource depth and capital allocation across basins. Both benefit when energy demand, geopolitics, and supply discipline keep molecules valuable.

Resource control does not remove the costs of producing and replacing the resource. Exxon’s FY2025 operating cash of about $52.0B less $28.4B of PP&E left roughly $23.6B before other claims. ConocoPhillips produced about $19.8B of operating cash against a disclosed $12.6B capital program, leaving roughly $7.2B. Those residuals are highly sensitive to commodity prices, reserve replacement, decline rates, and the distinction between sustaining and growth capital.

The scarce asset depletes. The shareholder must continuously buy the next barrel, molecule, or project. A high return on current reserves can coexist with poor long-term owner economics if replacement capital rises faster than production value.

EOG is the clearest independent-producer example in this group. Its 2025
operating cash flow was about $10.0B and its non-GAAP free cash flow was $4.663B
after $6.294B of total capital expenditures. Free cash flow was $5.108B in 2023
and $5.367B in 2024, so the three-year record is stronger evidence than any one
oil-price quarter. Even so, this residual is not fully distributable until the
company proves that drilling and reserve-replacement spending preserves the
future production base.

EOG's 70% minimum cash-return policy makes the tradeoff explicit. In 2025 it
returned about $4.7B through regular dividends and repurchases, while impairing
Barnett and Woodford assets. The policy can align management with owners when
capital is disciplined; it can also expose underinvestment if the company
maintains returns during a weak price cycle. The correct test is production,
proved reserves, decline, unit cost, and cash per share after capital—not the
percentage returned in a favorable year.

### 2. Corridor and conversion control: Cheniere, ONEOK, and Energy Transfer

Cheniere controls a scarce conversion point between U.S. natural gas and global LNG demand. ONEOK controls integrated gathering, processing, NGL, and refined-product logistics. Energy Transfer controls a dense corridor of pipelines, storage, processing, and export-adjacent infrastructure.

These businesses can be more durable than producers because rights-of-way, terminals, contracts, and network density create switching costs. But fee-based does not mean capital-light or risk-free. Utilization, counterparty quality, commodity differentials, joint ventures, debt, NCI, and expansion capital still determine common-equity cash.

Cheniere’s FY2025 post-PP&E residual was about $2.461B, below visible capital returns before debt and other claims. ONEOK’s operating cash and capex bridge was positive, but acquisition integration and affiliate cash must be separated. Energy Transfer’s consolidated cash is not automatically common-unit cash because partnership waterfalls, minority interests, preferred claims, and growth capital intervene.

The corridor gains competitive protection from infrastructure that is difficult to replicate. It earns an investment return only when incremental projects produce cash above their financing and replacement burden.

### 3. Embedded workflow control: Ecolab and Veralto

Ecolab controls customer workflows around water, hygiene, food safety, contamination, and process uptime. Veralto controls measurement, analytics, coding, traceability, and water-quality workflows. Their customers are not merely buying chemicals or instruments; they are buying reduced failure, compliance, and operational certainty.

This can produce cleaner economics than a commodity producer because the relationship is embedded in operating processes and the cost of failure is high. Veralto’s FY2025 operating cash of $1.077B against only $63M of PP&E produced a strong mechanical screen of about $1.014B. Ecolab’s screen was much weaker after acquisition and affiliate cash, about $283M, showing that embedded control does not eliminate field labor, acquisition cost, or working-capital burden.

The key test is whether pricing and service productivity rise faster than labor, input, working-capital, and acquisition costs. Embeddedness is valuable only if it produces cash returns after the company pays to maintain the workflow.

### 4. Capacity and commodity conversion: Nucor

Nucor controls domestic steelmaking, scrap access, downstream fabrication, and a network of production capacity. Its electric-arc-furnace and downstream strategy can create flexibility and customer proximity, but steel remains a spread business exposed to prices, scrap, energy, trade policy, utilization, and construction demand.

FY2025 operating cash less PP&E and acquisitions was approximately negative $190M. That is not proof that the franchise lacks value; it shows the cost of adding or maintaining capacity during a weak part of the steel cycle. The investment case requires mid-cycle margins, high utilization, and downstream projects to earn returns over a full cycle—not just a recovery year.

### 5. Mined resource and processing system: Freeport-McMoRan

Freeport controls copper resources, mines, processing facilities, and operating
rights. That is a different economic position from an oil producer or a steel
mill because future output depends on ore grades, mine sequencing, recoveries,
underground development, permits, and closure obligations.

Its FY2025 filing shows $5.610B of operating cash flow and $4.494B of productive-
asset spending, leaving about $1.116B before dividends and other claims. The
company also reports about $3.836B of asset-retirement obligations and $2.0B of
environmental obligations. Copper demand from power and digital infrastructure
can be durable while shareholder cash remains cyclical because Grasberg
disruption, reserve replacement, and capital spending determine actual output.

### 6. Contracted industrial-gas infrastructure: Air Products

Air Products supplies gases through customer-site plants, pipelines, and
merchant distribution. The customer may depend on uninterrupted supply, which
can support long contracts and switching costs. But the supplier still owns
and maintains physical plants, buys energy, employs technical operators, and
must fund replacement and expansion capital.

The FY2025 filing makes the reinvestment issue visible. Operating cash flow was
$3.257B. Management-defined capital expenditures were $5.064B, while total
additions to plant and equipment were $7.023B because part of the NEOM joint
venture spending was funded by partners. The simple shareholder-cash screen
was therefore negative after management capex and acquisitions. The later exit
from major clean-energy projects is evidence that a contracted or strategically
important project can still fail its required return.

The first-principles test is to separate the mature industrial-gas base from
large projects. For the base, measure contract minimums, energy-cost recovery,
plant utilization, maintenance capital, and return on invested capital. For
projects, measure customer funding, partner funding, parent guarantees, debt,
construction cost, start date, expected utilization, and cancellation or
impairment history. Contract duration improves visibility; it does not remove
capital risk or prove that the project creates common-shareholder cash.

### 7. Nuclear-fuel platform: Cameco

Cameco shows that a scarce physical input can be managed through a system rather
than sold directly from a mine. The system includes uranium production,
procurement, inventory, conversion, long-term customer contracts, and the
Westinghouse reactor-services investment. Production was 21.0 million pounds in
2025 while deliveries were 33.0 million pounds. The difference is explained by
inventory and purchased material, so mine output is not the same as sales.

The 2025 IFRS filing reported CAD $1.408B of operating cash flow and CAD $333M
of productive-asset spending, leaving a CAD $1.075B screening residual. That
cash result benefited from contract pricing and delivery activity, but it must
be tested against inventory replacement, mine sustaining capital, conversion
maintenance, political restrictions, and Westinghouse project economics.

The first-principles test is to ask where the scarce resource becomes valuable:
in the ore body, in qualified conversion capacity, in inventory available for
delivery, in long-term contracts, or in reactor services. Each layer has a
different owner and a different burden. A long contract can improve demand
visibility while forcing the supplier to obtain material later at a higher
cost. Equity-method earnings from Westinghouse can increase reported profit
without appearing as consolidated revenue or same-period cash. Nuclear demand
is therefore a strong strategic fact, not a complete shareholder-return model.

### 8. Royalty and streaming ownership: Franco-Nevada

Franco-Nevada sits above the operator. It owns contractual rights to a share of
production or revenue, while the mine or well operator pays the labor, energy,
equipment, processing, and sustaining-capital costs. In 2025 the company sold
519K GEOs, 85% of revenue came from precious metals, and operating cash flow
was about $1.494B against only $2M of property-and-equipment purchases.

This is cleaner cash conversion than a mine owner’s, but the capital burden has
not disappeared. It has moved into acquisition underwriting. Franco-Nevada
must pay for new royalties and streams before their production is certain. It
also depends on operators to obtain permits, build projects, produce material,
and make payments. A royalty can have excellent contractual terms and still
produce no cash when an operator is delayed or insolvent.

The first-principles test is to separate existing producing royalties,
development-stage optionality, commodity-price exposure, and undeployed
capital. Compare cash paid for each acquisition with actual GEOs, start dates,
operator capital, write-downs, and long-run returns. The royalty model reduces
direct inflation and operating risk; it does not remove valuation risk or
counterparty risk.

### 10. Crop-flow infrastructure: Bunge Global

Bunge sits between farmers and food, feed, biofuel, and industrial customers. It
creates value through origination, storage, transport, processing, merchandising,
and customer access. Its revenue is large because it handles large commodity
volumes; revenue is not the same as the processing or trading spread that the
company keeps.

The FY2025 filing shows the working-capital and acquisition burden. Operating
cash flow was $844M, PP&E purchases were $1.723B, and business acquisitions,
primarily the Viterra combination, were $4.201B. The simple post-growth screen
was negative $5.080B. Inventory was $13.198B, receivables were $3.870B, and debt
was $14.051B. The network can redirect crop flows when weather or trade changes,
but that flexibility requires financing.

The first-principles test is to separate processing spreads, merchandising
results, biofuel demand, Viterra synergies, and mark-to-market changes. Then
test whether synergies become cash after integration costs, inventory funding,
interest, and plant investment. Bunge is not a simple commodity producer: it
owns a coordination and processing network, but carries a large liquidity and
working-capital burden.

### Nitrogen inputs: CF Industries

CF starts with natural gas, air, and industrial plants and produces ammonia
and downstream nitrogen fertilizer. Its control point is reliable high-volume
production in a market where plant outages, gas access, and global trade can
change available supply. Food demand is important, but demand alone does not
determine shareholder returns.

In FY2025, CF generated $2.752B of operating cash flow and spent $950M on PP&E,
leaving a $1.802B filed screen. Cash was $1.982B, debt was about $3.250B,
inventory was $383M, and net receivables were $488M. Compared with Bunge, CF
has less working-capital financing pressure and more direct exposure to gas
cost, plant utilization, and maintenance requirements.

The first-principles test is to rebuild the margin from realized nitrogen
prices, gas cost per ton, other cash costs, production, utilization, and
outages. Then separate sustaining capital from growth and low-carbon projects.
Blue Point should be valued as a project with construction cost, partner cash,
offtake, policy support, and operating risk until those inputs are demonstrated.
High utilization and strong nitrogen prices can produce excellent current cash;
they do not by themselves establish a permanent return on capital.

### Integrated crop inputs: Nutrien

Nutrien adds potash, phosphate, nitrogen, retail stores, crop consultants, and
proprietary products to the upstream fertilizer analysis. Its proposed control
point is the combination of scarce nutrient assets and direct farmer access.
That combination can improve information, distribution, and product mix, but it
also creates more businesses whose capital and working-capital needs must be
funded.

Nutrien generated $4.007B of FY2025 operating cash flow and reported $1.979B of
free cash flow after sustaining capital, mine development, pre-stripping, and
lease-principal repayment. It held $6.977B of inventory and $5.675B of
receivables, with $10.736B of debt. Operating cash also benefited from an
increase in North American supplier financing. The first-principles question is
whether retail and potash scale make cash more resilient after these claims, or
whether the integrated model mainly increases the amount of capital exposed to
fertilizer prices and farmer financing.

The correct test is segment-level: compare Retail, Potash, Nitrogen, and
Phosphate margins and cash, then reconcile supplier-financing changes, mine
renewal, lease claims, environmental obligations, and portfolio-sale proceeds.
Automation can lower potash unit cost, and retail can improve customer access;
neither fact proves that consolidated free cash flow is durable through a
weaker fertilizer cycle.

### Integrated chemical conversion: Dow

Dow is a different heavy-input system. It converts feedstock and energy through
large plants into polyethylene, intermediates, coatings, silicones, and other
materials used by downstream manufacturers. Its control point is a combination
of scale, integration, process knowledge, customer qualification, and reliable
chemical supply.

In FY2025, continuing operating cash flow was $1.062B against $2.479B of PP&E
spending, $157M of gas-field development, and $32M of affiliate investment.
The resulting negative $1.606B screen sat beside $18.161B of gross debt, $6.595B
of inventory, and $4.762B of receivables. Dow's large revenue and operating
EBITDA therefore cannot be read as owner cash without a spread and renewal test.

The first-principles question is whether Transform to Outperform creates cash
after site closures, workforce reductions, environmental work, pensions, and
replacement capacity. Higher polyethylene prices can improve a quarter
quickly; durable value requires mid-cycle spreads, adequate plant renewal, and
debt that does not absorb the recovery.

### Polyolefin spread and feedstock advantage: LyondellBasell

LyondellBasell is a more direct polyolefin and petrochemical spread case. Its
North American feedstock position can lower input cost, while its global plants,
joint ventures, and commercial network determine where production earns a
return. The advantage is economic only after utilization, freight, inventory,
plant renewal, and debt are included.

In FY2025, LyondellBasell generated $2.262B of operating cash flow and spent
$1.878B on PP&E, leaving only $384M before dividends and financing claims. The
same screen was $3.411B in 2023 and $1.980B in 2024. The decline shows why the
Cash Improvement Plan must be separated into recurring savings, working-capital
timing, asset-sale proceeds, and deferred maintenance.

The first-principles test is to value regional chemical spreads at mid-cycle
levels and then ask whether the remaining assets can fund renewal capital. A
geopolitical disruption can improve a quarter's margin; it does not establish a
permanent cost-curve advantage.

### Diversified bulk materials and critical-minerals allocation: Rio Tinto

Rio Tinto shows why a large resource portfolio must be analyzed as a capital-
allocation system, not as a simple collection of commodity prices. In 2025 the
company generated $16.8B of operating cash flow but reported $4.025B of free
cash flow after the capital required by its mines, processing assets, projects,
intangibles, and leases. Capital expenditure before partner funding was
$12.285B, and net debt ended at $14.4B after the Arcadium acquisition and large
dividends.

The economic claim is that Pilbara iron ore can fund copper, aluminium, and
lithium growth. That claim works only if the new projects earn an acceptable
return after construction overruns, ramp delays, partner ownership, taxes,
environmental work, closure obligations, and the capital needed to replace
depleted reserves. First production is an operating milestone, not proof of
value creation.

The three-year cash record is important: free cash flow was $7.657B in 2023,
$5.553B in 2024, and $4.025B in 2025. Operating cash remained substantial, but
the residual declined as replacement and growth investment rose. A diversified
miner therefore needs two separate tests: whether the existing cash contributor is
durable through commodity cycles, and whether management can redeploy that cash
into projects whose per-share returns exceed the value of dividends or buybacks.

Forensic review should separate cash from controlled operations, equity-
accounted units, non-controlling interests, partner funding, asset sales, and
project debt. It should also track grades, recovery, unit costs, reserve life,
rehabilitation provisions, and the share of each project that belongs to common
owners. Diversification reduces dependence on one commodity only when the new
assets produce better risk-adjusted cash; otherwise it increases the number of
ways capital can be trapped.

### Brazilian ore corridor and trust-repair burden: Vale

Vale is a useful countercase to Rio Tinto because both have large iron-ore and
logistics systems, but Vale's cash must be read alongside a more visible legacy
burden. In 2025 Vale generated $8.801B of operating cash flow and reported
$4.762B of recurring free cash flow. It also spent $6.006B on PP&E and
intangibles, paid $874M for Brumadinho and $2.298B related to Samarco, and ended
the year with $15.579B of expanded net debt.

The company is trying to fund a second major business. Vale Base Metals adjusted EBITDA
rose to $3.355B in 2025 from $1.453B in 2024, while iron ore production reached
336 million tonnes. That is meaningful operating evidence, but it is not yet
proof that diversification creates value. Vale also recorded a $4.599B
impairment and disposal charge, which shows that prior assumptions about some
assets or prices did not hold.

The first-principles test is to show recurring FCF both before and after the
cash actually paid for remediation, dam de-characterization, and Samarco. A
liability can be classified as non-recurring for a particular year while still
being a recurring economic feature of ownership. The next test is to separate
iron-ore cash, copper and nickel project returns, derivative settlements,
working-capital timing, partner claims, and common-owner distributions.

Vale demonstrates that trust is part of the asset base. Dam safety, remediation,
water, community obligations, and permitting are not external to valuation;
they affect the cash that can be distributed and the capital required to keep
the operating assets licensed.

### Copper-centered diversified allocation: BHP

BHP is the third large allocator in this comparison. It still has a major
Australian iron-ore cash contributor, but copper provided 51% of Underlying EBITDA in
the FY2026 half-year results. In FY2025 BHP generated $18.692B of operating cash
flow and $5.342B of free cash flow after $9.794B of capital and exploration
spending. It also paid $1.8B of Samarco settlement obligations and ended with
$12.924B of net debt.

The first-principles issue is the size of the reinvestment step. Free cash flow
was $5.636B in FY2023, $11.903B in FY2024, and $5.342B in FY2025 while operating
cash remained near $19B-$21B. The residual is therefore sensitive to prices,
capital timing, project funding, and legacy claims. A record copper year does
not prove value creation unless the additional copper cash exceeds the capital
required to produce it and increases cash per BHP share.

BHP's potash and copper projects make the lifecycle analysis explicit. Each
project needs an original and revised capital budget, expected production,
ownership, ramp schedule, unit costs, taxes, closure costs, and return on
capital. Divestitures and streaming proceeds can strengthen the balance sheet,
but they are capital-recycling events rather than repeatable earnings. The
clean conclusion is conditional: BHP may become a higher-quality copper
platform, but the market already requires a large increase in normalized owner
cash.

### Mining plus marketing: Glencore

Glencore adds a different form of resource control. It owns mines and
processing assets, but it also buys, transports, finances, and sells commodities
through a large marketing business. In 2025 Marketing Adjusted EBIT was about
$2.9B, Funds from Operations were $8.714B, and net capex was $6.9B. The simple
FFO-less-capex residual was only about $1.814B after the industrial capital
burden.

The missing denominator is working capital. Net funding reached $39.405B,
including readily marketable inventories, and 2025 cash benefited from a $1.6B
release in non-RMI working capital plus about $1.0B of investment inflows,
mainly from the Viterra disposal. Those are not equivalent to recurring
earnings. They show how the marketing model can generate cash, but also how
inventory prices, prepayments, counterparties, leases, and financing can change
the cash requirement quickly.

The first-principles test is to value the industrial assets and marketing arm
separately. Marketing deserves credit for logistics knowledge, customer access,
and risk management only after through-cycle EBIT covers financing, inventory
losses, leases, staff, and compliance. A copper-production target is also not
enough: the next project must earn a return after the funding required to carry
the broader trading system.

### 9. Streaming portfolio: Wheaton Precious Metals

Wheaton is another way to own precious-metals exposure without operating the
underlying mines. The operator pays the labor, energy, equipment, processing,
and sustaining-capital costs. Wheaton pays upfront for the right to receive
future metal and then makes contractually defined ongoing payments.

The 2025 filing shows why physical capex is not enough to measure reinvestment.
Wheaton generated $1.905B of operating cash flow, but total investing cash
outflow was $1.279B. The conservative residual was about $626M, while property-
and-equipment purchases were only about $2M. The business is light on physical
capex but not light on capital allocation.

The Antamina transaction makes the financing question concrete. Wheaton moved
from a debt-free year-end balance sheet to roughly $2.0B of debt by Q2 2026.
Future production can justify that debt, but only if the stream starts on time,
operators deliver, metal prices support margins, and the cash base reduces
leverage. Streaming is therefore a cleaner operating model than mining, not a
model without reinvestment or balance-sheet risk.

## The real distinction: scarcity versus scarcity plus renewal

The strongest physical competitive advantages have two components:

1. Scarcity or embeddedness creates pricing power today.
2. Reinvestment creates the next unit of productive capacity without destroying returns.

Producers face depletion and reserve replacement. Midstream networks face expansion and maintenance capital. Embedded services face labor and acquisition reinvestment. Steelmakers face utilization and modernization capex. The shareholder should ask whether the business can renew its scarce position from internally generated cash.

## Earnings-quality tests across the cohort

### Commodity price versus operating improvement

For Exxon, ConocoPhillips, and Nucor, reported earnings can rise because prices or spreads rise. That is not necessarily management-created durability. Normalize through a mid-cycle price deck, then test volume, unit cost, decline, utilization, and capital intensity.

### Adjusted EBITDA versus common-equity cash

For Cheniere, ONEOK, and Energy Transfer, adjusted EBITDA can include unconsolidated affiliates, derivative marks, or cash flows not wholly available to common owners. Reconcile consolidated OCF, maintenance capex, growth capex, debt, NCI, preferred claims, distributions, and unit-level cash.

### Service revenue versus service burden

For Ecolab and Veralto, recurring workflow revenue is more durable than spot commodity sales, but it still requires technicians, consumables, software, support, and acquisitions. Track organic growth, retention, price/volume/mix, field productivity, working capital, and acquisition cohorts.

### Capital returns versus renewal capital

Buybacks and dividends are only evidence of shareholder friendliness if they remain covered after the business funds the next reserve, pipeline, plant, service network, or compliance requirement. A company can create an attractive per-share history by distributing cash at the top of a cycle and borrowing through the bottom.

## Lyn Alden lens

The cohort is exposed to the long physical cycle of energy, materials, infrastructure, and industrial capacity. Inflation can help nominal prices but also raises replacement cost, labor expense, and required capital. Higher rates can delay projects, impair commodity-sensitive customers, and lower the value of long-lived infrastructure. Geopolitics, energy security, trade restrictions, and grid or water scarcity can strengthen the strategic value of assets while simultaneously increasing regulatory and capital requirements.

The most durable cash tends to sit where demand is non-discretionary and substitution is difficult, but the best macro position can still be a poor stock if the market capitalizes peak cash or the company must reinvest every dollar to preserve the asset.

## Damodaran lens

Value these companies by lifecycle:

- Upstream energy: normalize price, production, decline, reserve replacement, and sustaining capital.
- Midstream/LNG: separate contracted maintenance cash from growth projects, affiliate claims, leverage, and commodity-linked exposure.
- Steel/materials: use mid-cycle spreads and utilization, not peak margins.
- Embedded service: model organic growth, retention, labor productivity, and acquisition returns separately.

The narrative becomes investable only when reinvestment is explicit. “Scarcity” is not a cash flow. It is an input into pricing, volumes, duration, and return on new capital.

## Cohort conclusion

Resource producers own the raw input but bear depletion and commodity risk. Midstream networks own the corridor but bear leverage and expansion risk. Embedded service companies own the workflow and may produce cleaner cash, but they bear labor and acquisition risk. Materials producers own capacity but bear utilization and spread risk.

The cleanest economics belong to the company that converts a non-discretionary customer problem into repeat cash while requiring the least renewal capital. The most dangerous accounting illusion is to mistake current scarcity, adjusted EBITDA, or one favorable commodity period for durable owner cash. Future filings should be read for the renewal burden: reserve replacement, maintenance capex, affiliate cash, working capital, field labor, acquisition returns, utilization, and capital-return coverage.
