# Contractor economics: backlog, field execution, and collected cash

## Business economics: the first principle

A contractor converts a customer’s infrastructure plan into a finished asset.
The contractor must estimate the work, secure labor and equipment, procure
materials, manage changes, complete milestones, bill the customer, and collect
cash.

`customer capital plan -> bid and contract -> labor/equipment/materials -> field execution -> milestone billing -> collection -> replacement and new-work capacity`

The company’s control point is execution under constraints. The customer still
controls whether the project is funded, redesigned, delayed, or cancelled.

## Why backlog is an incomplete operating metric

Backlog is a stock of contracted or expected work. It becomes owner cash only
after scope, cost, schedule, margin, billing, collection, and execution are
proven. A backlog can grow while value falls if new work is priced too low,
costs rise, or fixed-price projects absorb losses.

The useful backlog table separates fixed work, master-service agreements,
options, unsigned or undefined scope, cancellations, expected burn, and margin
by project cohort. It also asks how much of the backlog requires customer
financing or regulatory approval.

## Contract accounting requires a second reading

Long-term construction contracts often recognize revenue as work progresses.
That requires estimates of total cost, percentage complete, change orders,
claims, and expected losses. Profit may be recognized before the customer pays.

Contract assets represent work recognized before billing. Contract liabilities
represent billing or cash received before performance. Either can be legitimate.
The forensic test is whether these balances convert through normal project
completion and collection or accumulate because estimates and billing disputes
are worsening.

## Who carries the burden?

The contractor carries labor availability, wage inflation, equipment ownership,
maintenance, safety, weather, productivity, procurement, bonding, warranty,
claims, and often fixed-price cost risk. The customer carries the asset’s
financing and long-term operating economics. Equipment manufacturers may carry
the product warranty while contractors carry installation risk.

Self-performance can increase control and margin capture, but it also creates a
larger fixed cost base. The correct question is whether the company can reduce
capacity and equipment commitments when project awards slow.

## Cash framework

For a contractor, analyze:

1. GAAP operating cash flow by year and by working-capital component.
2. Contract assets, liabilities, receivables, retainage, and customer advances.
3. Cash capex for equipment and facilities, split between maintenance and growth.
4. Equipment leases, debt repayment, interest, bonding, and surety requirements.
5. Project-loss provisions, cost-to-complete revisions, claims, and legal cash.
6. Acquisitions and integration costs, if growth is purchased.
7. Cash remaining before dividends, repurchases, and equity issuance.

A cash increase from customer advances is not equivalent to a cash increase from
completed, profitable work. A cash decrease from contract-asset growth is not
automatically bad, but it must reverse when billing and collections occur.

## Alden and Damodaran conclusions

From a durability and liquidity perspective, grid renewal, power generation,
communications, and industrial maintenance can support long demand cycles. The
contractor remains exposed to rates, permitting, customer budgets, weather,
labor, and project financing. Liquidity is strongest when cash collection,
bonding capacity, and equipment commitments remain flexible.

From a narrative-to-numbers perspective, “infrastructure buildout” must become
segment revenue, backlog conversion, project margin, equipment returns, working
capital, and normalized owner cash. Growth without cost discipline destroys
value even when the market opportunity is large.

## Valuation: backlog must earn its funding

Valuation should separate awarded fixed work, master-service arrangements, options, unsigned scope, equipment and systems revenue, and recurring service. The scenario inputs are backlog burn, project margin, cost-to-complete revisions, labor and equipment utilization, contract assets, retainage, customer advances, maintenance and growth capex, bonding, debt, acquisitions, and diluted shares.

A backlog premium is justified only when work converts into collected gross profit and cash after the contractor funds crews, equipment, claims, working capital, and replacement capacity. A large backlog with weak project margins, rising contract assets, or expanding equipment commitments is an obligation to execute, not a measure of intrinsic value.

## Cohort conclusion

Primoris, Quanta, EMCOR, Comfort Systems, MasTec, Sterling, and Vertiv occupy
different points in the field-execution and infrastructure chain. Quanta and
EMCOR may benefit from recurring utility or mechanical-electrical relationships;
Comfort Systems has strong project demand but labor and backlog conversion
remain critical; MasTec carries communications and energy project risk;
Sterling carries heavy-civil execution and equipment burden; Vertiv sells
equipment and systems into data-center capacity but carries manufacturing and
project mix risk. Primoris is especially useful because strong utilities and
energy demand coexists with visible renewable-project losses.

The cohort conclusion is conditional: the cleaner economics sit with the party
that controls a scarce standard, repeat service relationship, or differentiated
execution capability, but every contractor must prove that backlog converts into
cash after labor, equipment, claims, and working capital.

## Risks, accounting tests, and what to check next

The thesis weakens if backlog grows while project margins fall, contract assets and retainage age, loss provisions or cost-to-complete revisions rise, customer advances are required to support cash, labor and equipment commitments exceed awards, or acquisitions and adjusted measures hide weak organic cash.

Future filings should provide project-loss reserves, backlog composition, contract-asset aging, cash collection, equipment utilization, capex returns, labor productivity, and margin by work type. If those improve while backlog converts, the thesis strengthens. If backlog grows while contract assets, loss provisions, and equipment commitments rise, the thesis weakens.
