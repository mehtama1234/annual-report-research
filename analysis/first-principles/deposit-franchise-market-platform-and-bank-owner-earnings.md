# Deposit franchise, market platform, and bank owner earnings

## Business economics: the comparison question

Large banks are often described as diversified, but diversification does not
make earnings interchangeable. A bank can earn from deposits, lending,
payments, cards, wealth, investment banking, trading, and securities. Each line
has a different funding source, risk, capital requirement, and cycle.

The common question is:

`which customer relationship funds the balance sheet -> which activity earns the spread or fee -> who carries the loss and capital burden`

## JPMorgan, Bank of America, and U.S. Bancorp

| Company | Main control points | FY2025 starting evidence | Main burden |
|---|---|---|---|
| JPMorgan | Deposits, payments, corporate relationships, markets, cards, technology | $182.4B revenue, $57.0B net income, $2.56T deposits | $14.2B credit provision, market risk, technology, capital, legal and control cost |
| Bank of America | Consumer deposits, digital banking, payments, wealth, corporate and markets relationships | $113.1B revenue, $30.5B net income, $2.02T year-end deposits | Deposit repricing, consumer credit, markets cyclicality, technology, capital and regulation |
| U.S. Bancorp | Payments, treasury management, deposits, trust, and institutional servicing | $28.7B FY2025 net revenue, $7.6B net income, 18.1% RoTCE | Payment network and fraud cost, deposit competition, commercial credit, BTIG integration, technology, capital and regulation |

These banks have enormous customer and funding networks. The investment
difference is not simply size. It is the quality and cost of deposits, the risk
of the loan book, the proportion of earnings from markets and investment
banking, the ability to earn above capital costs, and the discipline of capital
returns.

U.S. Bancorp adds a specific test: do payments and trust workflows make
earnings cleaner after network fees, rewards, fraud, technology, and capital,
or do they merely move the burden from credit losses to operating and control
expense? Fee mix is valuable only if net fee profit and returns remain strong
when market activity and loan spreads normalize.

## The bank cash denominator

Industrial owner cash is usually operating cash less maintenance capital and
acquisitions. That formula does not work for banks. Deposits fund loans and
securities; loans are operating assets; capital is a regulatory requirement;
and cash retained in the bank supports liquidity and loss absorption.

The more useful denominator is normalized common earnings after:

- deposit and wholesale funding cost;
- expected and unexpected credit losses;
- employee compensation and technology;
- legal, compliance, fraud, and operational resilience;
- required common equity and stress capital;
- prudent balance-sheet growth and dilution.

Reported net income is a starting point, not the final owner-cash measure.

## What confirms a deposit moat

Deposit scale is valuable when balances remain stable at a low cost across a
rate and liquidity cycle. The relevant measures are:

- average deposits and end-period deposits;
- insured versus uninsured balances;
- deposit beta and rate paid;
- checking-account retention and direct-deposit activity;
- payment volume and active digital users;
- wholesale funding dependence;
- liquidity coverage and stress outflows.

Growing deposits at a high rate paid may increase balance-sheet size without
creating attractive earnings. A large deposit base that is stable, low-cost,
and connected to payments and wealth services is more valuable.

## Credit is the principal hidden burden

Credit losses are recognized through provisions and charge-offs, but the full
risk is distributed across consumer cards, mortgages, commercial loans,
commercial real estate, leveraged finance, small business, and securities.

The test is not whether current delinquencies are low. It is whether the reserve
framework and capital buffer cover losses if unemployment rises, asset prices
fall, collateral weakens, and loan growth has recently accelerated.

Reserve releases increase current earnings without improving the portfolio.
Reserve builds reduce current earnings without necessarily showing a permanent
loss. The analysis must reconcile reserve coverage, loss emergence, charge-off
vintage, portfolio growth, and macro assumptions.

## Market earnings and cycle normalization

Markets and investment banking can generate high fee income during volatility,
issuance, mergers, and active client trading. The same platform can produce
lower revenue when volatility and issuance fall.

The proper valuation uses a through-cycle contribution. A record market quarter
should not be treated as a permanent annual run rate, and a weak quarter should
not be treated as a permanent franchise failure. The relevant test is whether
the platform earns an attractive return after compensation, technology, risk
capital, and control costs.

## Technology, fraud, and compliance

Bank technology spending has two parts:

1. growth and productivity investment, such as digital onboarding, payments,
   analytics, and AI; and
2. maintenance and control spending, such as cybersecurity, fraud prevention,
   resilience, regulatory reporting, and system replacement.

Only the first category can reasonably be evaluated as discretionary growth
investment. Reducing the second can improve the current efficiency ratio while
raising future loss, outage, remediation, and regulatory costs.

The filing test is whether cost per account, service quality, fraud loss,
operational incidents, and regulatory findings improve together.

## Capital allocation and valuation

Bank shares are valued through earnings, tangible book value, return on tangible
common equity, dividends, repurchases, and expected capital requirements. A
buyback is accretive only if the shares are purchased below a reasonable measure
of intrinsic value and the bank retains enough capital for stress conditions.

The market is paying for more than current earnings when it assigns a premium
to tangible book value. It is paying for sustained return on equity, stable
deposits, credit quality, and the ability to grow without raising expensive
capital.

## Alden and Damodaran integration

The macro question is whether the bank can fund withdrawals and absorb credit,
market, and collateral stress when liquidity tightens. Deposit stability and
capital matter more than a single quarter of net interest income.

The lifecycle question is whether each growth engine earns above the cost of
capital after credit and regulatory burdens. Mature deposit franchises can
compound, but growth in low-quality loans or expensive deposits destroys value.

The valuation question is whether the market’s implied return on tangible
equity remains possible after normalizing rates, credit losses, markets income,
technology, and stress capital.

## Risks, accounting tests, and what to check next

The bank-franchise thesis weakens if deposit costs rise faster than asset yields,
credit losses rise faster than reserves and capital, market revenue carries too
much of earnings, technology and compliance costs recur without control gains,
or capital returns force a future equity raise or payout reduction.

The cleanest confirming evidence is stable low-cost deposits, risk-adjusted loan
growth, through-cycle fee income, improving efficiency after full technology
cost, strong stress capital, and lower diluted shares without weakened buffers.
