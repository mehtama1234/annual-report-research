# Enterprise services economics: AI demand, labor, and owner cash

## Business economics: the first principle

Enterprise technology adoption requires more than software. A client must
choose a problem, redesign work, integrate systems, manage security and data,
train people, and operate the result. A professional-services firm can be
paid to perform those steps.

`enterprise problem -> advisory/design -> implementation -> operating support -> renewal/expansion`

The service firm controls access, expertise, delivery methods, and talent. The
client still controls the budget, internal capability, and decision to continue.

## Bookings are not a recurring contract

Bookings show contracted demand but not delivery margin, collection, renewal,
or the amount of labor required. AI bookings can include assessment, data
preparation, model implementation, workflow integration, governance, and
managed operation. Each has different duration and economics.

The required proof chain is bookings, revenue, collected cash, service margin,
renewal or expansion, and cash per employee after training and retention.

## AI changes the labor equation

AI can create more implementation work because clients need help redesigning
processes and governing new systems. It can also reduce the human hours needed
for routine delivery and increase customer bargaining power. The economic result
depends on whether the service firm captures more work, raises productivity,
or faces price compression.

Track utilization, pricing, wage cost, employee count, attrition, training,
revenue per employee, cash per employee, and managed-service attachment. AI
bookings without productivity or renewal evidence are incomplete.

## Who carries the burden?

The service firm carries talent, recruiting, training, bench, delivery,
cybersecurity, contract, travel, and acquisition-integration burden. The client
carries internal change, data, compliance, and technology budgets. The software
vendor owns the product and may capture the higher-margin recurring license.

This explains why an implementation intermediary can be strategically important
without owning the deepest software moat.

## Forensic cash framework

Analyze:

1. GAAP operating cash and working-capital movements.
2. Receivables, contract assets, deferred revenue, and milestone billing.
3. Acquisitions and acquired versus organic growth.
4. Stock compensation, diluted shares, and repurchase requirements.
5. Training, R&D, business optimization, severance, and delivery capacity.
6. Utilization, pricing, wage inflation, attrition, and revenue per employee.
7. Cash after all of those claims and before capital returns.

Reported free cash flow can be strong while the firm must continually replace
employee equity, retrain staff, and purchase capabilities to preserve its
market position.

## Alden and Damodaran conclusions

Enterprise transformation and cost-reduction work can remain important through
some downturns, but discretionary consulting and large technology programs are
budget-sensitive. Liquidity is strongest when clients pay predictably and the
firm can flex hiring and acquisitions.

The narrative-to-numbers model must state how AI changes revenue mix, labor
productivity, margin, renewal, training, and reinvestment. A large total
addressable market is not a substitute for cash per diluted share.

## Valuation: price the delivered economics

Valuation should separate project revenue, managed services, software resale, and recurring platform or support fees. The relevant scenario inputs are bookings conversion, utilization, pricing, wage and training cost, attrition, managed-service renewal, contract-asset collection, acquisition spending, stock compensation, and cash per diluted share.

A service firm deserves a higher valuation when implementation creates a durable renewal relationship and productivity improves without weakening quality. A high bookings multiple is not justified when the work still requires rising headcount, repeated acquisitions, heavy customer concessions, or working capital that grows faster than revenue.

## Cohort conclusion

Accenture, Adobe, ServiceNow, Microsoft, and implementation-heavy contractors
monetize different layers of the technology stack. Software platforms may own
the recurring application relationship; Accenture owns the trusted change and
deployment workflow but carries more human-capital burden. Physical contractors
carry equipment and project risk. The cleaner economics depend on who controls
renewal and who must continually fund delivery capacity.

The strongest service model is one where implementation creates recurring
managed operations, retention is high, productivity improves, and acquisition
spending does not outrun per-share cash growth.

## Risks, accounting tests, and what to check next

The thesis weakens if AI bookings do not convert to collected revenue, utilization and pricing fall, wage and training costs rise faster than productivity, attrition damages delivery quality, contract assets or receivables grow without collection, acquisitions hide weak organic growth, or stock compensation absorbs the reported cash benefit.

At each filing, track AI bookings conversion, managed-services attachment, utilization, pricing, attrition, wage cost, training, stock compensation, acquired growth, contract assets, collections, and cash per employee. The next test is whether the service firm is capturing a larger recurring relationship or merely staffing a temporary implementation cycle.
