# Financial companies: which money belongs to shareholders?

## Business economics: start with what the company owes

A bank accepts deposits and uses funding to make loans or buy securities. An insurer collects premiums and promises to pay covered claims. An investment manager earns fees for managing assets that usually belong to clients. An exchange earns fees for trading and related services and may hold collateral to protect against participants failing to meet their obligations. These are different businesses, even though each handles money.

The first accounting question is ownership. A customer's deposit creates an obligation for the bank. Assets managed for a client do not become the investment manager's property. Collateral held to protect a market cannot be treated as cash freely available for dividends. Large balances therefore tell us little about shareholder value until we understand the associated obligations.

A second question concerns timing. A company may receive cash today and pay a claim much later. In the meantime, its accounts may rely on estimates of future losses or benefits. If those estimates prove too low, later costs can reduce the profit that appeared available today. This is a reason to examine the estimates and their history; it is not, by itself, evidence of misconduct.

Finally, some capital must stay in the business. Banks and insurers need resources to absorb losses and satisfy regulatory requirements. A profitable company may therefore be unable to distribute all its earnings. The analysis must connect reported profit to losses, funding costs, customer obligations and the capital needed to continue operating.

The studies below apply these questions to exchanges, card issuers, banks and investment managers. Figures belong to the reporting periods identified in the company studies. They should not be compared as though fees, insurance earnings and lending income were interchangeable measures of business quality.

## Why profit and cash available for dividends differ

**Interest spread** is the difference between what a financial company earns on
assets and what it pays to fund them. **Float** is money held before a future
claim or payment is due; it can be invested, but it is not automatically owned
by common shareholders. **Reserves** are amounts set aside for expected losses
or future benefits. **Regulatory capital** is equity that must remain available
to protect depositors, policyholders, counterparties, or the financial system.

The reading sequence is:

`customer funds or client assets -> financial asset or risk written -> interest, fee, premium, or performance income -> claims, losses, rewards, funding, and operating expense -> reserve and capital requirement -> amount available for common owners`

This sequence matters because the income statement records revenue and expense
before the analyst has decided how much capital must remain inside the business.
A bank can report net income while retaining earnings to support loan growth. An
insurer can report adjusted earnings while keeping assets against future
benefits. A card issuer can report fee and interest income while funding
receivables and rewards. An asset manager can report fee income while the client
owns the assets and the manager bears mostly compensation and distribution cost.

The correct question is not “How much cash entered the company?” It is:

1. What customer or client obligation created the cash?
2. What asset, claim, or counterparty exposure must remain funded?
3. Which losses could occur after the current period?
4. What capital must remain in the regulated subsidiary or operating platform?
5. What compensation, partner, debt, and dilution claims reduce the common
   shareholder's residual?

Only the amount left after those questions is a reasonable starting point for
common-owner distributions. The exact calculation differs by sector, but the
order of reasoning does not.

## Different sources of financial earnings

### 1. Trading and clearing services: CME Group

CME controls a trusted exchange, clearing, collateral, and market-data workflow. Its value comes from liquidity, standardization, netting, risk management, and participant habit. The network becomes more valuable as more users need to hedge or transfer risk, and physical capital requirements are low relative to revenue.

The complication is collateral. CME reported approximately $159.7B of performance bonds and guaranty fund assets. Interest on collateral can lift revenue, but collateral is not corporate cash. The clean owner-cash question begins with exchange and data fees, then separately models collateral interest, clearing obligations, default resources, technology, regulation, and capital returns.

### 2. Closed-loop payments and membership: [American Express](../deep-company-pages/american-express-company.md)

American Express controls more of the payment relationship than a pure card
network. It earns merchant discount revenue, card fees, interest income, and
partner revenue while using spending data to manage rewards, fraud, credit, and
product design. The same integration leaves it responsible for card receivables,
credit losses, funding, merchant acceptance, marketing, and a Membership Rewards
liability of approximately $16.5B at the end of 2025.

American Express reported 2025 billed business of about $1.67T and net income of
$10.8B. The correct owner model is not operating cash flow minus capex. It is
earnings after normalized credit losses, rewards cost, funding expense, required
capital, technology, and customer acquisition. New accounts and premium fees are
useful only if activation, retention, spend, rewards cost, and lifetime credit
returns remain favorable.

### 3. Deposit-funded banking: [JPMorgan Chase](../deep-company-pages/jpmorgan-chase-co.md)

JPMorgan adds the full banking balance sheet. Deposits fund loans and securities,
while payments, markets, investment banking, and asset management diversify the
revenue base. The scale creates a valuable funding and client relationship, but
the bank remains exposed to credit losses, deposit repricing, liquidity stress,
market volatility, regulatory capital, conduct, and technology failure.

JPMorgan reported 2025 common net income of $55.7B, $2.559T of deposits, and
$4.425T of assets. Provision for credit losses was $14.2B, compensation was
$54.5B, and technology expense was $11.0B. These are not secondary adjustments.
They are the cost of operating the balance sheet and preserving the franchise.
The correct model is normalized earnings after credit losses, funding cost,
technology, compensation, legal and compliance expense, required capital growth,
and prudent distributions.

### 4. Fee-paying capital platforms: BlackRock, T. Rowe Price, Ares, Blackstone, and KKR

These companies control access to capital, products, distribution, origination, and institutional trust. Their best economics are recurring management fees after compensation and operating costs. Their most dangerous presentation risk is treating AUM, capital raised, available capital, performance income, marks, or consolidated fund activity as equivalent to durable common-owner cash.

Blackstone’s FY2025 packet reports approximately $921.7B of fee-earning AUM and $5.7B of fee-related earnings. BlackRock’s comparable analysis must begin with its much larger public-market AUM, net flows, product mix, fee rate, technology revenue, and acquisition burden; its dossier does not use Blackstone’s FRE figures as BlackRock figures. Ares emphasizes $1.775B of FRE against much more variable realized/performance income. The important unit is fee-paying capital by product, fee rate, retention, and operating margin—not headline AUM.

T. Rowe Price provides the clean public-markets contrast: parent economics are capital-light, but over 90% of revenue is advisory-fee-linked and the effective fee rate has declined as flows move toward lower-fee products. A business can have excellent cash conversion and still have a deteriorating economic asset if client flows and fee rates compound against it.

Invesco adds a stronger ETF-and-index case. FY2025 ending AUM was about `$2.170T`,
average AUM about `$2.000T`, and net flows about `$104.4B`; QQQ alone represented
about `$407.2B` of AUM. The cleaner question is whether those flows remain broad
and fee-paying after separating market appreciation, fee compression,
distribution cost, and QQQ concentration. Its `$1.8B` Q4 2025 intangible
impairment is non-cash in the period but still evidence that acquisition and
franchise carrying values must be tested against future cash generation.

Franklin adds the product-migration case. FY2025 AUM was about `$1.661T`, but
long-term net outflows were about `$97.4B` and the effective investment-management
fee rate fell to `40.5` basis points from `41.1`. Q1-Q3 FY2026 then produced
approximately `$28.0B`, `$16.9B`, and `$18.4B` of long-term net inflows. The
correct conclusion is conditional: newer ETF, SMA, alternatives, and cash-
management products may be repairing the platform, but flows must be tested by
fee rate, client channel, distribution cost, Western Asset performance, and
cash conversion rather than accepted as a group-level turnaround.

Blackstone and KKR add perpetual capital, insurance, wealth, and monetization businesses. Those can extend fee income, but they introduce insurance spread, statutory capital, partner/NCI claims, marks, realization timing, leverage, and product liquidity. Consolidated OCF is not a sufficient valuation denominator.

Apollo makes the distinction especially clear. Its Q2 2026 call reported $74B
of quarterly origination, $60B of organic inflows, and a $35B financing
associated with Broadcom's AI expansion. Management also reported approximately
$785M of fee-related earnings and $877M of spread-related earnings. The call
said that roughly $50B of signed or announced transactions would affect later
periods because Apollo records results when transactions close.

Those figures identify a valuable origination relationship, but they do not
prove common-owner cash. The $35B financing must be followed for closing,
collateral, credit quality, funding cost, losses, liquidity, co-investor shares,
Athene obligations, and cash received by Apollo. Fee-related earnings can be
valued closer to an asset manager; spread-related earnings require an insurer
and credit-intermediary analysis. This is why Apollo belongs beside Blackstone,
KKR, Ares, and Chubb but should not be assigned a single fee multiple.

### 5. Fee-led risk distribution: Aon and Arthur J. Gallagher

Aon and Gallagher control client relationships, producer talent, renewal workflows, placement data, and risk advice. They generally do not bear the underwriting balance sheet of Chubb. Their competitive protection comes from retention, expertise, client switching cost, and the ability to place increasingly complex risk.

The burden is acquisition capital, producer compensation, integration, debt, working capital, and amortization. Aon’s FY2025 residual after capex and acquisitions was approximately $2.824B, but required normalization for working capital, pension, acquisition effects, and gain items. Gallagher’s FY2025 cash paid for acquisitions was approximately $15.766B against $1.930B of OCF, producing a negative current-year residual. Gallagher can still be a good business; the price is capitalizing future acquired cash earnings, not current-year owner cash.

### 6. Underwriting and float: Chubb

Chubb controls risk selection, pricing, distribution, claims service, and investment of float. Unlike a broker, it bears claims, catastrophe, reserve development, reinsurance recoverability, regulatory capital, and asset-liability matching. Its operating cash cannot be valued like an unrestricted fee stream because policyholders have prior claims.

The correct owner model is normalized underwriting income plus after-tax investment income, less required capital growth and adverse reserve development. Buybacks can create per-share value only if they occur after catastrophe, reserve, and capital requirements are properly funded.

### 7. Institutional custody and administration: [State Street](../deep-company-pages/state-street-corp.md)

State Street controls the operating workflow around institutional assets: custody,
fund accounting, administration, settlement, reporting, foreign exchange,
securities finance, and related technology. The customer's securities are not
State Street's corporate assets. They create a fee relationship and a large
operational obligation.

State Street reported approximately `$11.0B` of FY2025 fee revenue and Q2 2026
AUC/A of approximately `$50.7T` and AUM of approximately `$5.1T`. Those measures
must be separated. AUC/A supports servicing fees; AUM supports management fees;
both can rise because markets rise without new client flows. The correct owner
model is recurring servicing and management fees plus normalized transaction,
FX, securities-finance, and net-interest income, less compensation, technology,
cybersecurity, control, liquidity, and regulatory-capital costs.

State Street is lighter than a lender in direct credit exposure, but it is not
light on trust and operational risk. A settlement, valuation, cyber, or reporting
failure can create client losses, remediation cost, regulatory action, and lost
relationships. The forensic test is whether operating leverage reflects durable
unit-cost improvement or deferred investment in the controls that protect the
franchise.

## Recurring does not mean riskless

The word “recurring” hides several different economic realities:

- Exchange fees recur because users need liquidity and risk transfer, but collateral and default obligations remain.
- Card fees and merchant discount recur while spending, acceptance, credit quality, and reward economics remain favorable; receivables and regulatory capital remain on the balance sheet.
- Asset-management fees recur only while clients retain fee-paying assets at acceptable rates.
- Performance income is episodic and depends on actual investment results and completed realizations.
- Brokerage fees recur with renewals, but acquisitions may be required to sustain growth.
- Insurance premiums recur, but claims and capital requirements are the economic cost of revenue.

The cleanest economics come from a relationship that remains valuable when markets fall, while the company’s capital claims remain limited. A broker or exchange may require little balance-sheet capital, while a private-capital business with insurance or a carrier with float needs a careful balance-sheet model.

## The forensic bridge

For every financial intermediary, test:

`client relationship -> capital / risk / transaction volume -> reported revenue -> required client or regulatory capital -> compensation and partner claims -> common-owner cash`

Specific falsifiers:

- Exchange: collateral interest, default resources, volume concentration, market-data retention, and technology/regulatory spend.
- Card issuer/network: billed business, discount rate, card fees, rewards liability, credit-loss reserves, funding cost, receivables, capital, and customer retention.
- Banks: net interest margin, deposit stability and pricing, loan growth, credit losses, market revenue, liquidity, regulatory capital, technology, conduct, and payout.
- Asset managers: FPAUM, fee rate, net flows, performance-fee share, compensation, partner/NCI claims, realizations, clawbacks, and insurance capital.
- Brokers: organic fee growth, client retention, producer compensation, acquisition cohort cash, earnouts, debt, and amortization.
- Insurers: reserve development, catastrophe losses, combined ratio, reinsurance, investment spread, and capital adequacy.

The common “shenanigan” risk is not usually fabricated revenue. It is category error: treating client assets as company assets, performance revenue as recurring fee income, collateral as excess cash, consolidated fund cash as parent cash, or acquisition-driven EPS as organic economics.

## Lyn Alden lens

Financial intermediaries are liquidity businesses. They benefit from rising nominal asset values, transaction volume, risk complexity, and institutional demand for capital access. They are vulnerable when liquidity contracts, rates rise, credit losses increase, clients redeem, collateral requirements expand, or asset values fall while expenses and regulatory capital remain sticky.

The durable intermediaries are those with network effects, trusted distribution, low marginal capital requirements, and conservative balance sheets. The fragile ones rely on perpetual asset appreciation, refinancing, realizations, or customer risk tolerance.

## Damodaran lens

Value each business component separately:

- Exchanges: recurring transaction and data fees, adjusted for collateral economics and required infrastructure.
- Card networks and issuers: payment volume and fee income after rewards, credit losses, funding, required capital, marketing, and technology.
- Banks: normalized net interest income and noninterest revenue after credit losses, funding, compensation, technology, required capital, and market-cycle normalization.
- Asset managers: FRE based on fee-paying AUM, fee rates, retention, compensation, and operating margins; keep performance income separate until investment results are realized and durable.
- Brokers: organic fee growth and producer economics first; acquisitions as explicit reinvestment with return hurdles.
- Insurers: underwriting and investment spread after required capital, not OCF alone.

The narrative becomes investable only when capital raised becomes fee-paying capital, fee income becomes owner cash, and the company can fund growth without transferring the economic risk to partners, policyholders, debt holders, or future shareholders.

## Cohort conclusion

The cleanest financial economics generally sit where a trusted relationship or network produces recurring fees while clients or policyholders—not common shareholders—provide most of the capital. That advantage disappears if the business must buy growth, guarantee liquidity, absorb credit risk, or return capital before the claims are known.

The key cross-sector lesson is simple: **recurring revenue is a starting category, not a valuation conclusion**. The next filing should show who owns the assets, who bears the losses, who receives the cash, and what capital must remain reserved or unavailable for common shareholders while the relationship continues.
