# Franchise IP, membership, and experiences owner cash

## The first-principles question

Disney's franchise system can be traced as:

`IP and fan relationship -> streaming, advertising, sports, parks, cruises, merchandise -> content and physical reinvestment -> owner cash`

The relationship is valuable when the same creative asset increases retention,
pricing, attendance, merchandise, and licensing without requiring a new full
cost base at each surface. The burden is that content must be renewed and
physical experiences must be maintained, expanded, and staffed.

**Intellectual property (IP)** means characters, stories, brands, sports rights,
and other protected creative assets. **Owner cash** means cash left for common
shareholders after content, rights, parks, ships, technology, labor, debt,
maintenance, and dilution are funded. A franchise is durable only when customer
use and willingness to pay remain high enough to cover the cost of renewing it.

| Business area | Relationship control | Main burden |
|---|---|---|
| Entertainment and streaming | Franchise IP, audience, distribution, and identity | Content, marketing, technology, churn, impairment, and advertising cost |
| Sports | Live audience, league rights, and distribution | Rights inflation, production, affiliate bargaining, and DTC conversion |
| Experiences | Locations, capacity, service systems, and physical franchise | Parks, cruise, labor, safety, maintenance, travel, and expansion capital |

## The owner-cash denominator

Segment operating income is not owner cash. Consolidated owner cash starts with
operating cash flow and subtracts property investment, then separately tests
content spending, corporate costs, debt, noncontrolling interests, rights
commitments, and required maintenance. FY2025 operating cash flow of $18.1B less
$8.0B of property investment is a useful $10.1B screen, not a final answer.

The valuation must distinguish growth capital from maintenance and recognize
that content cash spending is embedded in operating cash flow rather than shown
as property capex. A streaming business may own little physical property while
still requiring heavy spending on content and sports contracts.

## Who controls the relationship and who carries the burden?

| Layer | What the company controls | Main burden |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Creative IP | Characters, stories, brand identity, and rights | Content development, talent, marketing, and impairment risk |
| Streaming and media | Distribution, audience data, advertising, and subscriptions | Technology, content, churn, rights, and customer-acquisition cost |
| Sports | Live rights and distribution access | Rights inflation, production, league bargaining, and schedule risk |
| Parks and cruises | Physical capacity, service, and customer experience | Property, ships, labor, safety, maintenance, weather, and debt |
| Franchisees and licensees | Local execution, merchandise, and distribution | Inventory, retail execution, royalties, and partner health |
| Common shareholder | Residual value after all layers | Failed releases, rights cost, capex, leverage, and dilution |

The same story or character can create several revenue streams, but each stream
has a separate cost and customer behavior. Cross-selling a franchise is valuable
only when the added revenue exceeds the added content, marketing, technology,
service, and physical-capacity costs.

## Earnings quality, accounting tests, and cash conversion

1. Separate subscription revenue, advertising, licensing, sports rights, parks,
   cruises, merchandise, and franchise fees. Their margins and capital needs are
   not interchangeable.
2. Reconcile operating cash flow to property investment, content spending,
   sports rights, technology, maintenance, acquisitions, debt, and dilution.
3. Track subscribers, churn, average revenue, content cost per user, and
   customer-acquisition spending. Subscriber growth without contribution margin
   is incomplete evidence.
4. Track sports-rights commitments and distribution economics. A larger audience
   does not prove that the rights were purchased at a good return.
5. Track attendance and per-capita spending separately from hotel, cruise, labor,
   maintenance, safety, and expansion cost.
6. Treat impairments, restructuring, and acquisition charges as evidence that
   earlier expected cash flows or integration benefits changed. A charge is not
   proof of misconduct, but repeated “one-time” charges are operating evidence.
7. Review loyalty liabilities, deferred revenue, redemption assumptions, and
   breakage. Customer prepayment is a future service obligation, not completed
   profit.
8. Read the proxy for incentives tied to subscribers, adjusted EBITDA, EPS,
   attendance, revenue, cash flow, return on invested capital, and per-share
   value. Growth targets can reward content or capacity expansion before returns
   are known.

## Lyn Alden lens: discretionary demand and capital cycles

Media subscriptions may be relatively resilient, but advertising and premium
content are sensitive to economic liquidity. Parks, cruises, hotels, merchandise,
and travel are more exposed to household income, employment, fuel, rates, and
consumer confidence. Rights and content commitments can remain fixed when demand
weakens.

The balance-sheet test is whether the company can fund content renewal, physical
maintenance, safety, and debt service during a downturn without cutting the
quality of the franchise or issuing equity at a poor price. A large audience does
not remove the need to pay for future content and capacity.

## Damodaran lens: convert franchise value into numbers

The narrative is that a trusted creative asset can be reused across media,
experiences, and products. The valuation must specify retention, churn, price,
attendance, per-capita spending, licensing rates, rights cost, content cost,
marketing, capex, maintenance, debt, taxes, acquisitions, and terminal growth.

Value media, sports, experiences, and licensing separately. Give credit for
shared customer data and brand distribution only when the incremental cash
benefit is visible after the shared cost. The lifecycle question is whether the
franchise is gaining cultural and commercial relevance or merely requiring larger
spending to maintain prior demand.

## Cohort comparison

| Business type | Cleaner economic feature | Main uncertainty |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Franchise IP owner | Reuses creative assets across products and channels | Content renewal, relevance, rights, and impairment |
| Franchise restaurant or hotel system | Fees and royalties with property capital shifted to operators | Franchisee health, brand standards, and guarantees |
| Streaming service | Direct subscriber relationship and usage data | Churn, content cost, pricing, and technology |
| Physical experience owner | Pricing, capacity, and customer spending at owned locations | Labor, maintenance, safety, travel, weather, and debt |
| Licensing company | Royalty revenue with limited production assets | Licensee health, product quality, and consumer relevance |

The cleanest economics usually occur where the company controls customer demand
and collects fees while another party funds physical production. That benefit is
real only if the partner remains healthy and the brand owner funds enough content,
support, and quality control to preserve demand.

## Risks and what to check next

The conclusion should be revised if subscribers grow while contribution margin
falls, sports or content rights consume increasing cash, parks attendance depends
on discounts, franchisees or licensees reduce investment, loyalty liabilities or
guarantees rise faster than related cash, impairments recur, debt grows faster
than owner cash, or buybacks and dividends are funded before maintenance and
content obligations are covered.

The next filing should show subscribers, churn, average revenue, content and
rights cost, attendance, per-capita spending, partner health, loyalty liabilities,
capex, debt, diluted shares, and cash per share.

## Who controls the relationship and carries the burden

Disney controls the IP and the customer interface, but creators, leagues,
distributors, park employees, construction suppliers, regulators, and capital
markets carry essential parts of the system. Shareholders bear residual risk if
content fails, rights inflate, parks require more capital, or customer demand
weakens.

The confirming evidence is rising household value per relationship, stable
retention, profitable streaming, disciplined rights spending, strong park
returns, and owner cash after full reinvestment. The falsifier is cross-business
growth that requires larger content and physical spending without higher cash
per share.
