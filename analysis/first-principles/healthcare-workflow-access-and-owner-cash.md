# Healthcare: who gets paid, who provides the care, and what remains after costs?

## One treatment supports several different businesses

When a patient receives treatment, several companies may earn revenue. A hospital provides the building and clinical staff. A manufacturer supplies equipment or medicines. A distributor delivers supplies. An insurer pays covered claims, and a software company may help the hospital submit those claims and collect payment. Each company has different costs and obligations, even though all serve the same patient need.

That is why rising demand for healthcare does not tell us which business will become more profitable. A hospital may treat more patients but pay much more for nurses. A device manufacturer may sell more instruments but spend heavily on product development or acquisitions. An insurer may collect more premiums while the cost of covered treatment rises faster. Demand and profit need to be examined separately.

To compare these businesses, start with the service and follow the payment. Who chooses the product or treatment? Who agrees to pay, and at what price? Who must provide staff, inventory, buildings or financing before payment arrives? Finally, how much cash remains after the company pays its obligations and maintains the business?

In this essay, **reimbursement** means payment for covered care by an insurer or public program. **Working capital** concerns the money tied up in day-to-day operations: for example, supplies bought before a customer pays, partly offset by amounts still owed to suppliers. Both affect the time between recording revenue and receiving cash.

A company's position can make customers reluctant to change suppliers. A surgeon may need training to use another system; a hospital may need to replace software and retrain staff. These costs can help retain customers, but they do not prove that prices can rise indefinitely or that profits are secure. Product quality, clinical evidence, payment coverage and the cost of supporting customers still matter.

The company cases below examine those differences. Their numbers are historical filing snapshots, not a statement that every business has been assessed over an identical period. Cash after equipment spending is an initial calculation; acquisitions, debt obligations, other owners' claims and required reinvestment can change what shareholders may receive.

## How the different businesses earn money

### 1. Insuring patients and arranging pharmacy services: UnitedHealth and Cigna

UnitedHealth and Cigna control plan administration, provider networks, claims,
pharmacy workflows, and care coordination, but they also carry medical-cost,
policy, trust, and required-capital burdens. UnitedHealth's 2025 operating cash
flow benefited from a $5.824B increase in medical-cost payable. Cigna's cash
flow included $1.868B from pharmacy and service costs payable and $1.247B from
insurance liabilities. These are legitimate insurance timing effects, but they
are not the same as durable underwriting profit.

The right comparison is not revenue or membership alone. It is premium and
service growth after medical and pharmacy cost, claims settlement, capital
requirements, cyber and compliance investment, and client or member retention.
The payer may control the money flow, but it does not control the underlying
health event or all of the cost.

### 2. Running hospitals: HCA Healthcare

HCA owns the physical sites and processes through which care is delivered. Its
2025 network included 190 hospitals, 121 freestanding ambulatory surgery
centers, and 31 freestanding endoscopy centers. It generated $12.636B of
operating cash flow against $4.944B of PP&E capital spending, but the
consolidated cash figure included noncontrolling interests and the business
spent $397M on acquisitions. The post-capex number is therefore a starting
diagnostic, not clean shareholder cash.

HCA is the clearest burden carrier in this cohort. It must staff capacity,
collect negotiated reimbursement, fund facility renewal, absorb uncompensated
care, and manage coverage and Medicaid-payment changes. Same-facility admission
growth can be economically useful only if it converts into payer-adjusted
revenue, margin, and collected cash. The hospital is essential, but essential
demand does not by itself create clean returns.

### 3. Supplying medical equipment and instruments: Intuitive Surgical and Stryker

Intuitive Surgical controls a robotic procedure platform whose value compounds through installed systems, instruments, services, training, and surgeon familiarity. Stryker controls procedure ecosystems through implants, instruments, hospital relationships, and acquisition-built breadth. Both are stronger than commodity medical suppliers because adoption changes the workflow and creates switching friction.

But the installed base creates obligations. Intuitive’s FY2025 operating cash was about $3.031B, while PP&E and other investment uses consumed roughly $554M, leaving about $2.477B before the full burden of inventory, stock compensation, and capital returns. Stryker’s FY2025 operating cash was about $5.044B, but acquisitions consumed approximately $4.960B and capex was meaningful, leaving a negative screen of about $677M after listed growth investments.

The first-principles difference is lifecycle. Intuitive’s instruments and services can create recurring use economics if procedure volume rises. Stryker’s acquisition program may deepen the platform, but it also means adjusted EPS can grow while common-equity owner cash remains acquisition-dependent.

Thermo Fisher extends the installed-workflow idea upstream into research,
laboratory, diagnostics, and biopharma production. Its 2025 operating cash flow
was $7.818B and reported FCF was $6.337B, but acquisitions consumed $4.037B and
debt rose to $39.384B. The platform may have cleaner workflow economics than a
hospital, yet the owner-cash test still depends on whether consumables and
services grow organically and whether major acquisitions earn more than their
funding and integration costs.

Abbott occupies the next layer: installed diagnostics, cardiovascular and
diabetes devices, nutrition, and established pharmaceuticals used across
providers and patients. Its 2025 operating cash flow was $9.566B against
$2.171B of PP&E spending, but the portfolio includes different lifecycles and
the Exact Sciences acquisition changes the organic-growth denominator. Abbott's
breadth can reduce dependence on one product, but it does not remove
reimbursement, quality, evidence, working-capital, or product-renewal burdens.

Johnson & Johnson adds a diversified drug-and-device portfolio. Its 2025
operating cash flow was $24.530B against $4.832B of PP&E spending, but
acquisitions consumed $17.541B and acquired in-process R&D consumed another
$385M. That contrast matters: high cash generation and a long dividend record
can coexist with a large recurring requirement to replace patent-losing drugs
and refresh the MedTech portfolio. Diversification reduces concentration risk;
it does not make replacement capital optional.

### 4. Delivering medicines: McKesson, Cencora, and Cardinal Health

Drug distributors control regulated logistics, availability, purchasing scale, specialty access, cold-chain reliability, and the working relationships that keep products moving. Their social value is high because stockouts and delivery failures are costly. Their accounting and financial profile is harder: revenue is enormous, gross margins are thin, payables and receivables are huge, and acquisitions can reshape the apparent growth rate.

McKesson produced FY2025 operating cash of about $6.085B and a post-PP&E/software/acquisition residual near $5.202B. That is a relatively clean distribution screen, but it remains exposed to working-capital timing, specialty mix, litigation, debt, and portfolio actions.

Cencora generated about $3.875B of operating cash but spent roughly $668M on PP&E and about $4.292B on acquired companies and investments, producing a negative post-growth residual. Cardinal’s comparable post-acquisition residual was also deeply negative. These are not proof that the distribution franchises are worthless; they show that broad customer access can coexist with acquisition-heavy economics and a common shareholder cash deficit.

The key question is who benefits from scale: the distributor, the manufacturer, the payer, or the customer. A distributor can move more product and report higher revenue without gaining proportional gross profit or owner cash.

### 5. Supplying dental and medical practices: Henry Schein

Henry Schein sits inside the daily operating workflow of dental and medical practices through distribution, specialty products, technology, and practice solutions. Its relationship is valuable because switching suppliers, software, and workflow tools creates friction even when individual products are substitutable.

The burden is less visible than in a device factory but still real. FY2025 OCF was about $712M; after PP&E, capitalized software, and acquisitions, the residual was approximately $322M. Restructuring, cyber, KKR-related dilution, technology investment, and working capital can consume the benefit of embedded customer presence. The workflow advantage is credible only if practice retention and mix growth fund the cost of maintaining the people, software, and processes that support the relationship.

### 6. Helping providers collect payment: Waystar

Waystar controls the administrative path between providers, payers, and
patients: eligibility, claims, denials, payment processing, and patient-balance
collection. Its 2025 revenue was `$1.099B`, and operating cash flow was
`$309.7M`. The workflow is embedded because replacing integrations and payment
processes can disrupt collections and reimbursement.

The economics are not uniform. Subscription revenue is the cleaner recurring
layer. Volume-based revenue depends on healthcare activity and payment dollars.
Patient-payment solutions carry much higher third-party costs than provider
solutions. The company also paid `$629.5M` for Iodine in 2025 and carried
`$1.481B` of debt at year-end. Waystar therefore tests whether workflow
control survives transaction mix, acquisition spending, leverage, stock
compensation, and the cost of maintaining healthcare integrations.

## Who pays, who controls, who carries the burden?

| Control point | Value captured | Main burden |
|---|---|---|
| Procedure platform | Surgeon adoption, instruments, services, clinical workflow | R&D, quality, hospital budgets, inventory, acquisition integration |
| Implant/procedure ecosystem | Hospital standardization, surgeon preference, breadth | Acquisitions, amortization, quality, salesforce, procedure utilization |
| Drug distribution | Availability, logistics, specialty access, purchasing scale | Thin margins, receivables/payables, regulatory claims, debt, acquisition cash |
| Practice workflow | Replenishment, software, customer relationship, switching friction | Cyber, field support, restructuring, technology, KKR/dilution, working capital |
| Healthcare payment workflow | Claims, eligibility, denial, payment, and patient-balance coordination | Payer policy, provider liquidity, patient collections, processing cost, regulation, acquisitions, debt |
| Hospital throughput | Admissions, procedures, facility access, local network, service-line development | Labor, payer mix, collection, uncompensated care, physical renewal, policy, debt |
| Payer and pharmacy coordination | Premiums, plan design, networks, claims, pharmacy, care management | Medical trend, claims timing, policy, required capital, trust, cyber, client affordability |

The cleaner economics usually belong to the company that owns a workflow or decision point rather than merely transporting volume. But workflow control does not eliminate capital requirements. It changes their form—from warehouses and receivables to R&D, salesforce, quality systems, software, inventory, and acquisitions.

## The forensic bridge

Every healthcare company should be tested through:

`patient or practice need -> adoption / procurement -> utilization -> billing and reimbursement -> collection -> quality and service burden -> reinvestment -> owner cash`

The weak link differs by cohort:

- Intuitive: procedure growth, instrument utilization, inventory, SBC, and platform reinvestment.
- HCA: admissions, payer-adjusted collections, labor, supplemental payments, maintenance capex, NCI, debt, and buybacks.
- UnitedHealth/Cigna: medical-cost trend, claims and pharmacy payable timing, retained margin, policy, required capital, trust, and distributions.
- Stryker: acquisition cohorts, amortization, quality, and whether OCF survives M&A.
- McKesson: payable timing, specialty margin, litigation, and working-capital normalization.
- Cencora/Cardinal: acquisition-funded growth, GLP-1 or specialty mix, debt, NCI, and common cash.
- Henry Schein: practice retention, technology economics, restructuring, cyber, and KKR claims.
- Waystar: subscription versus volume mix, provider versus patient contribution margin, receivables and deferred revenue, acquisition returns, debt, SBC, and diluted cash per share.

The possible “shenanigan” is usually not fabricated revenue. It is a presentation that makes volume, adjusted EPS, or operating cash look more durable than the capital and liability structure supporting it. Watch for payables-driven OCF, acquisition add-backs, purchase-accounting amortization exclusions, stock compensation, special-item recurrence, and quality or litigation costs that keep returning.

## Lyn Alden lens

Healthcare has a durable-demand floor because illness, aging, diagnosis, surgery, and medication access do not disappear in a normal recession. But liquidity still matters: hospitals defer capital equipment, patients lose coverage, practices delay technology purchases, distributors carry more inventory, and governments or payers pressure reimbursement.

The most resilient control points are those tied to non-discretionary treatment, regulated access, or workflow switching costs. The most fragile economics are those dependent on elective utilization, hospital budgets, debt-funded acquisition, or reimbursement that does not cover labor and capital.

## Damodaran lens

Value healthcare companies by lifecycle and sustainable owner cash:

- Payers: separate premium and service growth from medical and pharmacy cost, payable timing, reserve development, required capital, and shareholder cash.
- Hospitals: separate volume from payer-adjusted collections, maintenance from growth capex, and consolidated cash from shareholder cash.
- Procedure platforms: separate installed-base recurring use from system-placement growth and R&D.
- Medtech consolidators: model acquisition cohorts, not just adjusted EPS.
- Distributors: normalize working capital, gross-profit dollars, litigation, debt, and acquisition cash.
- Practice workflow platforms: split recurring customer relationship value from technology and restructuring investment.

The narrative only earns a premium if the company can reinvest in quality, evidence, workflow, and capacity at returns above its cost of capital. A high gross margin or strong market share is not enough if every growth dollar requires another acquisition or if the common shareholder never receives cash after reinvestment.

## Cohort conclusion

Healthcare’s strongest positions are often the least visible: the instrument set used in a procedure, the surgeon’s routine, specialty access, and the practice processes that make care run. Payers control money and coordination but carry medical-cost, policy, trust, and capital burdens. Hospitals are essential but carry the heaviest labor, reimbursement, facility, and public-policy burden. Distribution is socially essential but financially thin; medtech can be economically rich but acquisition- and quality-intensive; practice workflow is sticky but exposed to technology and customer-budget cycles.

The central cross-sector lesson is that “mission critical” does not identify who earns the cleanest return. The right question is who controls the decision, who pays to keep the workflow reliable, and whether the resulting cash survives labor, reimbursement, reinvestment, working capital, quality, regulation, and capital returns.

## Risks, accounting tests, and what to check next

The healthcare thesis weakens when patient or practice volume rises without payer-adjusted collection, when payable timing or reserve releases carry operating cash, when acquisitions and amortization exclusions hide weak shareholder cash, when quality or litigation costs recur, or when required capital and labor consume the apparent margin.

At each filing, compare utilization with reimbursement and collection; operating cash with payable and receivable movements; acquisition growth with organic growth and integration cost; adjusted earnings with stock compensation and dilution; and procedure, patient, or practice growth with the capital and service burden required to preserve the workflow. The next evidence should show which layer is retaining cash after the full healthcare obligation is funded.
