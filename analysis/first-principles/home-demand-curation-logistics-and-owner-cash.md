# Home furnishings: what remains after the product is delivered?

Date baseline: `2026-09-14`

## The sale is only part of the transaction

Home-furnishing companies are often grouped together because they sell products
used inside or around the home. That grouping is not enough for investment
analysis. RH, Williams-Sonoma, and Wayfair serve different customer needs,
control different parts of the purchase process, and carry different capital
and operating burdens.

A customer may like a product and place an order, but the company still has to obtain it, deliver it in good condition and handle any return. Advertising, staff, storage and delivery can consume much of what appeared to be an attractive margin. Large items can also tie up space and cash while they wait for a buyer.

That makes the central question practical: after completing the sale and maintaining the business, how much money remains for shareholders? Start with how the company attracts customers, then follow the product, the payment and the costs through delivery and support.

RH emphasizes its design choices, galleries and customer experience. Williams-Sonoma serves several home-related needs through different brands. Wayfair emphasizes selection and the ability to find and deliver products from many suppliers. These approaches can attract different purchases and require different spending. The linked company studies supply the historical evidence; their figures are not a synchronized set of current results.

## Plain-language definitions

- **Demand** is a customer signal such as an order placed or a comparable-sales
  change. It is not automatically recognized revenue or cash.
- **Gross profit** is revenue minus the costs included in the company's cost-of-sales definition. Check that definition: delivery, storage and related costs may be classified differently between companies. Gross profit still has to cover expenses excluded from that calculation and is not cash available to shareholders.
- **Contribution** is the amount left after the costs needed to obtain and serve
  an order. The exact definition differs by company and must be stated.
- **Owner cash** is the cash available to continuing common owners after the
  operating system is maintained, required working capital is funded, recurring
  stock compensation is charged, and necessary capital investment is paid.
- **Curation** means reducing or organizing choice through a brand, design
  point of view, assortment, or service.
- **Logistics control** means making fragmented product available, deliverable,
  returnable, and understandable to the customer.

## The three operating models

| Model | Customer problem solved | Main control point | Main burden |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| RH | Wants design authority, high-end home expression, and a physical or digital destination | Brand, taste, galleries, design services, hospitality, and product presentation | Inventory, freight, tariffs, galleries, labor, delivery, leases, housing sensitivity, and destination capital |
| Williams-Sonoma | Wants cooking, hosting, decorating, gifting, and practical design across repeated occasions | Multi-brand assortment, content, loyalty, e-commerce, B2B, and design relationship | Inventory, merchandising, tariffs, fulfillment, advertising, stores, labor, and returns |
| Wayfair | Wants broad selection, price comparison, supplier access, and workable delivery of large products | Search, supplier intermediation, customer data, marketplace, fulfillment, and delivery coordination | Advertising, payment, delivery, fulfillment, returns, customer support, leases, supplier quality, technology, and SBC |

The models overlap, but a single revenue multiple cannot explain their differences. A gallery requires rent, staff and physical investment. A broad online selection requires usable product information, customer acquisition and delivery coordination. Compare the earnings and cash after each company's actual costs rather than assuming the same proportion of every sales dollar remains available.

## What a home purchase requires

A home purchase is economically complete only when all of these steps work:

1. The customer identifies a need, project, occasion, or design goal.
2. The company presents products the customer can understand and trust.
3. The customer can compare price, quality, delivery, and return conditions.
4. The supplier has product available with acceptable quality and lead time.
5. The company collects payment and records the sale at the correct time.
6. The product is delivered without damage, excessive delay, or an expensive
   return.
7. The company retains enough contribution after service and acquisition cost.
8. The customer returns for another project or occasion at acceptable cost.
9. The company maintains the physical, digital, and supplier system with
   enough reinvestment to preserve that contribution.

Failure at any step can make reported sales a poor measure of owner economics.

## Product selection and delivery solve different customer problems

RH controls taste and destination. The company can influence what customers
consider desirable, how products are displayed, and whether the gallery or
hospitality environment increases project engagement. This can support pricing
and customer trust. It also requires physical presentation, inventory, labor,
and occupancy.

Williams-Sonoma controls a broader set of home occasions. Cooking, entertaining,
decorating, gifting, and design can create more frequent demand than one large
furnishing project. The company still owns inventory and fulfillment exposure,
but a multi-brand and multi-occasion system may reduce dependence on a single
high-ticket purchase.

Wayfair controls choice and execution. More than `22,000` suppliers and more
than `21M` active customers create a large matching problem: product must be
found, described, priced, and delivered in a way that makes the customer trust
the purchase. Wayfair can avoid owning all supplier inventory, but it does not
avoid the cost of delivery, returns, support, technology, or fulfillment.

The control point is valuable only if it improves the customer's completed
purchase and leaves contribution after the associated burden.

## Read the company figures in the context of their costs

### RH

`orders placed -> recognized revenue -> product gross profit -> freight,
tariffs, markdowns, delivery -> galleries, labor, service, hospitality,
leases, and interest -> maintenance and growth capital -> owner cash`

RH's demand measure can lead revenue. Q3 FY25 revenue was `$883.8M` with
operating income of `$105.9M`; Q1 FY26 revenue was `$800.3M` with operating
income of `$34.2M` and a net loss of about `($13.7M)`. The change demonstrates
why order formation, high-ticket timing, housing activity, and fixed costs must
be analyzed together.

### Williams-Sonoma

`customer occasions and traffic -> comparable brand revenue -> product gross
profit -> inventory, tariffs, fulfillment, advertising, stores, labor, returns
-> maintenance capital and diluted owner cash`

Comparable revenue and operating margin are useful, but inventory and tariff
cost can change future margin and cash. Fiscal 2025 comparable brand revenue
grew `3.5%` and operating margin was `18.1%`; Q1 FY26 comparable revenue grew
`4.8%`, operating margin was `16.2%`, and inventory rose `9.0%` to `$1.46B`,
including about `$60M` of incremental tariff costs.

### Wayfair

Begin with customers, order frequency and average spending per order. Then establish which costs are already included in the company's profit measure. Deduct delivery, returns, advertising and other expenses only to the extent they have not already been included. A measure called “contribution” is not comparable across companies unless its definition is the same.

Wayfair's FY2025 revenue was `$12.5B`, up `5.1%`. Q2 2026 revenue was `$3.5B`,
gross profit was `$1.054B` or `30.0%` of revenue, adjusted EBITDA was `$242M`,
active customers were `21.7M`, and orders delivered were `10.6M`. These facts
show operating improvement, but gross profit and adjusted EBITDA remain before
several costs that determine owner cash.

## What the customer and order counts leave unanswered

| Activity measure | What it tells us | What remains unanswered |
| --- | --- | --- |
| RH demand/orders placed | Future customer intent | Cancellation, recognition timing, gross margin, inventory, and cash collection |
| Williams-Sonoma comparable revenue | Existing-brand sales change | Traffic, price, mix, inventory aging, tariff burden, and repeat contribution |
| Wayfair active customers | Reach and possible demand base | Retention, orders per customer, acquisition cost, delivery cost, returns, and contribution |
| Wayfair orders delivered | Completed transaction volume | Profit per order, damage, support, returns, and supplier economics |
| Room or gallery count | Physical network reach | Productivity, occupancy, capital return, and maintenance burden |

The discipline is to pair every activity measure with its cost and cash
bridge. A larger network can improve customer access while reducing return on
capital if the required support system grows faster than contribution.

## Working capital and inventory tests

Inventory is not automatically a problem. It can improve selection and
availability. The questions are:

- Is inventory growing with credible demand or ahead of it?
- Are product costs, tariffs, and freight included in the margin outlook?
- How much inventory is owned, committed, or controlled by suppliers?
- Are cancellations, damage, and returns rising?
- Does inventory turn quickly enough to avoid markdowns and cash lock-up?
- Are payment terms providing temporary cash that reverses later?

RH's high-ticket inventory can create large margin and cash swings. Williams-
Sonoma's inventory supports a broad occasion assortment but exposes the
company to tariff and timing effects. Wayfair's marketplace structure can limit
owned inventory while its fulfillment systems still create fixed and service
claims.

## People and delivery systems still need to be paid

The customer sees a product, but the company must fund people and systems that
make the product usable. Those include designers, merchandisers, warehouse and
delivery workers, customer service, technology teams, store employees, and
supplier-support personnel.

RH retains more direct gallery, service, hospitality, and delivery burden.
Williams-Sonoma retains retail, fulfillment, merchandising, and customer
relationship burden. Wayfair shifts product ownership toward suppliers but
retains digital acquisition, customer support, delivery coordination, returns,
and platform trust.

The burden may be transferred, but it is not removed. If suppliers, carriers,
or property operators become financially weak, the platform may need to spend
more on support, incentives, quality control, or guarantees.

## Lyn Alden lens: housing, rates, and liquidity

Home purchases vary in urgency. Routine cooking and gifting can continue while
large furniture and design projects are postponed. RH is most exposed to
affluent project activity, housing turnover, wealth effects, and financing
conditions. Williams-Sonoma has a wider range of occasions. Wayfair can gain
share in a weak category, but customer affordability, supplier working capital,
freight, tariffs, and delivery utilization still matter.

Higher rates increase the required return on inventory, galleries, technology,
and delivery capacity. A liquidity contraction can reduce order frequency,
delay projects, increase promotional pressure, and weaken suppliers. A company
with low physical ownership can still be exposed if its service promise
requires a costly logistics network.

## Damodaran lens: lifecycle and reinvestment

The lifecycle question differs by company:

- RH is investing in brand expansion, galleries, hospitality, and destination
  experiences while its core demand is cyclical. Growth capital must be tested
  against mature-location cash returns.
- Williams-Sonoma is a scaled multi-brand retailer. Its reinvestment test is
  whether design, loyalty, digital, B2B, and fulfillment improve repeat
  contribution without excessive inventory or support cost.
- Wayfair is a recovery and share-capture case. Its reinvestment test is
  whether supplier access, logistics density, and technology reduce the total
  cost of completing an order and raise cash per customer.

The valuation denominator must match the lifecycle. Use normalized owner cash
after maintenance capital, working capital, recurring stock compensation,
leases, and service burden. Do not capitalize a peak demand period, a gross
booking measure, or an adjusted EBITDA number without identifying the excluded
claims.

## Cross-company forensic questions

1. Is the company growing customers or only increasing price and average order
   value?
2. Is gross margin improving because of durable operating improvement or
   because freight, tariffs, returns, and support costs have not yet appeared?
3. Are adjusted measures excluding costs that recur as the company operates?
4. Does inventory growth match sell-through and future demand?
5. Does logistics investment create higher contribution or only higher service
   volume?
6. Are suppliers, franchisees, carriers, or property partners healthy enough to
   keep funding their part of the system?
7. Does growth require more advertising or discounting for each incremental
   customer?
8. Does diluted cash per share improve after stock compensation and buybacks?
9. Are leases, commitments, debt, and guarantees included in the valuation?
10. What next filing could disprove the current interpretation?

## Current conclusions

RH has the strongest design and luxury identity control but the highest
high-ticket and fixed-presentation sensitivity. Williams-Sonoma has the
widest repeat-occasion exposure and a stronger recurring-home mix, but still
owns inventory and fulfillment claims. Wayfair has the broadest selection and
logistics-mediated reach, but must prove that category share gains convert into
contribution and diluted owner cash.

Inventory ownership alone does not determine the better business. Compare the revenue earned on a completed purchase with all the costs of obtaining and serving it, then include the investment needed to keep doing that work. When using a contribution measure that already deducts some of those costs, do not deduct them twice. Repeat purchases help only if retaining the customer is profitable after discounts, marketing and service.

## Skeptical Reader Test

- Are demand, revenue, gross profit, contribution, and owner cash kept separate?
- Can the reader identify the control point and the burden carrier for each
  model?
- Does each valuation statement charge maintenance, working capital, leases,
  stock compensation, and required growth investment?
- Are missing raw artifacts and missing current valuation inputs disclosed?
- Could the ten cross-company questions produce a different conclusion after
  the next filing?
