# Hotels: how the brand and the building owner earn different profits

## Business economics: a booking pays for more than a room

A hotel stay requires several functions to work at the same time:

1. a guest must choose the property;
2. a reservation and payment system must complete the booking;
3. the hotel must provide a room and related services;
4. employees must operate and maintain the property; and
5. the building must be renewed often enough to preserve its quality.

Different companies control these functions. A brand company can control
customer choice, reservations, loyalty, pricing tools, and the relationship
with the property owner while owning few hotels. A property owner can control
the location, rooms, renovation schedule, and residual real-estate value while
depending on another company for brand and distribution.

The distinction changes what a hotel investment means. A brand can receive fees from many hotels without buying their buildings. A property owner receives the income left from operating a particular hotel, but must also pay to maintain and renovate it. Both depend on guests, yet a rise in bookings can affect their profits differently.

To compare them, follow the guest's payment through the business. Which company receives a fee? Which pays the staff and owns the equipment? Who pays when rooms need renovation? How much remains after those costs and borrowing obligations? The following comparison uses Marriott for the brand business and Host Hotels & Resorts for property ownership, with historical figures from their company studies.

## What the room statistics tell us

**Room demand** is the number of nights guests want to buy. **Occupancy** is
the portion of available rooms sold. **ADR**, or average daily rate, is the
average room price. **RevPAR**, revenue per available room, combines occupancy
and room price. RevPAR is an operating measure; it is not cash available to
shareholders. For example, occupancy can fall while the average room price rises. Revenue per available room combines the two effects, but still says nothing about how much wages, insurance or renovations cost. Always examine those costs separately.

**Fee income** arises when a brand, manager, reservation system, or
loyalty program receives fees from hotel owners and guests without funding most
of the hotel buildings. These fees may be attractive, but the platform still
has technology, marketing, loyalty, guarantee, contract-acquisition, and debt
obligations.

**Property income** arises when the company owns the hotel asset and
receives the property's residual cash after wages, supplies, insurance,
management fees, taxes, interest, and renewal spending. The property owner also
retains exposure to location value, hotel condition, refinancing, and asset-sale
prices.

**Renewal capex** is spending needed to preserve the existing hotel's earning
capacity. It includes major room renovations, systems, furnishings, and other
replacement work. It is not optional growth spending merely because the cash
leaves the company irregularly.

## Compare the sources of income

The brand's fees depend on a functioning hotel business. The hotel's earnings may depend partly on the guests the brand helps attract. Neither company's results can fully explain the other's returns.

### Marriott: earning fees from the hotel network

Marriott helps guests find and book hotels through its brands, reservation services and loyalty program. It earns franchise and management fees, along with income from arrangements such as branded credit cards. The relevant questions include how fees are calculated, what services Marriott must provide in return, and whether hotel owners earn enough to keep participating.

Marriott's FY2025 evidence fits this chain: 9,805 properties, 1,779,936 rooms,
about 610,000 pipeline rooms, and loyalty-member booking shares of about 75% of
U.S. room nights and 68% of global room nights. These facts show relationship
control and future fee capacity. They do not show that every pipeline room will
open, that every owner earns an adequate return, or that loyalty points will
cost less than the cash received to fund them.

### Host: paying to operate and maintain the properties

Host's properties need to attract guests at prices that cover operating costs and justify the investment in the buildings. A good location can support demand, but the rooms must remain competitive. Renovation can require both a cash payment and a temporary reduction in rooms available to sell.

Host's FY2025 evidence fits this chain: comparable RevPAR rose 3.8%, comparable
Total RevPAR rose 4.2%, operating cash flow was $1.510B, and capital spending
was $644M. The post-capex screen was approximately $866M before debt service,
dividends, repurchases, and other claims. The property chain therefore has
real cash, but it needs much more capital than a fee platform to preserve the
asset that creates the cash.

## Marriott still has costs and obligations

Marriott owns less than one percent of the hotels in its system, which shifts
most building, labor, and renovation cost to owners. That is a valuable form of
capital efficiency. It is not a removal of risk.

The platform must still protect the relationship through:

- technology and reservation infrastructure;
- loyalty rewards and redemption capacity;
- brand standards and owner support;
- marketing and customer service;
- franchise guarantees and potential support payments;
- new-contract and acquisition spending; and
- debt and shareholder distributions.

The guest-loyalty liability was $7.992B at Marriott's 2025 year-end. The
liability requires estimates of breakage, redemption behavior, funding, and
the cost of benefits. A change in those estimates can change reported revenue
and the liability without changing the number of hotel rooms. That makes the
loyalty program both a relationship asset and an accounting judgment area.

The correct conclusion is that Marriott transfers much of the physical burden
but retains a contingent obligation to keep the system credible and useful.

## A desirable property still needs reinvestment

Host owns scarce luxury and upper-upscale hotel locations. Scarcity can support
pricing, group demand, and property value. But the property is valuable only if
it remains suitable for the demand it targets.

The owner must fund:

- employees and benefits;
- utilities, food, and other operating supplies;
- insurance and property protection;
- periodic renovation and replacement;
- interest and debt maturities;
- property taxes and other obligations; and
- the cost of carrying rooms during renovation downtime.

Host reported $148M of property-sale gains and $17M of condominium-sale income
in 2025. These transactions may create value, but they are not recurring hotel
operating earnings. A property-owner valuation must separate operating cash,
asset-sale proceeds, and changes in estimated net asset value.

## The accounting questions are different

| Layer | Useful reported measure | Main risk of overstatement | Required correction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Brand platform | Adjusted EBITDA, fee revenue, rooms, RevPAR, pipeline | Pipeline and fee growth may hide loyalty cost, guarantees, technology, or owner distress | Reconcile fee cash after loyalty, support, acquisition, debt, and dilution claims |
| Property owner | Adjusted FFO, EBITDAre, RevPAR, NOI | Depreciation add-backs and asset-sale gains may hide renewal capex and financing burden | Subtract long-run renewal capex, interest, debt claims, and REIT/OP attribution |
| Both | Occupancy, ADR, RevPAR, room growth | Higher prices can coexist with lower occupancy, owner returns, or labor margins | Track total hotel margin, owner economics, cash collection, and capital per room |

An adjustment is not automatically a shenanigan. It becomes a financial-
engineering concern when the same cost is repeatedly removed, when cash does
not support the preferred measure, or when the adjustment shifts a burden to a
party whose weakening economics threaten the platform.

## The three analytical lenses

### Alden: liquidity and durability

The hotel system is sensitive to household income, corporate travel budgets,
interest rates, property financing, labor supply, insurance prices, currencies,
and geopolitics.

For Marriott, the key liquidity question is whether hotel owners can finance
new rooms and required renovations. A platform can report fee growth while
future supply slows or guarantees rise. For Host, the key liquidity question is
whether hotel cash flow and unencumbered asset value cover renewal capital and
debt maturities through a weak travel period.

The stress test should combine lower RevPAR with owner financing stress, wage
inflation, insurance inflation, renovation needs, and higher refinancing costs.
The company with the cleanest operating model is not necessarily the company
with the lowest exposure to a credit contraction.

### Damodaran: narrative, reinvestment, and lifecycle

Marriott's narrative requires explicit assumptions for:

- net room growth and pipeline conversion;
- fee rates by brand and geography;
- RevPAR and loyalty-member behavior;
- co-brand and payment economics;
- guarantee losses and owner support;
- technology and contract-acquisition spending; and
- share count after repurchases and equity compensation.

Host's narrative requires explicit assumptions for:

- normalized property NOI;
- occupancy, ADR, and total RevPAR;
- hotel labor, insurance, and operating margins;
- renewal capex per room over a full renovation cycle;
- interest and refinancing costs;
- acquisition and disposition returns; and
- NAV after debt, OP units, and known obligations.

Marriott is a mature relationship platform with continuing reinvestment in
loyalty, payments, brands, and room distribution. Host is a mature property
portfolio in a renewal and asset-recycling lifecycle. Their growth rates should
not be assigned the same reinvestment assumptions.

### Forensic framework: cash and incentives

The central forensic test is:

`reported operating measure -> cash collected -> recurring burden -> financing and ownership claims -> common-owner cash`

For Marriott, compare fee-related cash with loyalty-liability growth, redemption
cost, guarantees, technology spending, acquisition spending, debt, and
buybacks. For Host, compare FFO and EBITDAre with renewal capex, cash interest,
asset-sale gains, dividends, repurchases, and OP-unit claims.

Management incentives can favor room pipeline, adjusted EBITDA, adjusted FFO,
RevPAR, distributable earnings, or per-share results. These measures are useful
when reconciled. They become incomplete when they omit the capital needed to
preserve the underlying relationship or property.

## Who captures value and who carries the burden?

| Economic function | Primary beneficiary | Primary burden carrier |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Brand and customer choice | Marriott and comparable brand platforms | Brand company through loyalty, technology, guarantees, and reputation |
| Hotel construction and room renewal | Brand platform receives future fee capacity | Franchisees and property owners |
| Daily hotel labor and service | Guests, brands, and property owners share the output | Property owners and hotel operators |
| Location scarcity and residual asset value | Host and its equity holders | Host equity and lenders through capital and refinancing exposure |
| Loyalty and co-brand relationship | Marriott and card partners | Marriott, hotel owners, and ultimately guests through funding and redemption economics |
| Hotel demand shock | Strong brands may retain share | Owners, employees, lenders, and the platform's guarantee exposure |

The economic system is therefore a negotiated distribution of cash and risk.
The brand platform often captures a higher-margin recurring fee. The property
owner may capture more residual upside if location and NOI appreciate, but it
also absorbs more losses and capital requirements.

## Valuation discipline

Marriott should be valued with a fee-platform model. The recurring base should
be separated from performance, asset-sale, and unusual guarantee effects. The
model should subtract loyalty, technology, support, acquisition, tax, debt,
and dilution claims before assigning a multiple.

Host should be valued using both property cash after renewal capex and NAV. A
single FFO multiple can hide whether the current capex year is unusually low,
whether sale proceeds support distributions, and whether debt or OP units
reduce the common claim.

The valuation question is not “which company has the higher EBITDA?” It is:

1. how much recurring cash is produced;
2. how much capital is required to keep producing it;
3. which stakeholders have priority claims on that cash; and
4. what growth and margin assumptions the current price already requires.

## What future filings can confirm or falsify

The next Marriott filing should show net rooms opened, pipeline conversion,
fee rates, RevPAR, loyalty liability, breakage, redemption cost, co-brand cash,
guarantees, technology capital, debt, buybacks, and diluted shares. The thesis
strengthens if fee-paying rooms and cash grow faster than loyalty and support
costs. It weakens if pipeline rises without owner openings or if guarantees and
loyalty obligations consume the fee advantage.

The next Host filing should show RevPAR by market, occupancy, ADR, hotel EBITDA
margin, renewal capex per room, renovation downtime, insurance, debt maturities,
asset sales, FFO after capex, distributions, repurchases, and OP-unit dilution.
The thesis strengthens if property cash covers renewal and debt while renovated
assets sustain NOI. It weakens if RevPAR rises but margins and post-renewal cash
fall, or if asset sales and refinancing fund ordinary distributions.

## Final principle

The best hotel economics do not come automatically from owning the guest,
owning the brand, or owning the building. They come from controlling a scarce
part of the lodging system while charging that control for less than the cost of
maintaining it.

Marriott controls the relationship and shifts most property capital outward.
Host controls scarce physical capacity and retains the property's residual
value. The proper investment conclusion remains conditional on the cash bridge
for each model.
