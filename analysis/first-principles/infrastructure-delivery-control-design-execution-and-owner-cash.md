# Infrastructure delivery: who controls the project, who carries the burden, and who keeps the cash

## The first principle

Infrastructure demand becomes economic output through a sequence of decisions
and physical work:

`funding authorization -> planning and design -> permit and procurement -> site, utility, and material execution -> project acceptance -> billing -> collection -> operation and follow-on work`

No company controls the whole sequence. Different companies control different
points and carry different risks. A backlog number is therefore incomplete
until the analysis identifies:

- who owns the customer relationship;
- who controls the technical standard or project workflow;
- who supplies the labor, equipment, materials, and financing;
- who bears estimate, claim, warranty, and collection risk; and
- how much cash remains after the next project can still be delivered.

This cohort covers six distinct roles:

| Role | Company cases | What is controlled | Main burden |
|---|---|---|---|
| Design and advisory authority | AECOM | planning, permitting, technical standards, owner-side program management | professional liability, claims, DSO, contract assets, international collections |
| Advanced-facility and program coordination | Jacobs | data-center, semiconductor, water, transport, and life-sciences delivery plus PA advisory | pass-through content, receivables, project estimates, acquisition debt, employee claims |
| Government mission and process execution | KBR | federal readiness, engineering, logistics, process technology, and licensing | contract mix, joint ventures, pension, debt, separation cost, project execution |
| Civil execution and materials integration | Granite | roads, bridges, public works, site development, aggregates, and asphalt | equipment, materials plants, contract estimates, weather, acquisitions, debt |
| Mission-critical site and electrical execution | Sterling | site development, utilities, electrical and mechanical integration | labor, project estimates, acquisition integration, equipment, concentrated capex |
| Utility and infrastructure field execution | Quanta | utility, communications, energy, and field-service relationships | labor, equipment, safety, acquisitions, working capital, project completion |
| Telecom and critical-facility field execution | Dycom | fiber, wireless, outside-plant construction, inside-facility cabling, electrical, and security systems | customer concentration, MSA volume estimates, crews, equipment, receivables, acquisition debt |

## The customer relationship changes the economics

### AECOM: the owner-side and design relationship

AECOM often reaches the customer before the physical contractor. Its value is
the ability to define the project, obtain approvals, manage programs, and
coordinate specialists. It can influence what gets built and how the work is
specified, but it does not own the project asset and does not control the
customer's ultimate capital budget.

The cleaner part of its economics is fee-bearing technical work. The burden is
that gross revenue can include pass-through activity, while contract assets,
claims, DSO, and international collections can delay cash. FY2025 operating
cash was `$821.6M`, but a `$334.4M` source from receivables and contract assets
followed a `$500.8M` use in FY2024. That is why adjusted net service revenue
and cash conversion matter more than gross revenue alone.

### Jacobs: broader complexity management with acquisition financing

Jacobs also controls design and program-management relationships, but it is
more exposed to advanced facilities, water, transportation, and customer
capital programs. PA Consulting increases advisory and public-sector reach.

The benefit is broader participation in modern infrastructure spending. The
burden is a larger acquisition and financing claim. Jacobs paid about
`£997.6M` cash for remaining PA shares, issued shares, and incurred additional
employee-related consideration. Receivables and contract assets used `$595M`
of nine-month operating cash in Q3 FY2026. A high backlog does not remove the
need to test whether PA and the project portfolio produce cash after debt and
dilution.

### KBR: two unlike engines in one balance sheet

KBR combines government mission support with sustainable technology and
industrial process work. It controls program delivery, readiness, engineering,
and licensing relationships, but the planned MTS spin-off shows that the two
engines have different customers, contract structures, and valuation inputs.

KBR's FY2025 continuing-operations operating cash was `$557M`, and its
recurring-capital residual was about `$515M`. The next question is how much cash
belongs to each future standalone entity after pension, joint-venture cash,
debt, corporate overhead, and separation cost. A spin-off can expose economics;
it does not automatically improve them.

### Granite: materials control improves execution but increases capital

Granite sits closer to the physical project. Its materials operations can
support construction schedules, pricing, and local supply. This can create
better control than buying all materials at market prices, but it adds plant,
quarry, equipment, environmental, and maintenance capital.

Granite's CAP reached `$7.0B` in FY2025, while operating cash was `$468.9M`
and recurring-capital residual was about `$330.6M`. Acquisitions consumed
`$777.5M`. The comparison shows why CAP is not an owner-cash measure: the
company must still fund equipment, contract assets, project execution, and
acquired capability.

### Sterling: concentrated control at the mission-critical site

Sterling combines site development with electrical and mechanical capability.
It can capture more of a data-center or semiconductor campus when it supplies
both site and electrical work. That may improve coordination and margin, but it
also concentrates the company around a specific customer-capital cycle.

Sterling's signed backlog reached `$4.33B` in Q2 FY2026 and combined backlog
`$5.62B`, with mission-critical projects representing `92%` of E-Infrastructure
backlog. The evidence is strong for demand. The burden remains acquisition
integration, labor, working capital, equipment, and the distinction between
signed work and future-phase opportunities.

### Quanta: field capacity and repeat utility relationships

Quanta is closer to recurring utility and communications execution than to
owner-side design. Its relationship can be durable because utilities and
network operators repeatedly need qualified field capacity, safety systems,
and project management.

The burden is correspondingly physical: labor availability, safety, fleets,
equipment, weather, project estimates, acquisitions, and maintenance capital.
Quanta can show high-quality backlog when field capacity and cash conversion
remain strong, but its larger project and acquisition program still must be
charged against owner cash.

### Dycom: digital-infrastructure field execution

Dycom converts communications and critical-facility demand through crews,
equipment, outside-plant construction, and inside-facility systems. Its work is
closer to physical delivery than to design authority, but much of its
Communications backlog is estimated from master service agreements and customer
information rather than fixed volumes. Building Systems backlog is more closely
tied to firm orders.

FY2026 revenue was `$5.546B`, backlog was `$9.542B`, operating cash was
`$642.5M`, and capital spending was `$240.8M`. The residual was strong, but the
Power Solutions acquisition used `$1.629B` of cash and increased the financing
claim. In Q1 FY2027, backlog reached `$11.906B`, while receivables and contract
assets consumed `$364.4M` of cash and operating cash flow was negative `$24.6M`.
The important test is whether backlog, customer concentration, equipment, debt,
and working capital convert into cash per diluted share over a full cycle.

## Backlog is a hierarchy, not one metric

The cohort should rank visibility measures by how much economic uncertainty
remains:

`collected cash > billed and collectible work > executed contract > funded award > signed backlog > assigned work > options > future-phase opportunity > pipeline`

These categories should not receive the same valuation multiple.

- AECOM's book-to-burn and backlog are useful, but the cash test is DSO,
  contract assets, claims, and adjusted NSR conversion.
- Jacobs' backlog and RPO are useful, but gross revenue can include pass-through
  work and PA financing must be recovered.
- KBR's backlog and options must be separated by MTS and STS and by contract
  type.
- Granite's CAP includes assigned and awarded projects at different stages of
  certainty.
- Sterling's signed backlog is stronger than combined backlog and future phases.
- Quanta's RPO and backlog require labor, equipment, and project completion.
- Dycom's Communications backlog includes master-service-agreement volume
  estimates, while Building Systems backlog is more closely tied to firm orders.

The correct question is not whether backlog is real. It is what must happen
before the company can keep the cash.

## Accounting and earnings-quality tests

### Percentage-of-completion revenue

Construction and long-term program businesses often recognize revenue as work
progresses. The accounting is appropriate when estimates are accurate, but it
allows current profit to depend on expected total cost and expected total
revenue.

For each company, track:

- original estimated margin versus final margin;
- favorable and unfavorable estimate changes;
- approved versus unapproved change orders;
- contract-loss provisions and claims;
- contract assets, retainage, and billing status; and
- whether cash collection follows recognized revenue.

### Gross revenue versus economic revenue

AECOM and Jacobs are the clearest cases. Gross revenue can include
reimbursable or pass-through costs. Adjusted net service revenue may better
represent the fee-bearing work, but it is still not cash. A valuation that uses
gross revenue and adjusted EBITDA without a working-capital bridge can assign
software-like economics to project coordination.

### Acquisitions as a recurring growth requirement

Granite, Sterling, Jacobs, KBR, and Quanta have all used acquisitions or
capability expansion. The test is not whether the acquisition increases
revenue. It is whether incremental cash after integration, working capital,
amortization, debt, earn-outs, partner claims, and dilution exceeds the price
paid.

### Joint ventures and noncontrolling interests

Consolidated revenue and operating cash can include projects where partners
own part of the economics. Distributions from joint ventures can support cash,
while capital calls and noncontrolling distributions consume it. Show:

`reported operating cash -> partner distributions and capital calls -> debt and interest -> cash available to common owners`

### Adjusted earnings and per-share presentation

Adjusted EBITDA and adjusted EPS can remove amortization, acquisition costs,
restructuring, spin-off costs, convertible-debt effects, or stock compensation.
Each adjustment may be understandable in isolation. The forensic question is
whether the same category recurs because the business model repeatedly requires
acquisitions, separation, employee equity, or financing.

## Who captures the cleaner economics?

The cleaner economics generally sit with the company that controls a repeat
relationship or scarce technical standard without funding the entire physical
asset.

AECOM may capture a cleaner fee from owner-side design and program authority,
but DSO and claims can weaken cash. Jacobs can capture more complex program
value, but PA financing and employee claims reduce the clean denominator. KBR's
mission work can be durable, but its separation and contract mix create a
capital-structure question.

Granite and Sterling can earn better project returns when materials, site, and
electrical capability are scarce, but they fund more labor, equipment, and
working capital. Quanta can benefit from repeat utility relationships, but it
still carries field capacity, safety, and acquisition burden.

There is no universal winner. The control point and the burden must be analyzed
together.

## Lyn Alden lens: demand durability does not remove liquidity risk

The cohort has several demand sources:

- public transportation, water, and resilience programs;
- defense, readiness, and federal mission support;
- data-center and semiconductor investment;
- grid, utility, and communications renewal; and
- industrial and process-technology modernization.

These needs can remain durable across cycles. The cash timing is still
liquidity-sensitive. Public funding can be authorized but delayed. Private
customers can defer projects when financing costs rise. Contractors can be
profitable on paper while receivables, contract assets, equipment, or
acquisitions absorb cash.

Higher rates therefore affect the cohort through several channels:

`customer project approval -> award timing -> contractor working capital -> debt and interest -> owner cash`

The most resilient company is not automatically the one with the largest
backlog. It is the one that can slow growth, preserve liquidity, maintain
qualified labor, and collect cash without adding leverage at the wrong point in
the cycle.

## Damodaran lens: turn the story into a model

The sector narrative must become:

`customer funding × relationship/control point × award share × labor and material capacity × project execution -> revenue or NSR -> gross profit -> working capital, maintenance capital, debt, taxes, acquisitions, and dilution -> common-owner cash`

The model should have separate cases for:

1. customer funding and award timing;
2. gross revenue versus fee-bearing or adjusted net revenue;
3. backlog conversion and project-margin stability;
4. labor, equipment, materials, and maintenance capital;
5. receivables, contract assets, retainage, and customer advances;
6. acquisition and joint-venture cash returns;
7. debt, interest, pension, claims, and guarantees; and
8. SBC, NCI, buybacks, dividends, conversion claims, and diluted cash per
   share.

Lifecycle matters. A high-growth company can rationally spend more on
acquisitions and equipment, but the valuation must show when growth spending
falls and mature owner cash appears. A mature contractor should not receive a
growth multiple merely because its backlog is large.

## The common cash bridge

For all six companies, start with:

`operating cash flow - maintenance capital - required project and contract funding - acquisition cash - partner / NCI claims - debt and interest burden - economic SBC and dilution`

Then disclose growth capital separately. This avoids treating every acquisition
as maintenance cost while still showing that acquisitions are not free growth.

The bridge should include a three-year history and a quarterly roll-forward.
One strong year is insufficient when contract assets, customer advances,
receivables, or acquisition timing can reverse.

## Next-filing evidence ladder

| Question | Confirming evidence | Falsifying evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Is demand durable? | Awards and funded programs remain diversified across public, private, utility, defense, and digital infrastructure customers | One customer, project type, or policy program explains most of growth |
| Is backlog high quality? | Backlog converts into adjusted revenue, margin, billing, collection, and cash without recurring estimate losses | Backlog rises through options, assigned work, or pass-through content while cash lags |
| Who controls the relationship? | Repeat awards, specification influence, renewal, cross-sell, and pricing or margin stability improve | The company is replaceable after the award and competes mainly on price |
| Is acquisition growth accretive? | Acquired capability produces incremental cash after debt, integration, earn-outs, and dilution | Revenue and adjusted EBITDA rise while owner cash per share falls |
| Is working capital normalized? | DSO, contract assets, retainage, payables, and customer advances move through the project cycle | Cash targets depend on recurring receivable releases or payable timing |
| Is the balance sheet resilient? | Debt declines, liquidity remains available, and equipment or project commitments can be reduced if demand slows | Growth requires new leverage while collections, margins, or project starts weaken |
| Does AI or modernization improve economics? | New demand produces measurable margin, productivity, pricing, or customer retention | AI is a narrative layer while labor, capex, and working-capital burden rise |

## Cohort conclusion

Infrastructure delivery is not one business. It is a chain of control points.

AECOM and Jacobs influence what gets designed and coordinated. KBR executes
mission and process complexity. Granite converts public and private funding
through civil work and materials. Sterling converts data-center and
semiconductor demand through site and electrical execution. Quanta supplies
repeat field capacity across utilities and communications.

The cleaner economics go to the company that controls a scarce relationship,
standard, or repeat workflow while keeping project and financing burden under
control. The largest backlog is not automatically the best economics. The
strongest evidence is collected cash after labor, materials, equipment,
receivables, contract assets, claims, acquisitions, debt, partner claims, and
dilution.

## What to check next

The next filing should connect new awards to funded backlog, project starts,
revenue, margin, billing, collection, and cash. Review contract assets,
retainage, customer advances, estimate revisions, loss provisions, labor and
materials availability, surety and guarantees, acquisitions, debt, and diluted
shares. For AI and modernization work, require evidence that the new demand
improves productivity, pricing, or customer retention after equipment,
working-capital, and financing costs. A larger order book without better
collection or owner cash does not confirm the thesis.
