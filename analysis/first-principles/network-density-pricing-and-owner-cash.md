# Network density, pricing, and owner cash: rail freight, parcels, and waste routes

Date baseline: 2026-09-14

## The first principle

Both a freight railroad and a waste operator earn value from a physical network
that customers do not easily reproduce. Their economics are not the same.

`customer need -> physical route or network -> service completion -> price and renewal -> maintenance and growth capital -> owner cash`

Waste operators collect material locally and deliver it to permitted disposal or
processing capacity. Railroads move large freight flows over right-of-way,
terminals, locomotives, cars, and interchange connections. UPS moves parcels
through pickup routes, sorting facilities, aircraft, vehicles, and delivery
routes. In all three cases, density can improve utilization and customer
retention. In all three cases, a large network can also create a large
replacement, labor, regulatory, and safety burden.

## What density controls

### Waste routes

Waste density can reduce empty travel, share depots and supervisors, improve
transfer-station utilization, and make disposal access more valuable. The key
measure is cash per route after trucks, labor, fuel, facilities, landfill
development, closure, environmental obligations, and acquisitions.

### Rail networks

Rail density can improve train and terminal utilization, spread dispatching and
maintenance costs, connect more origins and destinations, and make a railroad
more useful to shippers. The key measure is cash per freight movement after
track, bridge, locomotive, car, terminal, labor, safety, environmental, and
technology spending.

### Parcel networks

Parcel density reduces cost per package when existing routes, hubs, aircraft,
vehicles, and labor absorb more volume. It becomes less attractive when a
package requires new facilities, peak labor, extra aircraft capacity, or more
complex residential delivery. UPS's Amazon glide-down is the reverse case:
removing lower-quality volume may improve mix while leaving fixed assets and
labor that must be resized.

Density is not automatically a moat. It becomes valuable only when utilization,
service quality, pricing, and cash returns improve together.

## Comparative cash screen

Amounts are FY2025 dollars in billions, using each company's filed or company-
reported measures. The residual is a diagnostic screen, not normalized owner
earnings.

| Company | Network | Operating cash flow | Capital / growth spending | Residual before other owner claims | Core contradiction |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Union Pacific | Freight right-of-way, terminals, locomotives, cars | `$9.290B` | `$3.791B` capital investment | `$5.499B` | Strong cash after capital, but service and safety require continuous heavy renewal |
| UPS | Pickup, sorting, air, ground, and delivery network | `$8.450B` | `$3.685B` capex plus `$1.968B` acquisitions | About `$2.797B` | Amazon volume reduction may improve mix but leaves fixed-cost, labor, and transformation claims |
| Waste Management | Collection, transfer, landfill, recycling, RNG | `$6.043B` | `$3.227B` capital expenditures plus `$395M` acquisitions | About `$2.421B` | Route and sustainability cash must be separated from acquisition and environmental claims |
| Republic Services | Collection, disposal, recycling, treatment, RNG | `$4.296B` | About `$1.887B` capex plus `$1.1B` acquisitions | About `$1.309B` | Pricing and acquisition returns must fund a broad network and capital returns |
| Casella | Regional routes, transfers, landfills, resource solutions | `$329.8M` | `$245.0M` PP&E plus `$224.2M` acquisitions | About `($139M)` | Regional density is valuable but current growth requires significant reinvestment |

The comparison does not say rail is safer or waste is weaker. It shows that
scale and cash conversion must be measured against the type of network being
funded. Union Pacific has a larger residual because it operates a high-margin,
asset-intensive freight system. UPS has strong operating cash but loses part of
its residual to acquisitions and a network reset. WM and Republic have recurring
service density but spend more on sustainability, acquisitions, fleets,
landfills, and environmental claims. Casella is a smaller acquisition-led
network with a much narrower margin for error.

## Pricing is different in each network

Railroad pricing is affected by contract terms, commodity mix, fuel surcharge,
intermodal competition, service quality, and regulation. Core price must be
separated from fuel recovery because a fuel surcharge can raise both revenue and
fuel expense.

Waste pricing is affected by route density, local competition, landfill access,
fuel recovery, municipal contracts, customer retention, and disposal costs. A
price increase can improve cash if service quality holds, but it can reduce
density if customers leave.

UPS pricing is affected by package size and weight, residential versus
commercial mix, fuel surcharge, service level, customer concentration, and
labor and network cost. Higher revenue per package is valuable only if the
package still earns more after the route, sort, delivery, and transformation
cost required to serve it.

The common test is:

`core price -> customer retention and service -> gross profit -> working capital -> maintenance capital -> cash per share`

Price is durable only when it survives customer response, cost inflation, and
the investment needed to deliver the service.

## Reinvestment and accounting boundaries

Railroads capitalize large amounts of track, bridge, locomotive, freight-car,
terminal, and technology spending. Management must decide which expenditures
extend useful life, improve safety, improve efficiency, or create new capacity.
Depreciation follows the asset over time, but the current cash requirement is
immediate.

Waste operators capitalize trucks, transfer stations, landfill cells,
recycling equipment, RNG plants, and healthcare facilities. They also carry
closure, post-closure, remediation, and financial-assurance obligations that
may not appear as ordinary annual capex.

For both, the analyst should separate:

- replacement or maintenance capital;
- capacity and growth capital;
- acquisitions and integration;
- safety, compliance, and environmental spending;
- lease and financing claims; and
- asset-sale proceeds that are not recurring operating cash.

Adjusted EBITDA is especially incomplete for these businesses because it
excludes depreciation while the underlying assets must be renewed.

## Alden lens: durability with liquidity claims

The durable part of both networks is physical scarcity and customer dependence.
The fragile part is the balance sheet required to keep the network reliable.

Rail is exposed to industrial production, agriculture, automotive, chemicals,
energy, trade, labor agreements, safety, and merger regulation. Waste is exposed
to commercial activity, construction, municipal budgets, fuel, wages, recycling
prices, weather, landfill permits, and environmental regulation.

In a liquidity stress, end demand may remain essential while cash falls because
inventory, labor, maintenance, safety, closure, or debt claims rise. The
strongest operator is the one that preserves service and renewal without
requiring repeated external financing.

## Damodaran lens: lifecycle and valuation

The two networks should be valued by different operating drivers:

| Driver | Union Pacific | UPS | Waste operators |
|---|---|---|---|
| Demand | Carloads, freight categories, trade, industrial activity | Packages, shipper mix, e-commerce, healthcare, trade | Routes, customers, waste volumes, municipal and commercial activity |
| Price | Core price, fuel surcharge, mix, contract renewal | Revenue per package, fuel surcharge, service level, customer mix | Core price, yield, fuel recovery, disposal pricing, retention |
| Utilization | Train length, velocity, dwell, terminal and equipment utilization | Packages per route, facility throughput, labor hours, aircraft and vehicle use | Stops per route, transfer and landfill utilization, facility productivity |
| Capital | Track, bridges, locomotives, cars, terminals, technology | Hubs, automation, aircraft, vehicles, buildings, technology | Trucks, transfers, landfills, recycling, RNG, healthcare facilities |
| Burden | Labor, safety, environmental, merger and regulatory risk | Labor, transformation, pensions, aircraft, customer concentration | Labor, closure, remediation, permits, recycling, acquisitions and debt |
| Falsifier | Price and ratio gains with weaker service or deferred renewal | Higher adjusted margin with lower density, recurring transformation cost, or weak cash per share | Price gains with declining retention, rising claims, or acquisition cash deficits |

Union Pacific is a mature network with strong current cash after capital
investment. UPS also produces substantial operating cash, but its 2025 residual
after capital expenditures and acquisitions was much lower because the company
is reducing Amazon volume, resizing the network, and funding transformation and
automation. Its value depends on restoring package density and cash per share,
not only on reported adjusted operating margin. WM and Republic have similarly
durable local relationships, but their growth programs include acquisitions and
sustainability projects whose returns must be proven. Casella has more visible
acquisition and capital pressure.

## Conclusion

The broader pattern is that network ownership can produce cleaner economics
than commodity supply or project labor, but only when the owner can charge for
scarcity and fund renewal. Network density creates bargaining power; it does
not remove capital, labor, safety, environmental, or regulatory claims.

Future filings should be read through a common question: did the company turn
the physical network into higher cash per share after the complete cost of
keeping that network reliable, compliant, and difficult to replace?
