# Oilfield services: converting reservoir plans into collected owner cash

## Business economics: the first principle

An oil or gas reserve has no operating value until a company can drill a well,
complete it, connect it, and keep it producing. Oilfield-service companies sell
the equipment, crews, engineering, data, and field coordination required for
those steps. Their economics are therefore determined by customer capital
budgets and service intensity, not by reserves or oil prices alone.

The chain is:

`commodity outlook -> customer development budget -> well design -> service execution -> payment -> equipment renewal -> owner cash`

Halliburton sits in the execution step. ExxonMobil, ConocoPhillips, and EOG
own or develop reserves. Cheniere and midstream companies control later
transport or conversion steps. A service provider can be essential to a well
without owning the well's production cash flow.

## What is actually sold

Halliburton's Completions and Production segment sells pressure pumping,
cementing, completion tools, stimulation, artificial lift, and related work.
Its Drilling and Evaluation segment sells drilling systems, directional tools,
logging, formation evaluation, drilling fluids, bits, and engineering.

Customers pay for several outcomes:

1. The well is placed in the intended formation.
2. The well is completed so it can produce at the intended rate.
3. The well does not suffer avoidable integrity or execution failures.
4. The work is completed on schedule with acceptable cost and safety.
5. Data from the well improves later drilling and completion decisions.

The control point becomes stronger when service equipment is scarce, a crew is
qualified for a specific job, the service provider has field data, or multiple
steps are integrated under one contract. It becomes weaker when customers have
idle equipment, can switch suppliers, defer work, or negotiate service prices
more aggressively than the provider can reduce its costs.

## Why oil price is an incomplete explanation

The customer makes a capital-allocation decision before Halliburton records
revenue. A producer may have a high oil price but still reduce activity if it
expects costs to rise, production to decline, financing to tighten, or
shareholders to prefer cash returns. Conversely, a moderate commodity price
can support service activity if the producer has low-cost acreage, long-lived
contracts, or a strategic need to maintain supply.

This creates operating leverage in both directions. Service revenue falls when
customers cut activity, but equipment, facilities, trained personnel, and
support systems cannot always be reduced at the same speed. A later recovery
can then be profitable if utilization rises before major new capacity is
needed, but weak if the company must spend heavily to rebuild capacity first.

## Cash is earned after the job, not when the award is announced

An award or contract creates a future work opportunity. It is not cash. The
analyst must trace:

`award -> mobilization -> materials and labor -> work performed -> invoice -> collection -> warranty / dispute exposure`

Halliburton's FY2025 operating cash flow was $2.926B. Productive-asset
spending was $1.254B and acquisitions were $185M, leaving a simple residual of
about $1.487B. The residual was $2.396B in 2024 and $2.066B in 2023. This is
positive through the three-year period, but the 2025 decline matters because
dividends and buybacks totaled about $1.586B.

The cash bridge must include receivables and inventory. At December 31, 2025,
Halliburton reported $4.942B of receivables and $2.976B of inventory. In Q2
2026, receivables rose to $5.325B and inventory to $3.056B. Higher balances can
support future growth, but they also mean that customer payment and equipment
turns can absorb cash even while reported revenue and operating income improve.

## The burden stack

| Economic burden | Why it exists | Filing test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fleet renewal | Pumps, tools, vehicles, and field equipment wear out | Productive-asset spending, fleet age, utilization, depreciation |
| Skilled field labor | Jobs require trained crews and supervision | Headcount, wage pressure, safety, productivity, overtime |
| Inventory | Parts, chemicals, tools, and equipment must be available | Inventory turns, reserves, obsolescence, customer-specific items |
| Receivables | Customers are billed after work and may dispute invoices | Aging, allowances, concentration, contract assets, cash collections |
| Safety and environmental exposure | Field operations use heavy equipment and chemicals | Claims, insurance, reserves, incidents, regulatory costs |
| International execution | Work crosses borders and local operating systems | Currency, political risk, local partners, mobilization, repatriation |
| Technology | Digital tools require R&D, systems, data, and support | Capitalized software, R&D, hosting, cybersecurity, measurable savings |
| Financing | Equipment and working capital must be funded through the cycle | Debt, interest, maturities, liquidity, buybacks, covenant headroom |

The burden does not disappear because a company describes the business as
technology-enabled or asset-light. The burden may move from owned equipment to
leased equipment, vendors, joint ventures, or customers. The analyst must find
the party that ultimately pays and determine whether that party remains healthy
enough to continue paying.

## International work and North American work

North American pressure pumping is often more sensitive to customer timing,
equipment utilization, and short-cycle activity. International work can have
longer planning periods and more contract structure, but it adds political,
currency, mobilization, local-content, and collection risk.

Halliburton's Q2 2026 revenue was approximately $2.3B in North America and
$3.4B internationally. The useful conclusion is not that international work
is automatically better. It is that geographic breadth can reduce dependence
on one basin while increasing the number of risks that must be priced into the
return on equipment and labor.

## Three analytical lenses

### Durability and liquidity

The key stress test is whether Halliburton can preserve qualified crews,
equipment, and customer relationships through a weak activity cycle without
excessive debt or permanent fleet impairment. A $2.206B cash balance against
$7.158B of long-term debt at year-end 2025 provides operating flexibility but
does not remove cyclical risk. Liquidity should be judged against working
capital, debt maturities, equipment renewal, legal claims, and the length of a
customer-spending downturn.

### Narrative translated into numbers

“International growth” becomes revenue, segment margin, cash collection, and
return on mobilized assets. “Digital and automation” becomes job productivity,
equipment uptime, labor per job, pricing, retention, and software cost.
“Capital efficiency” becomes owner cash after sufficient fleet renewal, not
simply lower annual capex. “Integrated well delivery” becomes contract margin
after claims, change orders, idle time, and customer concentration.

### Forensic earnings quality

The most important checks are adjusted-to-GAAP reconciliation, working-capital
movement, productive-asset classification, customer receivables, recurring
impairments, legal and environmental costs, acquisition integration, and
stock-based compensation. The aim is not to label a company dishonest. It is
to prevent a favorable presentation from replacing a complete cash and risk
account.

## Cohort positioning

| Company type | What it controls | Main burden | Owner-cash question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Upstream producer | Reserves, acreage, production system | Depletion, drilling, decline, commodity price | Does capital preserve future production at a return above the cost of capital? |
| Oilfield-service provider | Execution capability, equipment, crews, data | Utilization, fleet renewal, labor, customer capex | Does service intensity produce cash after equipment and working capital? |
| LNG / midstream operator | Transport, storage, processing, export capacity | Debt, expansion capital, counterparty, utilization | Do contracts and corridors produce common cash after growth and financing? |
| Refiner | Conversion capacity, logistics, product placement | Feedstock, turnarounds, environmental costs, spread cycle | Does mid-cycle conversion margin cover renewal and compliance capital? |

Halliburton can have a stronger operating relationship than a generic equipment
vendor because it participates in a coordinated field workflow. It can still
have weaker shareholder economics than a high-quality producer or royalty owner
if customer bargaining power, fleet spending, and cycle volatility absorb the
value created by that relationship.

## Valuation discipline

Value should be based on normalized service-cycle cash, not a peak year or an
award headline. Halliburton's current screen uses $0.9B, $1.5B, and $2.1B of
normalized owner cash with 14x, 17x, and 20x equity cash multiples. The range
forces explicit judgments about North American recovery, international margin,
fleet spending, customer collections, debt, and legal risk.

The relevant reinvestment question is whether each additional dollar of
equipment and technology produces enough additional service cash after labor,
maintenance, and utilization risk. A higher multiple is justified only if the
control point becomes more durable and the incremental return remains high
through a full customer-spending cycle.

## What future filings must prove

- Segment and geographic revenue growth is accompanied by pricing and margin,
  not only higher activity.
- Receivables and inventory grow no faster than the work that will convert them
  into cash.
- Productive-asset spending is enough to preserve fleet quality and safety,
  while growth spending earns an identifiable return.
- Digital and integrated-contract claims reduce unit cost, improve utilization,
  increase pricing, or improve retention after their own cost.
- Dividends and repurchases remain covered after renewal capex, acquisitions,
  debt service, and a reasonable downturn reserve.
- Legal, environmental, safety, and customer-dispute costs remain visible in
  GAAP and cash rather than being repeatedly excluded as unusual.
