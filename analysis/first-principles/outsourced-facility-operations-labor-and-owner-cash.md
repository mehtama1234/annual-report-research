# Outsourced facility operations: why recurring contracts do not automatically create recurring owner cash

Date baseline: 2026-09-13

## The first-principles question

The basic economic chain is:

`facility operating need -> service contract -> labor and technical execution -> site uptime -> renewal, expansion, and new-site award`

An outsourced facility operator is paid because a customer needs a building,
airport, campus, factory, warehouse, or technical site to function. The
operator supplies people, supervision, engineering, equipment, safety systems,
and reporting. The customer keeps ownership of the site and usually retains
the power to rebid the work.

That distinction matters. A multi-year contract can make revenue more visible,
but it does not make the margin or cash flow fixed. The operator still has to
hire people, pay them before collecting from the customer, absorb wage changes,
manage claims, and deliver the defined service every day.

## What creates value

The strongest control point is reliable execution across many sites. A
provider can build customer knowledge, staffing processes, safety records,
technical capability, and operating systems. These can increase renewal and
expansion rates. They do not eliminate competition or protect the provider
from a customer reducing scope.

The key operating test is not simply bookings. It is whether a new or renewed
contract produces more cash after:

- wages, benefits, overtime, recruitment, training, and turnover;
- insurance, workers' compensation, safety, and legal claims;
- vehicles, site equipment, technology, and maintenance;
- working capital needed before billing and collection; and
- transition costs, acquisitions, leases, debt, and taxes.

## Recurring revenue versus recurring economics

“Recurring” should be separated into four questions:

1. Does the customer need the service every period?
2. Does the provider retain the contract when it is rebid?
3. Can pricing recover wages and other input costs?
4. Does the work convert to cash after the provider funds delivery?

The first question supports revenue visibility. The last three determine
owner economics. A contract may renew while its return declines. A booking may
be large while staffing is difficult. Revenue may rise while receivables or
unbilled costs consume cash.

## Why labor is the central financial variable

In a labor-heavy service business, employees are part of the delivered
product. Wage inflation is not a small overhead adjustment; it changes the
cost of every staffed contract. Turnover can create recruiting and training
costs, vacancies can create overtime, and weak supervision can create claims
or customer loss.

The useful measures are revenue and gross profit per employee, labor hours per
site, turnover, overtime, wage recovery, open positions, workers'
compensation, and renewal margin. A rise in adjusted EBITDA is less persuasive
if it comes with lower staffing, deferred training, weaker service, or costs
that later return as claims and contract losses.

## Bookings must pass an execution test

Bookings become economic only after a sequence of events:

1. The customer awards a defined scope.
2. The provider transfers or hires the required workforce.
3. Wage and benefit rates remain recoverable under the contract.
4. Service quality and safety meet the customer's requirements.
5. Work is accepted, billed, and collected.
6. The contract renews or expands at an acceptable return.

The next filing should therefore connect bookings to revenue conversion,
backlog or contracted work, staffing capacity, service-line margin, project
timing, and cash collection. Growth that cannot pass those checks is a
reported activity measure, not yet proof of value creation.

## Working capital is part of the business model

The operator may pay labor weekly or biweekly while billing customers later.
Receivables and costs incurred before billing can therefore grow with revenue.
This is not automatically a fraud signal; it may be normal contract timing.
It is a financing requirement and becomes a warning when collection days,
unbilled work, disputes, or bad-debt provisions rise faster than the business.

The cash bridge should show operating cash flow, capex, acquisitions, lease
principal, contingent consideration, and the working-capital movement. It
should also distinguish maintenance spending from growth spending where the
filing permits. Low property capex does not mean low reinvestment when the
business must continuously recruit, train, insure, equip, and supervise a
workforce.

## Acquisition accounting needs a full return test

Facility operators often buy technical capability, customer access, local
scale, or specialized labor. The cash cost is more than the initial purchase
price. It can include earnouts, contingent consideration, integration,
retention payments, acquired working capital, and follow-on equipment.

For each acquisition cohort, compare the total cash paid with acquired
revenue, gross profit, operating cash, customer retention, and incremental
capital needs. If management presents the deal as strategically important,
the return test should include the costs required to make the strategy work.

## Alden and Damodaran applied in plain terms

Lyn Alden's macro lens asks how the company behaves under changes in labor
costs, inflation, customer capital spending, liquidity, credit conditions,
and the business cycle. A facility operator may serve essential sites, but
customers can still reduce scope, rebid contracts, delay projects, or change
vendors. Balance-sheet liquidity and collection discipline determine how much
stress the company can absorb.

Aswath Damodaran's narrative-to-numbers lens requires the investment story to
be written as measurable assumptions: recurring site revenue, technical mix,
bookings conversion, renewal, wage recovery, productivity, working capital,
claims, maintenance needs, acquisition returns, and normalized owner cash.
The valuation should rise only when those assumptions improve in the reported
data, not merely when the company enters an attractive end market.

## Cohort comparison

| Cohort | Main control point | Main burden carried | First cash question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ABM | Distributed site execution and technical facility service | Wages, staffing, safety, insurance, receivables, transitions | What is cash per employee and site after labor and claims? |
| Cintas | Route density, customer retention, and garment/service workflow | Labor, fleet, inventory, route execution | Does density improve cash after replacement and fleet needs? |
| EMCOR | Local mechanical/electrical relationships and project execution | Labor, project estimates, working capital, surety | Does backlog become cash without estimate revisions? |
| Quanta | Skilled crews, permits, and infrastructure project delivery | Labor, equipment, contract estimates, safety, working capital | Does backlog convert after cost-to-complete changes? |
| Accenture | Enterprise relationships and skilled delivery capacity | Compensation, utilization, training, acquisitions | Does AI demand raise cash per professional after hiring? |
| Primoris | Specialty construction execution and backlog | Labor, equipment, project estimates, surety | Does contracted work produce cash after loss provisions? |

The cohort comparison separates the customer relationship from the burden
carrier. ABM and Cintas carry recurring route or site labor. EMCOR, Quanta,
and Primoris carry more project-estimate risk. Accenture carries skilled labor
and utilization risk. In every case, the control point must be tested against
the cost and working capital required to deliver it.

## Research conclusion

The durable question for this sector is not “How large is the facilities
market?” It is “Can the provider repeatedly convert operational complexity
into cash per employee and per site after all costs needed to preserve the
customer relationship?”

For ABM, FY2025 operating cash flow of $234.4M less $79.3M of capex and $36.7M
of acquisitions produced a $118.4M screen before a $59.0M contingent-
consideration payment and other owner claims. That result keeps the analysis
focused on labor productivity, collection timing, technical mix, acquisition
returns, and shareholder cash rather than on bookings alone.

## Risks, accounting tests, and what to check next

Test whether contract growth reflects higher price, added labor, acquisitions,
or real productivity. Compare organic revenue with bookings, renewal, customer
retention, wage recovery, site-level labor cost, receivables, contract assets,
claims, and contingent consideration. Read the proxy for incentives tied to
revenue, adjusted EBITDA, bookings, free cash flow, safety, or share price, and
check whether adjustments remove recurring transition, restructuring, or
acquisition costs. The next filing should show whether cash per site and per
employee improves after staffing, vehicles, insurance, working capital, and
acquisition payments.
