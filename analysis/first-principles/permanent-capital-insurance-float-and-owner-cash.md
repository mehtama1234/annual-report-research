# Permanent capital, insurance float, and owner cash

## Business economics: the question

Some financial companies earn fees for allocating other people’s capital.
Others retain permanent capital, assume insurance liabilities, or own operating
businesses and decide how cash is redeployed. These models cannot be compared
using one “free cash flow” formula.

The correct first-principles question is:

`who controls capital -> what liabilities fund it -> who carries the risk -> what cash can reach common owners`

## Four capital architectures

| Company type | Capital source | Control point | Main burden |
|---|---|---|---|
| Berkshire Hathaway | Insurance float, retained earnings, subsidiary cash, debt, securities portfolio | Allocation across insurance, operating businesses, securities, and acquisitions | Claims, regulated capital, railroad and utility renewal, successor judgment |
| Loews | CNA insurance, subsidiary cash, parent liquidity, and diversified operating businesses | Allocation across insurance, pipelines, hotels, packaging, and repurchases | Subsidiary capital, claims, maintenance, regulation, debt, taxes, and holding-company discount |
| Chubb | Premium float, shareholder capital, reinsurance, debt | Underwriting, claims, reserves, and investment portfolio | Catastrophe, reserve, reinsurance, asset/liability, and regulatory risk |
| Blackstone / Apollo | Fee-paying assets, performance allocations, insurance and credit capital | Fundraising, asset selection, financing, distribution, and product architecture | Marks, liquidity, leverage, fee pressure, insurance spread, and investor redemptions |
| Ares / KKR | Fee-paying assets plus credit origination; KKR also has Global Atlantic insurance and strategic holdings | Fundraising, underwriting, product design, insurance asset allocation, and monetization | Ares carries credit-deployment and acquisition-return risk; KKR adds insurance liabilities, carry timing, debt, preferred stock, and dilution |
| Insurance broker | Client relationship and risk-placement workflow | Advice, placement, renewal, data, and distribution | Talent, conduct, receivables, legal risk, and acquisition integration |

The capital source changes the denominator. A broker’s fee cash is closer to
common owner cash. An insurer’s operating cash includes premiums and claim
timing. A conglomerate’s operating cash must be divided among insurance,
regulated subsidiaries, capital renewal, and allocation choices. An alternative
manager’s fee-related earnings may be cleaner than performance income, but the
manager can still carry balance-sheet, insurance, financing, and illiquidity
risk.

Ares and KKR make this distinction measurable. In Q2 2026, Ares reported
`$409.9B` of FPAUM, `$491.1M` of FRE, `$521.5M` of Realized Income, and
`$170.0B` of available capital. Its main unresolved question is whether the
large credit and real-asset deployment pipeline earns good returns after
borrower losses, compensation, acquisitions, and financing. KKR reported
`$638.4B` of FPAUM, `$1.214B` of FRE, `$1.539B` of Total Operating Earnings,
and `$1.493B` of ANI. KKR's fee platform is larger, but Global Atlantic adds
insurance liabilities, required capital, asset-liability matching, and
liquidity obligations. Neither company's AUM, dry powder, or reported adjusted
earnings is unrestricted common cash.

## Why Berkshire is different

Berkshire combines the architectures. Its insurance operations generated
underwriting gains and provided float. BNSF and Berkshire Hathaway Energy
required substantial capital expenditure. Manufacturing, service, and retailing
generated operating cash with different working-capital and competitive risks.
The parent held a very large cash and Treasury-bill portfolio and could choose
whether to buy securities, acquire businesses, repurchase shares, or wait.

This creates an allocation option, not a guaranteed return. Cash held in
Treasury bills is liquid and resilient but may earn less than a productive
operating acquisition. A large acquisition can improve future earnings but
reduce liquidity and increase integration risk. A buyback can increase per-share
value if shares are cheap and destroy value if shares are expensive.

The control point is therefore management judgment under changing prices and
liquidity—not simply the ownership of a subsidiary.

Loews is a smaller, more mixed permanent-capital case. Its FY2025 net income was
approximately `$1.7B`, and the parent owns CNA, Boardwalk Pipelines, Loews
Hotels, and Altium Packaging. The value of diversification is not smooth
quarterly earnings; it is the ability to allocate capital and absorb unlike
operating cycles. The required denominator is subsidiary cash after insurance
capital, pipeline maintenance, hotel renovation, packaging investment, debt,
taxes, and parent costs.

## Cash and claims

| Company | Reported FY2025 cash indicator | Why it is not automatically owner cash |
|---|---:|---|
| Berkshire Hathaway | $46.0B net operating cash flow; about $20.9B consolidated capital spending | Insurance claims, subsidiary renewal, regulated capital, working capital, and allocation needs remain |
| Chubb | $12.8B operating cash flow | Premium and claim timing, invested assets, reserve obligations, and required surplus remain |
| Insurance broker | Fee-platform residual after capex and acquisitions | Receivables, talent, conduct, legal costs, SBC, debt, and acquisition maintenance remain |
| Ares / KKR | FRE, Realized Income, TOE, or ANI | Fee-related performance revenue, carry compensation, partner and NCI claims, acquisitions, debt, and for KKR the Global Atlantic liability and capital stack remain |

The cash bridge must be rebuilt around the business model. Subtracting capex from
Chubb’s operating cash does not produce the same economic result as subtracting
capex from a broker’s fee cash. Treating Berkshire’s insurance float as parent
cash double counts the asset and ignores the claim liability.

## Forensic tests

### Insurance underwriting

Underwriting quality requires accident-year loss ratios, catastrophe losses,
prior-period reserve development, rate, exposure, mix, expense ratio, and
reinsurance recoverability. A favorable combined ratio can be temporary if
reserve releases or unusually low catastrophe losses carry too much of the
result.

### Investment income

Investment income must be separated into recurring yield, realized gains,
unrealized marks, credit losses, duration exposure, private assets, and
liquidity. Higher rates can improve income, but claims inflation, asset losses,
and capital requirements can offset the benefit.

### Operating-company capital

For Berkshire, BNSF and BHE earnings must be assessed after renewal and growth
capital. A regulated utility can report higher rate-base earnings while needing
large debt and equity funding. A railroad can show a better operating ratio
while reducing maintenance or service resources.

### Allocation and incentives

Capital returns should be compared with the opportunity cost of retained cash.
The relevant questions are:

- Did an acquisition increase per-share value after integration and capital?
- Did a security purchase earn more than Treasury bills after risk?
- Did a buyback occur below normalized value and after required capital?
- Are executive incentives tied to per-share value, underwriting quality, and
  long-term cash, or to adjusted earnings and asset growth?

### Succession

The successor test is measurable. Future annual reports should show operating
earnings per share, cash deployment, acquisition returns, underwriting quality,
capital ratios, and the treatment of large cash balances. A successor can
preserve the balance sheet while failing to preserve allocation returns.

## Alden lens

Permanent capital is most valuable when liquidity is scarce. A company with
cash, low refinancing dependence, and a stable liability base can buy assets or
support subsidiaries when others cannot. But permanent capital can also become
a low-return asset if management cannot identify opportunities.

Insurance float is durable only when the insurer prices risk correctly. Retained
earnings are durable only when operating businesses reinvest at good returns.
The macro analysis must follow rates, inflation, credit, catastrophe, regulation,
and refinancing—not just the size of the cash balance.

## Damodaran lens

The narrative “permanent capital compounds” must be translated into:

`operating return after sustaining capital + underwriting return + investment return - required capital growth - allocation mistakes`

The valuation must account for lifecycle. Berkshire is mature and allocation-
driven; Chubb is a cyclical underwriting and investment compounder; alternative
managers are gathering and deploying capital while broadening product lines.
Each deserves a different growth, reinvestment, and terminal-value assumption.

## Filing-based falsifiers

The permanent-capital thesis weakens if cash remains idle while operating
returns decline, acquisitions dilute per-share value, insurance reserves worsen,
capital spending is deferred, regulated subsidiaries need repeated external
funding, or management presents adjusted earnings without including the costs
required to preserve the system.

The carrier thesis weakens if combined ratios rise, reserve releases reverse,
catastrophe losses exceed the modeled load, investment income depends on illiquid
marks, or capital returns reduce surplus below a prudent stress level.

For Loews, the conglomerate thesis also weakens if CNA or another subsidiary
requires repeated parent support, subsidiary cash cannot be upstreamed, operating
business returns deteriorate, or repurchases occur without a conservative
look-through valuation.
