# Electricity and backup power: who earns money from new capacity?

## More computing needs equipment, installation and continued service

An operating data center needs electricity delivered to its equipment, systems that distribute and protect that supply, cooling, and arrangements for interruptions. Expansion can create orders for several companies: equipment manufacturers, component suppliers, installation contractors and service providers. The companies participate in the same construction program but earn money from different work.

The customer must first fund and complete the facility. Equipment suppliers may buy components and build inventory before they collect payment. Contractors must recruit workers, coordinate installation and meet the contract's requirements. After commissioning—the checks needed to put the facility into service—equipment still needs maintenance, repairs and eventual replacement.

To compare investments, identify who pays each cost and who remains responsible when something goes wrong. A supplier can report rising orders while using cash to expand production. A contractor can win a large project but lose profit through delays or underestimated labor costs. A service business can earn repeat revenue, provided customers renew and the price covers technicians, parts and support.

The company cases below use historical figures from the linked studies. They distinguish reported results from our interpretation of future durability. Their reporting periods differ, so these figures do not constitute a same-period valuation ranking.

## What each supplier sells, and what it must fund

### Eaton: electrical distribution and protection equipment

Eaton sells distribution, protection, controls, and power-quality equipment. Its products are often specified into the architecture of a facility, which can create qualification, switching, and engineering advantages. That is a stronger control point than a generic component.

But the FY2025 bridge matters: $4.472B of operating cash less $919M of PP&E and $1.490B of acquisitions left about $2.063B, while dividends and repurchases totaled approximately $3.49B. Unbilled receivables rose to $759M. Eaton may own the cleanest interface while still requiring a much higher future cash denominator to justify its valuation.

### Vertiv: power and cooling systems for data centers

Vertiv combines power, thermal management, racks, and services inside high-density data centers. Its advantage is system integration and facility-level qualification. The customer is not buying a standalone box; it is buying a functioning power-and-cooling architecture.

The burden is execution: inventory, warranty, capacity expansion, customer concentration, acquisition integration, and the risk that a strong backlog converts into working-capital use before it becomes cash. The more complex the solution, the greater the potential switching cost—but also the greater the implementation liability.

### GE Vernova: generation and grid-equipment capacity

GE Vernova owns generation and grid-equipment technology, manufacturing sites,
installed-base service relationships, and scarce production capacity. Its 2025
RPO reached `$150.2B`, and first-half 2026 RPO reached `$176.3B`. Power and
Electrification produced strong segment margins, but the business also carries
large inventory, contract assets, warranty liabilities, customer deposits,
Prolec integration risk, and Wind project losses.

The control point is valuable because turbines, transformers, switchgear, and
grid systems are difficult to replace after qualification and installation. The
burden is heavier than for a narrow component supplier: GE Vernova must finance
factories and inventory, execute long-cycle projects, honor service and warranty
commitments, and absorb errors through the common balance sheet. Its first-half
2026 cash generation therefore has to be normalized for `$13.7B` of contract-
liability inflows and the Prolec transaction before it can support a durable
owner-cash conclusion.

### Hubbell: components used in electrical systems

Hubbell sits closer to the component and replacement layer. Utility Solutions
provides arresters, insulators, connectors, switches, meters, communications,
and protection and control products. Electrical Solutions supplies products for
buildings, factories, data centers, and industrial infrastructure.

Qualification and installed compatibility can create a durable position without
requiring Hubbell to fund the entire transmission line, power plant, or data
center. The burden is shorter-cycle but still real: manufacturing sites,
inventory, tariffs, distributor timing, pension funding, and acquisition debt.
FY2025 free cash flow was `$874.7M`, but acquisitions used `$958.3M`; Q1 FY2026
organic growth included meaningful price realization while Grid Automation
declined. Hubbell is therefore a useful test of whether qualified components
capture cleaner owner economics than larger equipment owners after acquisitions
and unit-volume normalization.

### Powell Industries: custom systems paid for as projects progress

Powell sits farther downstream in the delivery process than Hubbell. It designs
and fabricates power-control rooms, electrical houses, switchgear, automation,
and integrated systems for utilities, industrial facilities, LNG, petrochemical,
and commercial customers.

The control point is custom engineering and integration. The burden is project
execution: fixed-price estimates, customer milestones, receivables, contract
assets, warranty, facility capacity, and a ready workforce. Nine-month FY2026
bookings reached `$1.9B` and backlog reached `$2.4B`, but operating cash included
`$188.8M` from contract liabilities while receivables used `$146.4M`. Powell
shows why a project backlog can be valuable and still require a separate
milestone-to-collection cash test.

### nVent: electrical connections, enclosures and cooling products

nVent sits upstream of the complete compute-hall workflow. It supplies
connections, protection, enclosures, bus systems, cable management, control
buildings, power distribution, and liquid- and air-cooling products. Its control
point is qualification and physical fit inside a critical electrical system.

The FY2025 cash bridge shows the burden clearly: continuing-operations
operating cash was $649M, capital expenditures were $93.3M, free cash flow was
$561M, and the Electrical Products Group acquisition used $979.6M. The
residual after capex and that acquisition was approximately negative $424M.
nVent may have a strong technical interface, but its current growth phase is
acquisition- and capacity-funded. The next filings must prove EPG integration,
liquid-cooling returns, backlog conversion, warranty control, and cash per
diluted share.

### Cummins: generators, engines, parts and service

Cummins monetizes the reliability edge outside or alongside the facility: engines, generator platforms, distribution, service, and microgrids. Its installed base can create parts and service recurrence, and Power Systems gives it exposure to data-center backup demand without requiring it to own the data center.

The liability side is visible in the filing: $3.621B of operating cash less $1.235B of PP&E and $12M of acquisitions produced about $2.374B, while warranty accruals reached $2.778B and Accelera actions totaled $458M. Cummins’ control point is real, but emissions compliance, warranty, truck cyclicality, and transition losses are part of the economic cost.

### Generac: standby and commercial generators

Generac sells standby and commercial/industrial generation through a resilience channel. Its value rises as customers become less tolerant of outages, but its economics remain more exposed to product cycles, residential demand, tariff timing, channel inventory, and hyperscaler bargaining power.

This is the clearest example of why a reliability premium does not automatically become recurring profit. The company must turn equipment placement into service, monitoring, replacement, and customer retention before the installed base produces durable owner cash.

### Caterpillar: equipment, dealers, financing and service

Caterpillar owns the broadest network in this cohort: equipment, dealers, financing, parts, services, resale, and replacement. Its $51.2B FY2025 backlog and approximately $24B of services revenue provide visibility and a deep customer relationship. Power & Energy links the system to data-center and grid demand.

The burden is correspondingly broad. FY2025 operating cash of $11.739B less $2.821B of PP&E and $47M of acquisitions left $8.871B, but dividends and repurchases consumed about $7.978B. Inventory was $18.135B, Cat Financial carried roughly $25.2B of net finance receivables, and tariffs were a major 2026 variable. Caterpillar has meaningful customer dependence and service reach, but it is not light on assets.

### EMCOR: installing electrical and mechanical systems

EMCOR captures the installation layer: electrical and mechanical labor, project management, and field execution. Its control point is scarce skilled capacity and the ability to convert customer design into working infrastructure. Backlog is valuable because customers cannot easily add qualified crews.

But contractors carry a different burden: estimates, claims, labor, contract assets, customer-funded working capital, equipment, and project-level margin risk. A contractor can have excellent backlog and weak owner cash if project execution consumes capital or margins are revised.

## Compare the business model before comparing the valuation

A large order book identifies promised work under a company's definition of backlog. It does not establish the profit on that work or when payment will arrive. Similarly, a product's importance to a facility does not prove that its manufacturer can charge an attractive price. Customers can negotiate, change designs, seek alternative suppliers or postpone the project.

The following distinctions help form questions. They are not a fixed order of business quality.

| Business model | Why customers may continue buying | What could prevent attractive returns |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Equipment selected for a facility design | Replacing it may require engineering work, testing or approval | A delayed project, cheaper alternatives or a purchase price that assumes too much growth |
| A combined power and cooling system | Customers need compatible equipment and coordinated support | Warranty problems, difficult installation or expensive capacity expansion |
| Parts and service for equipment already installed | Operating equipment needs maintenance and repair | Low utilization, competition for service work or rising technician costs |
| Equipment manufacturing | Product performance, available supply and dealer support | Concentrated buyers, inventory losses or a costly product transition |
| Installation and project work | Customers need trained crews and reliable completion | Poor estimates, delays, disputed payments or labor shortages |

Even a business with repeat customers can be a poor investment at a price that assumes unrealistic future cash. Conversely, a contractor with well-priced work and disciplined execution may earn an attractive return. Each judgment requires the company's evidence and a stated valuation assumption.

## Follow orders through delivery and payment

Every company in the cohort should be tested through the same sequence:

`order/backlog -> specification or shipment -> installation/utilization -> billed revenue -> collected cash -> maintenance/growth capital -> owner cash`

Each company has a different weak link:

- Eaton: billed versus unbilled revenue and acquisition returns.
- nVent: organic versus acquired growth, EPG returns, backlog conversion,
  liquid-cooling capacity, warranty, and acquisition-funded cash.
- Vertiv: inventory, warranty, customer concentration, and backlog conversion.
- Cummins: warranty, emissions, inventory, service attach, and transition charges.
- Generac: channel inventory, tariff benefits, residential recovery, and service conversion.
- Caterpillar: dealer inventory, Cat Financial credit, used-equipment values, and capital returns.
- EMCOR: contract assets, project estimates, labor, claims, and collected cash.

The central accounting questions are who funds the work until the customer pays and which obligations remain after acceptance. Warranty costs, service commitments and unresolved contract claims can persist after the initial revenue has been reported.

## Macro and liquidity interpretation

Through the Lyn Alden lens, this cohort benefits from a capital-intensity cycle driven by electrification, AI, grid constraints, infrastructure policy, and the rising value of uptime. It also remains sensitive to rates, commodity inputs, public and private capex budgets, and liquidity. Higher rates can delay data-center projects, weaken construction financing, pressure customer equipment purchases, and increase the discount rate applied to long-duration growth.

The durable companies are those that own scarce physical relationships without needing perpetual balance-sheet expansion. The fragile ones are those whose growth requires more inventory, receivables, debt, or capacity than their normalized cash can fund.

## Damodaran interpretation

The cohort must be valued by lifecycle, not one multiple:

- Mature service and replacement cash deserves normalized-cycle assumptions.
- New data-center or grid capacity deserves growth assumptions only after incremental returns are observable.
- Acquisitions must be treated as reinvestment, not removed from the denominator simply because adjusted EPS rises.
- Backlog is a timing and probability input, not a cash-flow substitute.
- Capital returns should be compared with residual cash after required growth and maintenance investment.

This framework explains why Eaton can have the cleanest control point and the least attractive current valuation, while Cummins can have a less elegant interface and a more tolerable multiple. It also explains why Caterpillar’s large absolute cash flow cannot be compared directly with a smaller, more asset-light supplier.

## Cohort conclusion

The power-reliability economy is real, but the value is distributed unevenly.
Eaton captures specification and electrical control. nVent captures qualified
connection, protection, enclosure, and cooling content. Vertiv captures
integrated compute-hall complexity. GE Vernova captures generation and grid
equipment while carrying manufacturing, warranty, project, and working-capital
burdens. Cummins and Generac capture reliability and
generation. Caterpillar captures the installed machine network. EMCOR captures
field execution scarcity.

The cleanest economics belong to the company that can make the customer’s infrastructure difficult to replace while requiring the least incremental balance sheet to support it. The largest risks sit where backlog, inventory, financing, warranty, labor, and capital returns all rise together. Future filings should therefore be read less for another record-order headline and more for the conversion chain from committed demand to collected cash and sustainably funded owner returns.
