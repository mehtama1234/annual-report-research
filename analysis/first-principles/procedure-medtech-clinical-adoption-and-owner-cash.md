# Medical devices: how clinical use becomes revenue and profit

## A useful product still needs customers, payment and support

A medical device creates economic value only when it is approved, trusted by
clinicians, accepted by a hospital, used in a procedure, and paid for. A
technically good product is not automatically a good business. The company
must fund evidence, regulatory work, manufacturing, training, sales support,
quality systems, inventory, and the next generation of products.

Consider the purchase from the hospital's perspective. Clinicians need evidence that the product is appropriate for its intended use. The hospital needs trained staff, reliable supply and a way to pay for it. Even when a device meets a clinical need, staffing, budgets or purchasing decisions can limit its use. These are separate questions from whether the manufacturer has permission to sell the product.

For the manufacturer, the business becomes durable when customers keep using its products and revenue covers the cost of serving them and developing replacements. Repeat use can mean different things: purchasing another implant, replacing an instrument, buying consumables or maintaining an installed system. The company study must identify which payment recurs and what triggers it.

Boston Scientific adds a focused cardiovascular and MedSurg case to the
healthcare cohort. Intuitive Surgical controls a robotic procedure platform.
Stryker combines implants, instruments, and acquired procedure systems. Abbott
combines diagnostics, chronic-care devices, cardiovascular products, nutrition,
and pharmaceuticals. Johnson & Johnson combines medicines and MedTech. They
all participate in care, but their control points and capital burdens differ.

## Clinical adoption is a business process

For a specialized device, adoption normally requires:

1. Evidence that the device is appropriate for a defined use.
2. Regulatory authorization and continued compliance.
3. Physician training and confidence in the procedure.
4. Hospital approval, purchasing, stocking, and staff readiness.
5. Reimbursement or budget support.
6. Reliable supply and service during repeat procedures.

Each step can fail independently. A product may be approved but not adopted.
A physician may adopt it while a hospital limits its use. A hospital may stock
it while reimbursement remains inadequate. A procedure may be clinically useful
but constrained by staffing or operating-room capacity.

The useful metrics are therefore units, procedure volume, price, gross margin,
repeat accounts, training, reimbursement, product returns, and cash collection.
Revenue growth alone cannot show which step is creating value.

## Compare what customers buy repeatedly

| Model | Control point | Main burden | Owner-cash test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Focused procedure platform | Device category, physician use, hospital approval | R&D, clinical evidence, quality, inventory, acquisitions | Does procedure use grow faster than the cost of maintaining the category? |
| Robotic installed platform | Capital equipment, surgeon workflow, instruments, service | Platform R&D, training, hospital capital, service, inventory | Do recurring instruments and service cover platform reinvestment? |
| Broad MedTech portfolio | Implants, devices, brands, hospital relationships | Product lifecycle, salesforce, acquisitions, amortization, quality | Does portfolio breadth reduce concentration without hiding acquisition cost? |
| Diagnostics and chronic-care system | Tests, sensors, monitoring, clinical data, devices | Evidence, reimbursement, manufacturing, quality, portfolio renewal | Does repeated use produce cash after replacements, support, and claims? |

Boston Scientific is strongest in the first category. Its cardiovascular
business includes electrophysiology, structural heart, coronary, peripheral,
and related technologies. Its MedSurg business includes endoscopy, urology,
and neuromodulation. The company can expand a procedure category through a
better device, but it must keep earning physician and hospital acceptance.

## Did the existing business grow, or did the company buy more revenue?

**Organic growth** aims to describe growth in the comparable business after specified adjustments, often for acquisitions, disposals and currency movements. It is a company-defined measure, so read the reconciliation before comparing two issuers. An acquisition adds a business that was not previously included; its revenue cannot by itself show whether the original products gained customers.

Boston Scientific reported 2025 net sales growth of 19.9%, with 15.8% organic
growth after currency and specified acquisition or divestiture effects. That is
strong internal growth. But it also spent $1.593B on acquisitions in 2025,
after $4.640B in 2024 and $1.811B in 2023.

The correct question is not whether acquisitions are good or bad. It is whether
the acquired product creates incremental cash after integration, salesforce,
inventory, product-quality review, amortization, and the cost of replacing or
combining overlapping products.

An acquisition can raise revenue and adjusted EPS while reducing common-owner
cash if the purchase price is high or if the product requires heavy continuing
investment. A company that must buy a new category whenever an older category
matures has a different lifecycle from a company whose internal R&D replaces
most mature products.

## What remains after investment, and what adjusted profit excludes

Operating cash flow measures cash generated or used by ordinary operations. Purchases of property, plant and equipment—abbreviated **PP&E**—and certain software investments appear separately as investing payments. This matters when comparing profit with cash: equipment can require a large payment before its accounting expense is recognized over subsequent years.

Boston Scientific's 2025 operating cash flow was $4.534B. PP&E and internal-use
software used $876M, acquisitions used $1.593B, and investments and technology
acquisitions used another $254M. The simple residual after PP&E/software and
acquisitions was $2.065B, or about $1.811B after the additional investment
line.

The adjusted reconciliation excluded $897M of amortization, $245M of
acquisition/divestiture charges, $343M of restructuring charges, and $46M of
goodwill and intangible impairment in 2025. These items should not be treated
as one uniform category:

- Amortization is non-cash in the current period, but reflects a prior cash
  acquisition whose assets will eventually need replacement.
- Acquisition charges are recurring if the strategy requires frequent deals.
- Restructuring is a normal business cost when products, facilities, or
  acquired operations repeatedly need to be reorganized.
- Impairment is evidence that an earlier expected cash-flow path weakened.

R&D was $2.052B in 2025, up from $1.615B in 2024 and $1.414B in 2023. R&D is
necessary, but its economic return must be tested through approvals, adoption,
product life, gross profit, and replacement of mature revenue.

**R&D** means research and development. Its size tells us how much the company is spending to develop and improve products, not whether the spending will succeed. When starting from reported operating cash flow, do not subtract the cash operating payments already included in that figure again. Evaluate whether future spending will need to rise and whether products resulting from past spending earn enough to justify it.

Similarly, amortization records an accounting expense over the life of certain acquired assets; it is not a second payment for the acquisition. Adding it back can help explain current-period cash, but it does not erase the original purchase price or the need for future product investment. A reproducible analysis keeps the acquisition payment, accounting expense and forecast replacement spending distinct.

## Who pays when costs rise or products disappoint?

Hospitals and physicians bear procedure labor, facilities, training time, and
reimbursement risk. The device company bears evidence, manufacturing, quality,
inventory, sales, R&D, regulatory, and litigation risk. Payers and patients
bear affordability and coverage risk. Acquirers bear the risk that the category
will not grow enough to justify the purchase price.

The control point is valuable only if the company can retain enough of the
procedure economics after all parties are paid. Strong clinical adoption can
still produce weak shareholder returns if hospitals capture the benefit through
price negotiations or if the company must continually reinvest to defend its
position.

## Three analytical lenses

### Durability and liquidity

Procedure demand is supported by medical need and demographic trends, but
realized demand depends on hospital staffing, reimbursement, physician time,
and capital budgets. Liquidity matters because product failures, regulatory
issues, recalls, or delayed procedures can create costs before revenue recovers.

### Narrative translated into numbers

“Category leadership” becomes share, units, price, gross margin, and repeat
use. “Innovation” becomes R&D return, approval, adoption, and product life.
“Organic growth” removes acquisitions and currency. “Portfolio expansion” is
measured by acquired revenue, margins, integration cost, and cash return.
“Recurring procedure demand” is tested against utilization, reimbursement, and
hospital capacity.

### Forensic earnings quality

The central checks are adjusted EPS, acquisition accounting, intangible
amortization, impairment, restructuring, inventory step-up, contingent
consideration, stock compensation, product reserves, legal claims, and the
cash required for new businesses. The question is whether the reported growth
survives after the full cost of keeping the clinical platform useful.

## Cohort comparison

| Company | 2025 operating cash | Major cash burden | Current interpretation |

| Boston Scientific | $4.534B | $1.593B acquisitions; $876M PP&E/software | Strong organic procedure growth, but acquisition and product-lifecycle spending must earn returns |
| Intuitive Surgical | about $3.031B | Platform reinvestment, inventory, training, SBC | Installed robotic workflow creates recurring use, but hospitals fund capital equipment and the company funds platform renewal |
| Stryker | about $5.044B | Approximately $4.960B acquisitions plus capex | Broad procedure control is acquisition-dependent until acquired cash returns are proven |
| Abbott | about $9.566B | $2.171B PP&E, portfolio integration, quality, dividends | Broader and more diversified, but diagnostics and device lifecycle must be separated from nutrition and pharmaceuticals |
| Johnson & Johnson | about $24.530B | $17.541B acquisitions, $4.832B PP&E, litigation | Diversification improves breadth but creates pipeline replacement, litigation, and allocation complexity |

The comparison shows why “medtech” is not one economic category. Intuitive
has a tighter installed workflow. Boston Scientific has faster category and
acquisition expansion. Stryker has breadth but a heavy deal burden. Abbott has
more diversification. Johnson & Johnson has scale but carries drug, device,
litigation, and acquisition claims together.

## Valuation and lifecycle

The appropriate valuation denominator is normalized cash after necessary R&D,
quality, manufacturing, product renewal, and a realistic level of acquisitions.
Using reported adjusted EPS without charging these items gives a misleading
comparison between a mature product portfolio and a company still purchasing
new categories.

For Boston Scientific, a $1.2B / $2.0B / $2.8B normalized owner-cash range at
25x / 30x / 35x creates a wide value range because the current price requires
continued growth. The higher case requires strong organic procedure expansion,
successful clinical adoption, and acquisitions that add cash rather than merely
revenue and adjusted EPS.

## Filing-based falsifiers

- Organic growth drops sharply after recent acquisitions reach a full-year
  comparison.
- Product units rise but price, gross margin, or hospital economics weaken.
- R&D and selling costs rise faster than procedure use and durable gross profit.
- Recalls, regulatory action, litigation, or inventory reserves recur.
- Acquisition charges, restructuring, impairment, and contingent consideration
  remain normal rather than exceptional.
- Receivables and inventory grow faster than sales or cash collection weakens.
- SBC, debt, and acquisition spending consume the apparent benefit of EPS growth.
