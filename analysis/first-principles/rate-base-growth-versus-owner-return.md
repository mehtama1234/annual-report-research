# Rate-base growth versus owner return

Fortis provides a clean test of how regulated infrastructure creates value and where the accounting can be misunderstood.

**Rate base** is the portion of utility investment that regulators allow the
company to include when setting customer rates and calculating an allowed return.
It is not the same as cash, and a planned rate base is not yet an earning asset.
**Owner return** means the earnings and cash per common share after construction,
interest, taxes, maintenance, dividends, and the equity needed to fund growth.

## The control point

A regulated utility controls an operating service territory and a network that customers cannot easily replace. Regulation allows the company to recover prudent investment through rates and to earn an approved return. This can create durable economics because demand for reliable electricity and gas does not depend on a short product cycle.

The control point is conditional. The company must maintain reliability, satisfy the regulator, control costs, and keep service affordable. The regulator—not management alone—determines how much of the investment becomes earning rate base.

## Why rate-base growth is not cash growth

The sequence is:

`cash spent -> project built -> project approved -> asset placed in service -> rate recovery -> allowed return -> cash collection`

There can be years between the first and last steps. During that period, Fortis funds construction, interest, labor, materials, permits, and compliance. A larger rate base can therefore coexist with weak residual cash after capital spending and dividends.

The correct questions are: Was the project approved and placed in service? Was the final cost recovered or disallowed? What return is allowed and what return is actually earned? How much debt and equity funded the construction? Did common earnings and cash per share rise after dilution?

## The regulatory compact has two sides

Customers fund utility investment through rates, but they also bear affordability pressure. Regulators can delay recovery, reduce the allowed return, disallow costs, or require customer protections. A project may be strategically useful and still produce a poor shareholder return if its cost, schedule, or rate treatment changes.

Fortis’ geographic diversification reduces dependence on one regulator but adds exchange rates, different legal regimes, climate exposure, and multiple approval processes. Diversification improves resilience only if the parent can allocate capital without allowing weaker subsidiaries or projects to consume the group’s return.

## Who controls the relationship and who carries the burden?

| Participant | Value captured | Burden carried |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Utility | Reliable service, project execution, and approved return | Construction, operations, interest, maintenance, storms, and compliance |
| Regulator | Prudence review, allowed return, affordability, and reliability standards | Political pressure and the risk of underinvestment or delayed recovery |
| Customer | Electricity or gas service and reliability | Rates, connection charges, affordability, and service disruption |
| Lender | Interest on funding the capital program | Credit, refinancing, and regulatory-recovery risk |
| Common shareholder | Residual earnings after funding and regulatory claims | Dilution, project overruns, disallowances, and rate pressure |

This structure explains why a utility can be strategically essential but still
produce a poor shareholder return. The customer pays over time, while the
company and its lenders fund construction before recovery is certain.

## Earnings quality and forensic questions

1. **Rate-base composition:** Separate assets already placed in service from
   construction work in progress, regulatory assets, deferred costs, and planned
   projects. Only some of these are currently earning a return.
2. **Allowed versus earned return:** Compare the regulator’s allowed return with
   actual earnings after operating cost, interest, taxes, and parent expenses.
3. **Regulatory lag:** Measure the time between spending, approval, service entry,
   and customer collection. Longer lag increases financing cost and weakens the
   return on equity.
4. **Cost recovery:** Track disallowances, prudence reviews, storm costs,
   environmental costs, pension items, and customer contributions. An approved
   allowance can still be reduced or delayed.
5. **Capital spending:** Separate maintenance spending required to keep service
   reliable from growth spending expected to enlarge earnings. Both consume cash,
   but they have different valuation meaning.
6. **Adjusted earnings:** Reconcile adjusted EPS to GAAP earnings and identify
   whether storm, restructuring, regulatory, and holding-company charges recur.
7. **Funding and dilution:** Compare debt, preferred capital, equity issuance,
   dividend reinvestment, and subsidiary cash transfers with rate-base growth.
8. **Management incentives:** Read the proxy for weights on rate base, adjusted
   EPS, dividend growth, reliability, earned ROE, credit metrics, and project
   completion. Incentives tied only to asset growth can reward spending before
   shareholder returns are known.

## Lyn Alden lens: essential demand with financing sensitivity

Regulated utilities usually have more stable demand than merchant power or
discretionary businesses. Their cash remains sensitive to rates, inflation,
construction costs, weather, storms, wildfire, environmental requirements, and
customer affordability. Higher rates can reduce common returns even when the
rate-base plan remains unchanged because new debt and equity cost more.

The resilience test is whether the company can fund its capital plan while
maintaining credit quality and dividends through a period of higher rates,
construction inflation, delayed approvals, and weaker customer affordability.
Diversification across jurisdictions helps only if regulatory outcomes and
capital needs do not deteriorate together.

## Damodaran lens: translate the capital plan into cash per share

The narrative is that Fortis can compound rate base, earnings, and dividends
through essential infrastructure investment. The valuation must specify:

- capital placed in service versus announced spending;
- allowed ROE, earned ROE, regulatory lag, and disallowance probability;
- maintenance capex, growth capex, storm and environmental cost;
- construction schedule and cost variance;
- debt, interest, refinancing, equity issuance, and dividend reinvestment;
- subsidiary cash transfers and holding-company costs;
- customer affordability and regulatory support; and
- earnings and cash per diluted share after the plan is funded.

The current capital plan should not be valued as if it already earns a return.
Assign value only as projects are approved, completed, placed in service, and
shown to earn an acceptable return after funding costs.

## Cohort comparison

### Fortis, Duke, and American Water: the same regulatory mechanism with different burdens

Fortis, Duke, and American Water all depend on regulated essential-service
relationships, but their capital programs do not carry the same operating risk.
Fortis operates across several jurisdictions and emphasizes a C$28.8B
2026–2030 plan. Duke has a roughly `$103B` capital plan with generation, grid,
environmental, and large-load exposure. American Water's 2025 operating cash
flow was `$2.059B` against `$3.126B` of capital expenditures, because renewal,
quality, and resiliency work must be funded before all recovery arrives.

| Company | Control point | Main burden | Correct filing test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fortis | Diversified regulated electric and gas territories | Construction, interest, regulatory lag, currency, weather, and equity funding | Capital placed in service, allowed versus earned return, debt, dilution, and dividend capacity |
| Duke | Multi-state regulated generation and wires plus large-load opportunities | Large construction plan, fuel, environmental obligations, debt, equity, and affordability | Energized load, customer contributions, recovery, earned return, debt, and cost per project |
| American Water | Local water and wastewater systems and consolidation access | Renewal capex, PFAS and quality obligations, customer arrears, acquisition repair, and debt | Rate-base entry, collections, customer contributions, acquired-system cash, and unrecovered compliance cost |

The three cases show why rate base is only an intermediate measure. Fortis may
reduce single-regulator risk but still needs financing across jurisdictions.
Duke may have more load-growth upside but a longer path from a signed customer
commitment to collected revenue. American Water has essential demand but must
recover large renewal and compliance spending while customers face affordability
limits. The cleaner utility economics belong to the company that converts
approved investment into collected per-share earnings without excessive debt,
equity dilution, or political resistance—not automatically to the company with
the largest plan.

| Utility type | Cleaner economic feature | Main uncertainty |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Regulated wires utility | Established territory and visible recovery mechanisms | Regulatory lag, capex, debt, storms, and affordability |
| Regulated generation utility | Approved capacity and long-lived assets | Fuel, environmental rules, construction, and cost recovery |
| Merchant generator | Market pricing can rise quickly in scarcity | Commodity, weather, hedging, and refinancing cycle |
| Large-load growth utility | New demand may support rate-base expansion | Customer credit, connection timing, generation, and political resistance |

The regulated wires model often has cleaner demand visibility, while a large-load
model may have more growth but a longer chain from customer announcement to
collected cash. Neither should be valued from capital spending alone.

## Filing-based falsifiers

The conclusion should be revised if projects are delayed or disallowed, earned
returns remain below allowed returns, debt and equity grow faster than per-share
earnings, dividends depend on new capital, customer affordability limits rate
increases, regulatory assets accumulate without clear recovery, or storm and
environmental costs become recurring unrecovered burdens.

The next filing should show project cost and schedule, assets placed in service,
regulatory decisions, allowed and earned returns, debt and share issuance,
interest cost, customer bills, cash available after dividends, and earnings per
share after dilution.

## The valuation consequence

A regulated utility should be valued from per-share earnings, cash available after funding, credit quality, and the probability of rate-base realization. The model must not treat the announced capital plan as an asset already earning a return.

The cleanest economics occur when approved investment enters service on time, earns near the allowed return, and is funded without excessive dilution or credit deterioration. The burden carrier is the capital program that requires debt and equity before recovery is certain.
