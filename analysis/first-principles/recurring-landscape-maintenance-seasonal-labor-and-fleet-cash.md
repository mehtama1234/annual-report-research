# Recurring landscape maintenance: labor availability and fleet spending determine owner cash

Date baseline: 2026-09-13

## The first-principles chain

`property upkeep need -> maintenance contract -> seasonal and permanent crews -> vehicles and equipment -> completed service -> renewal or rebid`

Commercial landscaping is a physical service. The provider must place crews
at many sites, supply equipment and materials, meet a schedule, and maintain
the property to a standard the customer accepts. The contract can repeat, but
the work is performed again every period and the cost base must be funded
again.

The first economic question is therefore not whether a property needs
landscaping. It is whether the provider can repeatedly deliver the service at
a price that covers labor, equipment replacement, fuel, insurance, claims,
supervision, and financing.

## Maintenance and development are different businesses

Maintenance is recurring site work. Its economics depend on renewal, pricing,
route density, crew productivity, weather, fuel, and customer property
budgets. Development is project work. Its economics depend more on project
timing, customer construction budgets, materials, estimates, and completion.

Combining the two can make revenue growth and margin changes difficult to
interpret. A maintenance contract may support visibility while a development
project changes quarterly earnings. Each segment needs its own revenue,
margin, backlog, labor, equipment, and cash analysis.

## Labor supply is a capacity constraint

Landscaping requires people at the time and place where the work is needed.
Seasonality makes this harder: staffing demand rises during the main growing
season, while weather can change the amount and timing of work.

If workers are unavailable, the company can pay higher wages, use overtime,
delay service, accept lower growth, or reduce scope. A temporary-visa program
can expand labor supply, but it also creates exposure to government allocation,
worker availability, housing and travel logistics, and policy changes.

The right measures are seasonal headcount, visa workers, vacancies, wage
recovery, turnover, overtime, recruiting and training cost, revenue per crew,
labor hours per site, safety incidents, and renewal outcomes. Revenue growth
without adequate staffing can increase future claims and customer loss.

## Equipment is not a minor support cost

Mowers, trucks, loaders, irrigation equipment, and other fleet assets are
needed to deliver recurring maintenance. They wear out even when the customer
contract does not change. Capital expenditure therefore has a maintenance
component that must be paid before common shareholders receive cash.

Equipment-sale proceeds can reduce the current cash outflow, but they do not
remove the replacement requirement. A year with unusually low capex may mean
the fleet is young, or it may mean replacement was deferred. The next filing
should show fleet age, utilization, replacement cycle, maintenance versus
growth capex, finance leases, and equipment-sale proceeds.

## Reported cash can overstate durable cash

Operating cash flow is affected by collections, unbilled revenue, deferred
revenue, supplier payment timing, and other working-capital movements. A
source in one year can reverse in another. This is normal in many contract
businesses, but it means the analyst must study several years and not value a
single cash-flow peak.

A practical bridge is:

`operating cash flow - fleet and equipment capex + asset-sale proceeds - recurring acquisition and transition costs - lease principal - preferred claims - required debt service`

The result is closer to cash available to common equity. It still requires a
judgment about whether the capex level preserves service capacity.

## Adjusted metrics need a recurrence test

Adjusted EBITDA may exclude integration, transformation, restructuring, and
other costs. The exclusion can help compare operating periods, but the cost
should return to the owner-cash model when it recurs or is necessary to run the
business.

The same test applies to “cost management.” Lower personnel, vehicle, or
equipment expense can improve margin. It is durable only if service quality,
capacity, renewal, safety, and fleet condition remain intact.

## Alden and Damodaran applied

Lyn Alden’s lens asks how the model behaves when wages, fuel, weather,
commercial property activity, immigration policy, interest rates, and credit
availability change. High debt makes ordinary operating variation more
important because interest, lease payments, and working capital receive cash
before equity.

Aswath Damodaran’s lens turns the story into explicit assumptions: maintenance
renewal, price, route density, seasonal staffing, labor cost, development
volume, project margins, capex per crew, working capital, leverage, dilution,
and cash per common share. A favorable end market does not justify a higher
valuation unless these variables improve in the financial statements.

## Comparison with other labor-heavy services

| Company type | Recurring element | Main burden | Falsification test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| BrightView | Maintenance routes and site relationships | Seasonal labor, H-2B availability, fleet, fuel, weather, debt | Does cash remain after fleet renewal and staffing at market wages? |
| ABM | Facility and technical service contracts | Wages, hiring, claims, insurance, vehicles, receivables | Does cash per site rise after labor and working capital? |
| Cintas | Routes, uniforms, and recurring service | Labor, fleet, garment inventory, route density | Does customer density improve cash after replacement spending? |
| Accenture | Client relationships and skilled delivery | Compensation, utilization, training, acquisitions | Does AI demand produce cash per professional after hiring? |
| Primoris | Backlog and project execution | Labor, equipment, estimates, surety, working capital | Do projects finish without repeated loss provisions? |

The common issue is who bears the work required to make the recurring revenue
real. A contract or backlog is not the same as recurring owner cash.

## Research conclusion

The durable question is: “Can the operator convert a repeated physical need
into cash after maintaining the workforce and fleet required to serve it?”

BrightView makes that question unusually visible. It reported about 18,600
employees at September 30, 2025, including seasonal workers, and about 1,500
H-2B workers in FY2025. It generated $291.8M of operating cash flow, but
$254.2M of capex, leaving $65.2M of adjusted free cash flow after equipment
sale proceeds. The labor channel matters, but so does the equipment bill.

That is the correct starting point for a valuation and for a cross-company
comparison: measure the cash left after the system that produces the revenue
has been maintained.

## Risks, accounting tests, and what to check next

Separate maintenance revenue from development or project work, and compare
organic growth with acquisitions, price, route additions, and weather. Test
labor availability and wage recovery, seasonal-worker dependence, route
density, fleet age, maintenance and replacement spending, fuel, insurance,
receivables, debt, and customer retention. Review whether adjusted EBITDA or
free cash flow excludes recurring fleet, restructuring, integration, or labor
costs. The next filing should show whether recurring routes produce cash after
staffing the crews and replacing the equipment required to serve them.
