# Regional banks: what remains after funding costs and loan losses?

## Business economics: start with lending, deposits and services

U.S. Bancorp, PNC and the regional banks compared here earn money from a combination of lending, securities and customer services. Interest income has to cover the cost of deposits and other borrowing. Fees from payments, wealth management and business services help diversify revenue, but those services also require staff, technology, fraud controls and customer support.

Deposits are money owed to customers, not revenue earned by the bank. They can provide comparatively inexpensive funding, but customers can move their money or demand a higher rate. Lending creates a different risk: the bank must assess whether borrowers will repay. A higher loan rate does not necessarily improve returns if it comes with greater losses.

**Net interest income** is the interest earned on loans and securities minus interest paid on funding. A **credit provision** is an accounting expense for expected credit losses. Neither measure alone establishes cash available for shareholders. The bank must also pay operating expenses and maintain capital to support its assets and absorb losses.

The comparisons below use figures from the linked historical company studies. They ask whether customer relationships produce earnings after those costs, and whether acquisitions improve the business rather than merely make the balance sheet larger.

| Company | Potential source of repeat business | Costs and risks to examine |
|---|---|---|
| U.S. Bancorp | Payments, treasury, trust, and institutional servicing | Network fees, rewards, fraud, BTIG integration, and payment-system controls |
| PNC | Regional density, middle-market relationships, cash management, and FirstBank scale | Deposit beta, commercial credit, FirstBank conversion, capital, and integration cost |
| Truist | Combined regional scale, wealth, payments, and capital-markets relationships | Merger cleanup, deposit pricing, commercial credit, fee-cycle normalization, legal and control cost |
| M&T | Regional operating deposits, commercial relationships, treasury services, and local underwriting | Deposit repricing, regional credit, securities duration, liquidity, technology, and required capital |
| Regions | Low-cost Southeast deposits, treasury management, wealth management, and relationship lending | Deposit beta, Southeast credit and CRE, hedging, liquidity, technology, and required capital |
| WaFd | Western branch and transaction-deposit franchise being shifted toward business banking | Transition cost, legacy mortgage and real-estate exposure, deposit repricing, liquidity, and capital |

## Why customers keep their accounts with a bank

A business may use its bank to pay employees, receive customer payments, manage cash and obtain credit. These are repeated tasks, and moving them to another provider can require effort and testing. Local staff may also know the customer and its borrowing needs. Those relationships can help retain accounts, but the bank still has to price its services and lending well enough to cover costs and risk.

For U.S. Bancorp, the critical distinction is organic payment yield after
network fees, rewards, fraud, servicing, and technology. For PNC, it is organic
relationship growth after separating FirstBank's acquired balances and
integration cost from underlying customer economics.
For Truist, it is whether merger repair is genuinely nearing completion or
whether recurring technology, legal, employee, and control costs are being
classified as temporary while fee income is being supported by favorable
capital-markets conditions.
For M&T, it is whether relationship deposits and local underwriting support a
`3.70%` Q2 2026 net interest margin and approximately `$818M` of quarterly net
income after deposit repricing, credit provisions, and capital requirements.

## Why a bank needs a different cash analysis from a manufacturer

A manufacturer's operating cash flow less equipment spending can be a useful starting calculation. For a bank, loans, deposits and securities are central to the operating business, so that shortcut does not explain what shareholders can receive. Begin with earnings attributable to common shareholders, assess whether funding costs and credit losses are sustainable, then determine how much capital must remain in the business.

Ordinary expenses and the reported credit provision are already reflected in earnings. A forecast may replace an unusually low provision with a more representative loss assumption, but it should show that adjustment explicitly rather than subtracting the same expected loss twice. Required capital is a separate constraint: a profitable bank may need to retain earnings to support growth or strengthen its financial position.

Reported adjusted EPS should be reconciled to GAAP earnings and then tested for
recurring acquisition, restructuring, technology, legal, and control costs.
Capital returns are owner cash only after the bank retains enough capital for
stress losses and growth.

## What to check in the financial statements

- Separate balance growth from rate and mix effects in net interest income.
- Compare deposit growth with rate paid, noninterest-bearing mix, and wholesale
  funding.
- Reconcile fee growth to organic volume, market levels, client flows,
  acquisitions, and employee and technology expense.
- Track delinquencies, charge-offs, reserves, criticized loans, and risk-weighted
  assets by portfolio.
- Measure acquisition returns after integration, retention, goodwill,
  intangible amortization, dilution, and capital.
- Compare efficiency gains with uptime, fraud, regulatory findings, and control
  incidents.

## Macro and lifecycle interpretation

The macro question is whether operating deposits remain stable when liquidity
tightens and whether the balance sheet can absorb commercial and consumer
losses. The lifecycle question is whether payments growth or acquisition scale
earns above the cost of capital rather than simply increasing assets and
reported revenue. The valuation question is whether the market is paying for
proven through-cycle returns or assuming a favorable rate and credit outcome.

M&T adds the regional relationship-bank test. Its Q2 2026 CET1 ratio was about
`10.19%`, so earnings growth and capital returns must be read alongside risk-
weighted assets, criticized loans, reserves, deposit cost, and liquidity. A
regional franchise is valuable only if customers keep operating deposits with
the bank when rates and confidence change.

Regions adds a funding-and-fee quality test. Q2 2026 interest-bearing deposit
cost was about `1.69%`, annualized net charge-offs were about `42` basis points,
and CET1 was about `10.7%`. Treasury-management and wealth-management records
can make the model less dependent on loan spread, but they do not remove fraud,
technology, service, regulatory, credit, and capital requirements.

WaFd adds the business-model-transition test. It reported Q3 FY2026 NIM of about
`2.81%`, ROATCE of about `11.0%`, an efficiency ratio of about `53.69%`, and
approximately `$20.9B` of deposits. Those measures are useful only if the bank
can show that business deposits and treasury relationships are replacing weaker
mortgage dependence without creating new credit, staffing, or funding burdens.

The cleaner economics belong to the business whose recurring fee and payment
flows remain profitable after all service and control costs. The burden belongs
to shareholders when credit losses, fraud, technology failure, integration,
regulation, and capital requirements exceed the relationship's return.

## Definitions for a bank owner-cash analysis

**Net interest income** is interest earned on loans and securities minus
interest paid on deposits and other funding. **Net interest margin** divides
that spread by interest-earning assets. Margin can rise because loan pricing
improves, funding is cheap, or the bank takes more risk; the source matters.

**Deposit beta** measures how much of a change in market rates the bank passes
through to depositors. A high beta raises funding cost when rates rise. A low
beta is useful only if the deposits remain stable and do not later reprice.

**Credit provision** is the expense recognized for expected loan losses.
**Allowance coverage** is the reserve held against expected losses. Low current
charge-offs do not prove future losses are low if borrowers have not yet
become delinquent or collateral values are falling.

**Regulatory capital** is equity retained to absorb losses and support the
balance sheet under banking rules. It is not idle cash. Distributions are
available to common owners only after the bank retains enough capital for stress
losses, required ratios, planned growth, and liquidity.

For this framework, normalized common-owner earnings are the bank equivalent of
owner cash:

`normalized common earnings = recurring interest and fee income - funding cost - credit losses - operating control cost - taxes - capital retained for prudent growth`

This is an analytical definition, not a company-reported formula. It prevents
industrial free-cash-flow logic from being applied to a balance sheet where
loans and deposits are the operating resources.

## The deposit-to-owner-return chain

| Stage | Evidence to inspect | Main risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deposit relationship | Balances, rate paid, noninterest-bearing mix, uninsured share | Runoff or expensive repricing |
| Lending and securities | Portfolio mix, duration, yields, underwriting | Credit loss and rate risk |
| Payment activity | Purchase volume, treasury flows, client balances, fee yield | Fraud, rewards, network, technology cost |
| Fee collection | Organic volume, market levels, flows, service revenue | Acquired or cyclical revenue mistaken for recurring revenue |
| Acquisition conversion | Retention, cost saves, systems migration, capital use | Integration cost, control failure, dilution, goodwill impairment |
| Loss absorption | Provision, allowance, charge-offs, criticized loans | Loss recognition may lag deterioration |
| Capital decision | CET1, stress results, dividends, repurchases | Distribution reduces resilience before losses appear |

This chain explains why earnings and capital returns must be read with the
balance sheet. A bank can report fee growth while deposits become more
expensive, or report low losses while underwriting risk accumulates in loans
that have not yet matured.

## Payments and acquisitions require a full-cost test

Payments and treasury activity can be attractive because transactions recur
through customers' ordinary business. The bank still pays network fees,
rewards, fraud losses, disputes, technology, cybersecurity, operations labor,
compliance, and capital. The relevant measure is fee revenue after those costs
and after the capital needed to support settlement and counterparty exposure.

For U.S. Bancorp, compare organic payment volume and fee yield with rewards,
network expense, fraud, technology investment, and BTIG integration. For PNC,
separate FirstBank balances, revenue, expenses, retention, purchase accounting,
and integration cost from organic regional growth. For Truist, test whether
merger-related technology, employee, legal, and control expenses are actually
ending or are recurring costs of operating the combined franchise.

An acquired relationship is not worth its purchase price unless retained
deposits, payment flows, and fee income produce returns after integration,
capital, compensation, and control costs.

## Earnings-quality and possible financial-engineering tests

The main forensic risk is the combination of favorable rate, credit, fee,
acquisition, and adjusted-efficiency assumptions that creates an apparently
stable earnings stream. The filing tests are:

- Reconcile net interest income into balances, asset yield, deposit cost,
  wholesale funding, duration, and rate sensitivity.
- Compare deposits with rate paid, uninsured concentration, brokered or
  wholesale funding, and liquidity available under stress.
- Compare loan growth with underwriting standards, criticized and classified
  loans, nonaccruals, delinquencies, charge-offs, collateral, and allowance
  coverage.
- Separate organic fee growth from market-level effects, acquired revenue,
  capital-markets conditions, client flows, and one-time benefits.
- Add recurring technology, legal, cybersecurity, fraud, control, and
  acquisition costs back to adjusted efficiency or earnings measures.
- Reconcile goodwill and acquired intangibles with retained customers, expense
  savings, capital consumed, and impairment risk.
- Compare distributions with stress capital, diluted shares, balance-sheet
  growth, and through-cycle common earnings.

These are falsifiable economic questions, not accusations of improper
accounting.

## Management incentives and valuation

The proxy should show whether annual and long-term awards emphasize EPS, return
on tangible common equity, revenue, efficiency, stock price, fee growth, risk
measures, or regulatory capital. Record definitions, weights,
threshold/target/maximum levels, peer adjustments, and exclusions for mergers,
restructuring, integration, provisions, and legal or control costs.

An EPS target can reward repurchases or acquisition accounting even when the
relationship return is weak. An efficiency target can improve when the bank
underinvests in systems or controls. An asset-growth target can expand credit
and funding risk. A through-cycle return measure that includes credit losses,
capital, and control spending is more informative, but the proxy must establish
whether it is actually used.

For each acquisition, compare purchase price and dilution with retained
deposits, loans, payment flows, fee income, cost savings, integration spending,
capital consumption, and return on tangible common equity. Legal completion is
not an economic return.

## Lyn Alden and Damodaran application

The Alden lens asks how liquidity, rates, unemployment, commercial-property
values, household balance sheets, and confidence affect deposits, funding,
credit losses, and payment volume. A bank with strong fee businesses still
depends on confidence in its liabilities and access to liquidity.

The Damodaran lens values a bank through normalized return on equity, growth,
capital retention, and cost of equity rather than industrial free cash flow.
The forecast should separate net interest income, payments and treasury fees,
wealth and capital-markets revenue, credit provisions, control cost, taxes,
required capital, and diluted shares. Acquisitions enter only after retention
and integration results are measurable.

The market is paying for a through-cycle return, not one favorable margin
period. The key variables are recurring fee income, sustainable funding cost,
normal credit loss, required capital, and the price paid for growth.

## Cohort comparison and filing-based falsifiers

U.S. Bancorp has greater payments and institutional-servicing exposure, which
can produce cleaner fee economics but also network, fraud, rewards, and
technology costs. PNC has regional and middle-market relationship density,
with FirstBank adding scale and integration questions. Truist has combined
scale and broad relationships, but its case must prove that merger repair and
control spending have normalized.

Compared with global payment networks, these banks retain direct deposit,
lending, and balance-sheet exposure. Compared with pure asset managers or
brokers, they fund and absorb credit risk. A payment or treasury interface may
control the customer relationship while common shareholders still fund the
loan book, liquidity buffer, technology, fraud losses, and regulatory capital.

The thesis weakens if deposits leave or reprice faster than loan yields;
wholesale funding and liquidity dependence increase; commercial or consumer
losses rise faster than reserves; payment fees grow only through acquisitions
or market conditions; efficiency improves while control incidents worsen;
acquisition revenue fails to cover integration, dilution, and capital;
goodwill is impaired; or distributions reduce capital before a downturn is
visible.

The next filings should provide deposit beta and uninsured mix, liquidity and
stress-capital data, loan underwriting, criticized loans, nonaccruals,
charge-offs, allowance coverage, organic fee growth, fraud and rewards cost,
acquisition retention and integration spending, goodwill, compensation
metrics, diluted shares, and repurchase prices.
