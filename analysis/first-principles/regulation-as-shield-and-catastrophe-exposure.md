# Regulation as shield and catastrophe exposure

Edison International shows why regulation can stabilize ordinary earnings while leaving an equity holder exposed to rare, very large losses.

**Rate recovery** means that a regulator permits a utility to collect past or
future costs from customers through approved rates. It is not the same as cash
already collected. **Owner cash** means cash available to common shareholders
after grid investment, claims, insurance, debt, taxes, preferred claims, and
the capital required to keep the utility operating.

## The control point

The utility owns and operates an essential network in a defined territory. Regulation gives it a process for recovering prudent investment and earning an allowed return. That is a durable control point.

Wildfire changes the calculation. The same network can create liability if equipment is alleged to have caused or contributed to a fire. The utility must spend to reduce risk while also carrying claims, insurance, legal, and regulatory exposure.

## Why adjusted earnings need a recurrence test

Core earnings exclude significant discrete items. That improves the view of ordinary operations when an item is genuinely unusual. But wildfire claims, insurance costs, regulatory settlements, and recovery timing can recur because the underlying risk is part of the service territory.

The test is not whether management labels an item non-core. The test is whether the company must continue to incur the cost to preserve the operating license and whether shareholders must continue to fund the gap before recovery.

## The cash sequence

`grid investment and mitigation -> claim or avoided claim -> insurance or Wildfire Fund -> regulatory decision -> customer recovery -> cash available to owners`

The sequence contains several independent uncertainties. A safer grid can still require more capital. An insurance receivable is not cash until collected. A regulatory asset is not cash until recovered. A rate-base return does not necessarily cover catastrophe exposure.

## Who controls the relationship and who carries the burden?

| Participant | Value captured | Burden carried |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Utility | Essential service, approved rate recovery, and allowed return | Grid investment, operations, claims, mitigation, debt, and compliance |
| Regulator | Prudence review, reliability, affordability, and recovery rules | Political pressure, legal review, and risk of under- or over-recovery |
| Customer | Reliable electricity and service restoration | Rates, affordability, outages, and possible recovery of system costs |
| Insurer or public catastrophe fund | Premium or assessment for absorbing part of the risk | Claim limits, liquidity, exclusions, and funding adequacy |
| Common shareholder | Residual earnings after all claims | Unrecovered losses, dilution, debt, and catastrophe volatility |

Regulation can stabilize ordinary service revenue without guaranteeing that the
shareholder recovers every wildfire, storm, or environmental cost. The analysis
must therefore keep ordinary rate-base earnings separate from catastrophe
exposure.

## Earnings-quality and financial-engineering questions

1. **Core versus recurring risk:** Determine whether wildfire mitigation,
   insurance, legal, settlement, and regulatory costs recur because the service
   territory creates the risk. A management label such as “non-core” does not
   make a required cost disappear.
2. **Regulatory assets:** Separate approved recoverable costs from pending claims,
   disputed costs, and costs that depend on future political or legal decisions.
3. **Insurance receivables:** Track coverage, deductibles, exclusions, insurer
   credit, public-fund eligibility, and collection timing. A recorded receivable
   is not owner cash until collected.
4. **Wildfire liability:** Follow claims, settlements, legal provisions,
   insurance, public funds, regulatory treatment, and the effect on rate base and
   credit. A safer grid may still need more capital every year.
5. **Capitalization:** Test whether mitigation, legal, labor, and overhead costs
   are expensed or capitalized. Capitalization can move cost into future rates
   and depreciation without reducing its economic burden.
6. **Preferred and debt claims:** Measure interest, maturities, preferred stock,
   holding-company liquidity, and the equity needed to fund ordinary operations.
7. **Management incentives:** Read the proxy for weights on adjusted EPS, rate
   base, reliability, safety, mitigation spending, liability outcomes, credit,
   and common returns. Asset growth without liability adjustment can reward
   spending while risk remains with shareholders.

## Lyn Alden lens: essential demand with tail-risk liquidity

Electricity demand and regulated recovery can remain stable through ordinary
economic weakness. Wildfire, storms, inflation, insurance repricing, higher
interest rates, and legal judgments can create a sudden liquidity requirement.
The risk is not only the final loss; it is the timing mismatch between spending
to mitigate risk and later recovery from customers, insurers, or public funds.

The resilience test is whether the utility can fund mitigation and claims while
maintaining service, credit quality, and access to capital during a period when
rates rise and recovery is delayed.

## Damodaran lens: value ordinary earnings and catastrophe risk separately

The narrative may be that regulation protects the utility and wildfire risk is
contained by insurance or public mechanisms. Translate that into:

- ordinary rate-base earnings and allowed versus earned return;
- mitigation capex and the time until rate recovery;
- claims, legal provisions, insurance, public-fund recoveries, and deductibles;
- regulatory assets, liabilities, disallowance, and political support;
- debt, preferred stock, interest, equity issuance, and dilution;
- service reliability, customer affordability, and operating cost;
- tax effects, adjusted earnings, and recurring “special” charges; and
- common cash per share under ordinary and catastrophe scenarios.

Use a base case for ordinary regulated earnings and separate stress cases for
claim severity, recovery timing, insurance availability, and financing cost.
Do not hide catastrophe risk inside a single long-term growth rate.

## Cohort comparison

| Business type | Cleaner economic feature | Main uncertainty |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Regulated wires utility | Established territory and recovery process | Regulatory lag, capex, rates, and reliability costs |
| Utility with severe catastrophe exposure | Essential service and possible public recovery | Claims, insurance, legal outcomes, and dilution |
| Water utility | Essential demand and renewal investment | Affordability, quality rules, and capital funding |
| Property-and-casualty insurer | Pricing and investment of float | Catastrophe, reserve development, and capital |
| Fee-only broker | Fee income without underwriting liabilities | Client retention, acquisition spending, and talent cost |

Utilities generally have more stable demand than insurers, but a catastrophe-
exposed utility can combine regulated revenue with unusually large tail claims.
A fee-only broker has less direct catastrophe liability but also lacks the
utility’s protected service territory. The correct comparison is the cash left
after each business’s specific burden.

## Filing-based falsifiers

The conclusion should be revised if wildfire or storm costs recur without full
recovery, insurance or public-fund proceeds are delayed or unavailable,
regulatory assets accumulate, earned returns fall below allowed returns, debt or
equity rises faster than per-share earnings, mitigation capex expands without
lowering claims, customer affordability limits rate increases, or adjusted
earnings exclude costs required to preserve the service territory.

The next filing should show mitigation spending, claims and provisions,
insurance and public-fund recoveries, regulatory decisions, rate-base treatment,
debt, preferred claims, equity issuance, credit metrics, and common cash per
share.

## The valuation consequence

Edison should be valued using ordinary rate-base earnings plus a separate stress analysis for wildfire claims, insurance, recovery, debt, preferred stock, and equity access. The cleaner utility economics belong to the company that can fund resilience without repeated dilution or material unrecovered losses. Regulation is a shield only when the recovery mechanism remains politically, legally, and financially credible.
