# Rental, contracting, and labor scarcity: access versus ownership of the physical bottleneck

## The first principle

Infrastructure customers do not always want to own the equipment, crews, permits, and execution risk required to complete a project. Rental companies and contractors monetize that desire for access, but they do so differently.

The economic chains are:

`customer project -> equipment access -> utilization / rental rate -> fleet renewal -> resale cash`

and:

`customer capex -> award -> estimate -> labor, materials, equipment, and permits -> measured progress -> billing / collection -> repeat work`

The rental company owns the fleet and carries utilization, maintenance, financing, and residual-value risk. The contractor owns the project relationship and execution capability but carries labor, estimating, claims, contract assets, and collection risk.

## Rental economics: United Rentals

United Rentals controls equipment availability, local density, delivery, service, and the customer’s ability to flex capacity without owning a fleet. That is a real workflow control point when construction, maintenance, and industrial customers value speed and flexibility.

The burden is visible in the capital structure. Fleet purchases, non-rental equipment, maintenance, insurance, debt, utilization, rental rates, and resale values determine actual owner cash. Management-defined free cash flow is useful, but a simple OCF screen can overstate economics if fleet refresh is treated as discretionary growth when it is required to preserve competitiveness.

The first-principles test is not revenue growth. It is:

`rental rate × time utilization × fleet productivity - maintenance - logistics - financing - fleet renewal + resale proceeds`

Rental scale can improve purchasing and density, but a weak cycle can produce falling utilization and resale values simultaneously. The business provides equipment access while retaining an owned-asset balance sheet.

The Q4 2025 United Rentals earnings call adds an important operating test.
Management said more than 95% of the fleet was fungible across customer uses,
but also said that high utilization of some equipment reduced used-equipment
sales to about $2.73B versus an earlier expectation of approximately $2.8B.
Keeping equipment on rent can support current revenue while delaying resale
cash; selling too early can improve cash in the period while reducing future
rental capacity.

Management also described approximately $4.2B of 2025 total capital spending,
including about $3.4B of maintenance capital and $0.8B–$0.9B of growth capital,
and approximately $1.1B of planned 2026 growth capital. These categories are
management-defined and require reconciliation to fleet additions, disposals,
depreciation, utilization, and actual replacement needs. The call further
identified a 1.5% fleet-productivity hurdle and transportation/repositioning
costs as key margin variables. The cross-company lesson is that utilization
and rental rate must be evaluated after moving equipment, maintaining it,
financing it, and replacing it.

## Contractor economics: Quanta, EMCOR, Comfort Systems, MasTec, and Sterling

These contractors control coordination rather than a single patented asset. They assemble skilled labor, subcontractors, equipment, engineering, permits, safety systems, and project management into infrastructure that customers cannot easily execute themselves.

### Quanta

Quanta’s control point is decentralized craft labor and execution across utility, communications, energy, and industrial projects. Its 2025 OCF less capex, acquisitions, and investments was approximately negative $1.684B, even as accounting earnings and backlog were strong. The contradiction is not necessarily poor business quality; it is the cost of acquisition, equipment, contract working capital, and growth investment.

### EMCOR and Comfort Systems

EMCOR and Comfort Systems monetize scarce electrical, mechanical, HVAC, and building-system labor. Their backlogs and recurring customer relationships are valuable because qualified crews and local operating knowledge are difficult to assemble quickly. But percentage-of-completion accounting depends on estimates of labor, materials, subcontractors, schedule, claims, and final margin.

Revenue can grow while cash lags if contract assets rise, retainage accumulates, or customer advances reverse. A strong backlog is conditional future workload that still requires labor and execution; it is not a cash balance.

### MasTec and Sterling

MasTec and Sterling show how a strong data-center or infrastructure mix can coexist with weak current cash conversion. MasTec’s 2026 first-half OCF was approximately $120M against $188M of capex, producing a negative bridge while backlog exceeded $21B. Sterling’s mission-critical backlog was strong, but acquisition cash, contract capital, accounting changes, and working capital made the normalized owner-cash denominator unresolved.

These names are reminders that mission-critical work raises demand quality but does not remove project risk.

## Access versus ownership

The choice between rental and contractor models is a choice about where the customer places risk:

| Model | Customer buys | Company owns | Hidden burden |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rental | Flexible equipment access | Fleet, service, delivery, resale | Utilization, maintenance, financing, residual value |
| Contractor | Finished project and execution | Crews, estimates, project systems, relationships | Labor, claims, contract assets, safety, collections |
| Equipment OEM | Product and warranty | Factories, channel, installed base | Inventory, warranty, capex, pricing and channel risk |

The cleanest economics belong to the participant that controls scarce capacity while transferring the most variable burden without weakening the customer relationship. But the more burden is transferred, the more important it becomes to test the health of the counterparty—dealer, franchisee, subcontractor, or customer.

## The forensic bridge

Every rental and contractor should be tested through:

`customer need -> committed work -> capacity allocation -> utilization or measured progress -> billing -> cash collection -> maintenance / fleet / labor / acquisition reinvestment -> owner cash`

Company-specific weak links:

- United Rentals: fleet age, utilization, rental rate, maintenance capex, resale, debt, and acquisitions.
- Quanta: contract estimates, contract assets, claims, labor productivity, equipment, and acquisition returns.
- EMCOR/Comfort Systems: backlog conversion, estimate revisions, customer advances, labor/subcontractor costs, retainage, and cash.
- MasTec: backlog hierarchy, contract capital, customer concentration, capex, acquisition integration, and leverage.
- Sterling: organic versus acquired growth, contract capital, accounting changes, NCI, dilution, and data-center concentration.

The common category error is treating backlog, RPO, adjusted EBITDA, or percentage-of-completion revenue as equivalent to cash collected after all execution costs.

## Lyn Alden lens

Rental and contracting benefit from infrastructure, reindustrialization, grid, telecom, data-center, and energy investment. Their demand can be secular, but liquidity matters: customers finance projects, utilities approve rate base, hyperscalers release capital, and contractors hire or retain crews. Higher rates can delay projects and weaken equipment utilization even while long-term infrastructure needs remain.

Labor scarcity can create pricing power, but it also raises execution risk. A contractor that grows backlog faster than it can staff and manage work may convert scarcity into margin pressure and claims.

## Damodaran lens

Value the lifecycle explicitly:

- Rental: fleet growth, utilization, rental rate, maintenance, resale, debt, and replacement capex.
- Contractor: backlog probability, project margin, labor and materials, contract assets, claims, cash conversion, and acquisition returns.
- Data-center contractor: distinguish signed backlog from future phases and narrative pipeline; test concentration and customer funding.

The narrative earns a premium only if incremental capacity produces cash returns above the cost of capital after the company pays for fleet, equipment, crews, safety, acquisitions, working capital, and financing.

## Valuation: access economics after replacement and execution burden

Rental valuation should begin with a through-cycle fleet model: utilization,
rental rate, maintenance and growth capex, fleet age, resale proceeds,
financing cost, and the debt required to keep the network useful. A high
reported return is less durable if it depends on extending fleet lives or
keeping equipment on rent when the business normally needs resale cash.

Contractor valuation should begin with probability-weighted backlog rather than
the headline award balance. Each case should specify project margin, labor and
materials inflation, contract assets, retainage, claims, collection timing,
capex, acquisitions, and diluted shares. A backlog multiple is too generous if
the work requires working capital and acquisitions before it produces cash.

The useful comparison is owner cash per share under a normal cycle, a delayed
or lower-utilization case, and a strong infrastructure case. The current price
should be tested against the reinvestment needed to preserve fleet density or
craft capacity. If the valuation requires both sustained high utilization and
rapid fleet or labor expansion without a working-capital burden, it is pricing
the benefit while excluding the cost.

## Cohort conclusion

Rental companies sell flexibility and absorb asset risk. Contractors sell coordination and absorb execution risk. OEMs sell equipment and absorb manufacturing, warranty, and channel risk. The customer may be paying all three because physical infrastructure requires access, equipment, and labor simultaneously.

The cleanest economics are not determined by backlog size. They belong to the participant with scarce capacity, disciplined reinvestment, strong collections, and enough bargaining power to avoid carrying every downstream burden. Future filings should reveal whether utilization, project margin, backlog, and accounting earnings ultimately become owner cash.
