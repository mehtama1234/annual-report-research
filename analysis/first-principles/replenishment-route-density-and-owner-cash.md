# First Principles: Replenishment, Route Density, and Owner Cash

Date: `2026-09-13`

## The question

Some businesses have repeat demand without charging a subscription fee. Customers reorder food, medicines, medical supplies, or pet products because the underlying need continues. The important investment question is not simply whether demand repeats. It is whether the company that handles the repeat order captures enough value after inventory, delivery, labor, service, capital, and credit costs.

This essay develops that question through five companies:

- Chewy: consumer pet-care replenishment.
- AdaptHealth: home-medical equipment and supply replenishment.
- Cencora: institutional pharmaceutical replenishment.
- Sysco: foodservice replenishment.
- US Foods: a second foodservice and institutional replenishment test.

The common economic structure is:

`continuing need -> repeat order -> inventory and service execution -> relationship data -> switching friction -> owner cash`

The chain fails if any step consumes more capital or operating cost than the relationship can support.

## 1. Replenishment is a demand pattern, not a revenue label

Subscription revenue is usually identified by a contract, billing schedule, or renewal metric. Replenishment revenue may be reported as ordinary product sales. The repeat behavior is visible through different measures:

- orders per customer;
- deliveries per day;
- refill or resupply rates;
- case volume;
- active customers and spend per customer;
- prescription or specialty-product throughput;
- inventory turns and route density.

The absence of a subscription line does not mean the demand is irregular. It means the analyst must reconstruct repeat behavior from operating data.

The opposite error is equally serious. Repeat orders do not automatically create a good business. A distributor can have recurring sales and still earn inadequate returns if it must hold too much inventory, offer too much credit, operate inefficient routes, or spend heavily to maintain service quality.

## 2. The five-step economic chain

### Step 1: A need continues

Pet food is consumed, patients need supplies, pharmacies need medicines, and kitchens need ingredients. This creates a recurring demand base.

The demand can still be sensitive to price, reimbursement, customer failure, or substitution. “Necessary” does not mean “immune to economic pressure.”

### Step 2: Someone must organize the order

The customer may be able to order directly from a producer, but doing so can require managing many suppliers, delivery schedules, minimum quantities, product substitutions, quality requirements, and payment terms.

The intermediary earns a role by reducing that coordination work. Its value is not just transporting a product. It is making the customer’s operation easier to run.

### Step 3: The intermediary carries execution risk

The intermediary may have to finance inventory before the customer pays, maintain a delivery fleet, operate warehouses, hire route labor, manage temperature or quality controls, and absorb damaged or obsolete goods.

This is where recurring revenue becomes a capital question. The customer may enjoy a simple reorder process because the intermediary carries the complicated part.

### Step 4: Repeated use creates switching friction

Every successful order can add operational history: product preferences, delivery timing, credit terms, patient records, menu requirements, or ordering data. This can make the relationship more useful over time.

Switching friction is strongest when a service failure would disrupt patient care, kitchen operations, or a customer’s daily routine. It is weaker when another supplier can match assortment, price, delivery, and credit terms with little disruption.

### Step 5: The relationship must become owner cash

The final test is cash available to common owners after:

- maintenance capital expenditure;
- inventory and receivables required to support growth;
- leases and fleet renewal;
- acquisitions needed to preserve density;
- debt service and refinancing;
- stock compensation and dilution;
- product quality, compliance, and legal obligations.

If repeat demand grows but these costs grow faster, the company has a larger operating system without a better economic return.

## 3. The companies show different versions of the same pattern

| Company | Repeat need | Relationship control | Main burden |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chewy | Pet food, medicine, and care products | Autoship, pharmacy, account history, and convenience | Customer acquisition, fulfillment, retention, and service |
| AdaptHealth | Home-medical equipment and consumable supplies | Referral, payer, home-service, and resupply workflow | Reimbursement, field labor, supplier terms, and working capital |
| Cencora | Pharmaceutical and specialty-product flow | Distribution scale, specialty handling, and provider access | Thin spread, inventory, policy, customer concentration, and working capital |
| Sysco | Food, ingredients, and kitchen supplies | Local service, delivery reliability, assortment, and procurement | Fleet, route labor, facilities, food inflation, and restaurant cyclicality |
| US Foods | Food and institutional kitchen supplies | Route density, private label, vendor programs, local support, and MOXē | Inventory, vendor-rebate timing, customer credit, debt, leases, and network renewal |

The pattern is not that all five companies have identical economics. The pattern is that each sits between a continuing need and the operating work required to satisfy it.

## 4. Why route density matters

Route density means the number and quality of deliveries that can be served efficiently within a geographic area. It affects revenue per route, labor productivity, fuel cost per delivery, vehicle utilization, delivery frequency, and customer service.

Route density can improve through:

- more customers in an existing area;
- higher order frequency;
- greater share of the customer’s purchasing;
- acquisitions that fill geographic gaps;
- private-label products that increase gross profit per stop;
- digital tools that reduce ordering and service costs.

Route density can deteriorate when:

- customers close or reduce orders;
- customers shift to direct purchasing;
- delivery windows become more demanding;
- labor costs rise faster than gross profit;
- the company expands into low-density areas;
- customer mix shifts toward smaller or less creditworthy accounts.

The analyst should therefore ask whether reported sales growth uses the existing network more efficiently or requires a proportionate increase in trucks, facilities, people, and inventory.

## 5. US Foods and Sysco: the clean comparison

Sysco and US Foods are useful together because they test whether foodservice replenishment is a category pattern or a single-company story.

US Foods fiscal `2025` sales were `$39.424B`, operating cash flow was `$1.369B`, capital expenditures were `$410M`, and reported free cash flow was `$965M`. Sales grew `4.1%`, but total case volume grew only `1.0%`; food-cost inflation contributed to the difference. Independent restaurant, healthcare, and hospitality case volumes grew, while chain volume declined.

The interpretation is balanced:

- customer relationships and route density are producing real operating improvement;
- the company is not simply benefiting from more physical demand;
- vendor consideration, LIFO, food-cost timing, inventory, and customer credit need continuous reconciliation;
- the business remains financially leveraged, with approximately `$5.2B` of debt at fiscal year-end.

Sysco supplies the broader category comparison. In fiscal 2026, Sysco sales rose
3.9% to $84.6B, U.S. Foodservice volume rose 1.4%, operating cash flow was
$2.638B, net capital expenditures were $524M, and acquisitions used $189M. A
screening residual after those two uses was about $1.925B. That cash result was
better than fiscal 2025, but Sysco also sold $5.9B of receivables during the
year and received $176M from asset sales, primarily sale-leasebacks.

The comparison therefore has an important new risk. Sysco agreed to acquire
Jetro Restaurant Depot for approximately $29.1B, with about $21.6B of cash
consideration and 91.5M shares. The proposed financing includes approximately
$21B of new debt and hybrid debt. The transaction can increase purchasing and
route density, but it can also change interest expense, dilution, integration
costs, receivables funding, and ordinary capital available to the business. Its
synergies should not be included in normalized owner cash before they are
demonstrated.

If both Sysco and US Foods show stable volume, gross-profit discipline, route
productivity, and cash after capital spending, the replenishment thesis becomes
stronger. If both show revenue growth but weak volume, working-capital
dependence, and recurring capital needs, the category deserves a lower-quality
distribution multiple. Sysco adds the further test of whether a very large
acquisition improves the network or merely increases financial risk.

## 6. The accounting questions are part of the business model

The most important accounting questions are not separate from operations. They arise because the business has many transactions moving through inventory and vendor contracts.

### Vendor rebates

Vendor consideration may be recorded as a reduction of inventory cost and later cost of goods sold. The company must estimate amounts earned under supplier programs. The estimate affects gross margin before all related cash is collected.

The test is whether estimated vendor consideration is confirmed by vendor agreements, subsequent receipts, and stable historical ratios. A growing vendor-rebate balance without corresponding cash collection would weaken earnings quality.

### Inventory accounting

LIFO adjustments can affect reported cost of goods sold and adjusted EBITDA. Food-cost inflation can raise sales while changing the accounting value of inventory and the timing of gross-profit recognition.

The test is whether margins remain stable after separating price, volume, mix, food-cost inflation, LIFO, and vendor funding.

### Working capital

Operating cash flow can improve because inventory falls, receivables are collected faster, payables increase, or vendor receivables are realized. Those changes may be healthy or temporary.

The test is to compare several years and several quarters. A durable replenishment business should generate cash without requiring a permanent increase in payment delays or a repeated release of inventory.

### Adjusted profit

Restructuring, system transformation, impairment, share compensation, and integration charges may be excluded from adjusted earnings. A company can be operationally well managed and still have recurring costs that should remain in the owner-cash calculation.

The test is recurrence. If the same category of cost appears every year, it is part of the business model even if the amount varies.

## 7. Alden lens: durability depends on funding access

The durability question is not only whether people need the product. It is whether the intermediary can continue funding inventory, labor, vehicles, facilities, and credit during stress.

A higher-rate environment matters because distributors usually operate with low operating margins and meaningful working-capital requirements. Interest expense can consume a large portion of operating improvement. Customer failures can produce bad debts at the same time that inventory becomes harder to move.

The stronger businesses in this group have:

- high order frequency;
- low customer concentration or diversified customer types;
- short cash-conversion cycles;
- strong supplier relationships;
- route density that remains profitable after labor and fuel costs;
- enough liquidity to fund inventory through demand shocks.

The weaker businesses have repeat revenue but need constant external financing or favorable vendor terms to keep the system running.

## 8. Damodaran lens: growth must earn its reinvestment

Growth in replenishment businesses requires more than acquiring customers. It may require more inventory, warehouse space, trucks, sales staff, technology, credit, and delivery capacity.

The correct growth question is:

`incremental owner cash = incremental gross profit - incremental operating cost - incremental working capital - incremental maintenance and growth capital - financing and dilution costs`

US Foods can improve through customer share, private label, route density, and digital adoption. But each improvement must be tested against the capital and service cost required to achieve it.

A high valuation is justified only if incremental cash returns exceed the cost of capital for a long enough period. Mature distributors should not receive a growth multiple merely because adjusted EBITDA is rising from a prior efficiency program.

## 9. Who captures the cleaner economics?

The producer may own the product, but the distributor can own the repeated access relationship. The customer may receive convenience, but the intermediary absorbs inventory and routing complexity.

Cleaner economics usually appear in:

- customer data and workflow tools;
- private-label products with repeat demand;
- purchasing scale and vendor programs;
- dense local delivery networks;
- specialized handling that customers cannot easily reproduce.

The heavy burden usually appears in:

- inventory and receivables;
- fleet and facilities;
- frontline labor;
- quality, safety, and compliance;
- customer credit;
- debt and lease obligations;
- acquisition integration.

The best companies capture the cleaner relationship economics while keeping the physical burden per order falling. The worst companies grow the physical burden faster than the relationship value.

## 10. Filing-based falsifiers

The replenishment thesis should be downgraded if future filings show:

1. sales growing mainly through food-cost inflation or price while cases decline;
2. gross margin improving only because of vendor-rebate estimates or LIFO timing;
3. inventory and receivables consuming cash faster than gross profit grows;
4. customer credit losses increasing as smaller customers become a larger mix;
5. route productivity weakening despite higher sales;
6. maintenance capex and lease additions rising faster than reported free cash flow;
7. repeated restructuring and transformation costs excluded from adjusted earnings;
8. debt remaining high while repurchases continue;
9. digital-tool adoption failing to improve retention, order frequency, cost per order, or gross profit;
10. supplier or customer concentration increasing without a compensating contractual or operational advantage.

## Conclusion

Replenishment is a durable economic pattern only when repeated need, operational dependence, route density, and cash conversion reinforce one another. It is not enough that a customer must buy again. The intermediary must earn more from the relationship than it spends keeping the relationship reliable.

Chewy shows the consumer version. AdaptHealth shows the home-care version. Cencora shows the pharmaceutical-institution version. Sysco and US Foods show the foodservice version. Together they demonstrate why the archive should analyze repeat demand through both relationship ownership and burden allocation.

The decisive measure is owner cash after the system is fully funded. That is where the difference between a durable replenishment platform and a low-margin volume operator becomes visible.
