# Restaurants: who pays the costs and keeps the profit?

## Start with one restaurant

A restaurant earns money when customers spend more on their meals than the restaurant spends preparing and serving them. That calculation starts with food, workers, rent, utilities and supplies. But the profit left at the restaurant is not yet the money available to shareholders. The company must also pay for its offices, technology, taxes, borrowing and investment in the business.

This makes three questions necessary. Are more people buying meals? Does each restaurant keep enough of its sales after operating costs? And does the company still have cash after maintaining its restaurants and paying its other obligations? Revenue growth answers none of those questions by itself: it can result from higher prices or more locations even when established restaurants lose customers.

Ownership determines whose accounts contain those costs. Chipotle and CAVA operate their restaurants directly. A largely franchised company, such as McDonald's, receives fees from independent operators who run most of its restaurants. Those operators are the franchisees. A royalty is a payment for the right to use the brand and its operating system, often calculated as a percentage of sales. Rent and other payments may be separate.

The distinction matters because a franchise company can report strong fee income while its operators struggle with wages, food costs or debt. The costs still exist, even when they do not appear in the brand owner's accounts. To assess whether its earnings can last, we need to examine both the brand owner and its operators.

This essay explains that relationship using the linked company studies. Their figures are historical research snapshots, with reporting periods identified in each study. The remaining sections include the detailed accounting tests; the company sources should be checked before using any figure in a valuation.

## Two business models

### Company-operated systems: Chipotle and CAVA

Chipotle and CAVA directly own more of the store-level outcome. This gives management better control over food, labor, service speed, digital ordering, real estate, and customer data. It also means the parent absorbs wage inflation, food costs, occupancy, maintenance, opening costs, and traffic weakness.

Chipotle’s FY2025 traffic declined while average ticket increased, showing why revenue growth can conceal weaker customer frequency. CAVA’s operating cash was strong, but restaurant-level margin softened and its after-PP&E residual was only about $26M. In both cases, store count is not value by itself: new units must earn back construction, pre-opening, lease, labor, and maintenance capital without cannibalizing the existing system.

### Franchise and royalty systems: McDonald’s, YUM, RBI, and Wingstop

These companies control brand, menu architecture, franchise contracts, real estate, marketing, supply systems, and customer data while franchisees operate most restaurants. The parent’s cash can look highly asset-light:

- McDonald’s generated approximately $10.551B of FY2025 OCF less $3.365B of capex, leaving about $7.186B before financing and capital returns.
- YUM generated $2.010B of OCF, but capex and franchise-restaurant acquisitions left about $857M before $1.341B of dividends and repurchases.
- RBI generated approximately $1.297B after PP&E and acquired franchised restaurants, but franchisee incentives, remodels, debt, and advertising flows remain relevant.
- Wingstop’s OCF fell to about $153M and its reported gain on a U.K. master-franchisee investment makes recurring cash less obvious than headline growth suggests.

The parent may have cleaner cash, but the system can be unhealthy. If franchisees cannot earn an acceptable return after labor, food, rent, debt, remodels, and taxes, they stop opening stores, defer maintenance, discount aggressively, or sell back restaurants. Royalty growth can therefore coexist with burden transfer and weakening long-term system quality.

## Costs paid by franchisees still affect the brand

Franchise systems often appear capital-light because the parent does not report franchisee labor, inventory, rent, or restaurant debt on its own statements. Those costs have not disappeared; they have moved to another balance sheet.

The first-principles question is:

`parent royalty yield -> franchisee store contribution -> franchisee debt service and remodel -> ability to reinvest and open -> future royalty base`

If the franchisee return falls below the cost of capital, the parent eventually bears the burden through incentives, acquisitions of distressed stores, refranchising losses, closures, litigation, or weaker new-unit growth.

## Customer traffic is more informative than sales alone

Sales can grow through price, mix, delivery fees, larger orders, or inflation. Traffic and frequency better reveal whether the customer occasion is strengthening. A healthy system should show some combination of:

- positive transactions or visits;
- repeat behavior after promotions;
- stable or improving restaurant-level margin;
- acceptable franchisee cash return;
- new-store sales that are incremental rather than cannibalistic;
- digital ordering that improves throughput rather than merely adding complexity.

Value menus, loyalty programs, delivery, and digital ordering are not automatically durable advantages. They create value only if they increase profitable frequency and preserve store contribution after discounting, labor, delivery commissions, and technology costs.

## Follow sales through to cash

Every restaurant company should be tested through:

`traffic -> ticket -> restaurant sales -> food/labor/occupancy -> store contribution -> parent cash -> maintenance/growth capital -> franchisee or shareholder return`

Company-specific weak links:

- Chipotle: traffic, restaurant-level margin, Chipotlane economics, new-store payback, labor/food/occupancy, and cannibalization.
- CAVA: mature-store AUV, opening cadence, lease burden, pre-opening costs, SBC, and direct labor exposure.
- McDonald’s: U.S. traffic, value promotions, franchisee economics, rent/royalty collection, remodels, debt, and loyalty economics.
- YUM/RBI: franchisee profitability, refranchising gains, acquired restaurants, advertising-fund flows, debt, and incentives.
- Wingstop: master-franchisee health, systemwide sales versus parent cash, investment-sale gains, franchisee openings, and capital returns.

The common presentation risk is to treat system sales as parent revenue, royalty growth as independent of franchisee health, adjusted restaurant margin as owner cash, or refranchising gains as recurring economics.

## Household budgets and borrowing conditions

This applies the macroeconomic questions in our Lyn Alden framework: how household spending power, inflation and credit conditions affect a company's ability to keep earning money.

Restaurants are highly exposed to household liquidity. Inflation in rent, food, fuel, debt payments, and wages changes both customer demand and operator margins. Value-oriented brands can benefit when consumers trade down, but they also face pressure to discount. Strong brands with everyday frequency are more durable than occasion-only concepts, yet even a low-ticket meal competes with groceries and at-home consumption.

Franchise systems can be more resilient at the parent level because they transfer fixed operating costs, but that resilience is conditional on the franchisee balance sheet. When credit tightens, the parent’s hidden operating partners may become the weak link.

## What the growth assumptions must justify

This applies our Damodaran framework: connect a company's growth story to the sales, costs and reinvestment required to support its valuation.

Value restaurant companies by lifecycle and ownership model:

- Company-operated: mature-store sales, traffic, restaurant margin, maintenance capex, new-unit payback, lease obligations, and labor productivity.
- Franchised: royalty/rent cash, franchisee health, system growth, incentives, acquired restaurants, advertising-fund separation, and debt.
- Emerging concepts: unit economics, mature-store cohort curves, cannibalization, real-estate quality, G&A, SBC, and the time to self-fund growth.

The narrative only earns a premium when a new store creates value after all store and parent costs, and when franchisees can reinvest without repeated corporate support. A high royalty margin can produce clean cash or represent a delayed claim on a weakening operator network.

## Cohort conclusion

The parent with the cleanest reported cash is not necessarily the system with the healthiest economics. Company-operated brands retain more burden but also more control and learning. Franchise platforms transfer burden and can scale royalties faster, but their moat depends on the franchisee’s return and willingness to keep investing.

The conclusion depends on whether customers keep visiting and operators can keep investing. Future filings should be read for customer visits, spending per order, store profit, franchisee returns, closures, renovation support and debt. Dividends and share repurchases should be compared with cash remaining after the company pays what is needed to maintain its restaurants and support its operators.

## Definitions for the restaurant owner-cash analysis

**Traffic** is the number of customer visits or transactions. **Average
ticket** is the amount spent per visit. Sales can rise because traffic rises,
because the ticket rises, or because the company opens more locations. These
sources have different durability.

**Restaurant-level margin** is store sales minus food, labor, occupancy, and
other directly attributable store costs, divided by store sales. It is not
corporate owner cash because it excludes corporate overhead, construction,
technology, taxes, financing, and maintenance.

**Franchise royalty** is a fee paid by a franchisee for use of the brand,
operating system, location, and support. It can be high-margin to the parent,
but the franchisee must still earn enough after wages, food, rent, debt,
remodels, taxes, and local operating costs to keep the relationship healthy.

For this framework:

Start with parent operating cash flow less cash capital expenditure. Show acquisitions and any other investing or financing payments separately. Store wages, food, ordinary rent and operating support payments are already reflected in operating cash flow when classified there; do not subtract them again. Franchisee assistance needs its own classification: an operating payment, loan, investment or purchase of a restaurant can affect different sections of the cash-flow statement. Reconcile the actual payment before adjusting the subtotal, then explain how future support and share dilution affect the valuation.

For company-operated restaurants, assess whether store labor, food, occupancy and opening costs are likely to rise relative to the amounts already reported. For franchised systems, parent cash is sustainable only if operators can fund their stores and continue to maintain them and serve customers. These are forecasts of required spending, not instructions to deduct the same historical cost twice.

## The customer-occasion-to-cash sequence

| Stage | Evidence to inspect | Main burden |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Customer choice | Traffic, frequency, price, value, loyalty | Household budget and competition |
| Order | Ticket, mix, promotions, delivery | Discounting and commission cost |
| Store execution | Food, labor, speed, accuracy, occupancy | Wage, food, rent, and service risk |
| Store contribution | Margin after direct costs | Store may not repay construction or lease |
| Parent capture | Store profit or royalty/rent/fee | Corporate support and brand spending |
| Reinvestment | Remodel, technology, new unit, training | Capital and franchisee funding |
| Repeat visit | Retention, loyalty, unit health | Churn, closure, or franchisee distress |

The customer occasion is valuable only when the restaurant delivers it
repeatedly at a price that works for the customer, operator, franchisee, and
parent.

## Company-operated and franchised systems

Company-operated systems such as Chipotle and CAVA retain more store revenue
and store profit. They also carry food, labor, rent, equipment, construction,
maintenance, opening, and traffic risk directly. New units should be measured
by mature sales, store margin, build cost, payback, cannibalization, and cash
after maintenance.

Franchise-heavy systems such as McDonald's, YUM, Restaurant Brands, and
Wingstop collect royalties, rent, advertising-related fees, and other payments
while franchisees carry much of the labor, food, rent, and store financing.
That improves parent cash conversion, but it creates a hidden balance-sheet
test: the franchisee must earn enough to maintain the store and fund the next
unit.

The parent can eventually absorb franchisee stress through incentives,
remodel assistance, closures, repurchases of stores, litigation, lower
royalties, or slower unit growth. Burden transfer is therefore conditional,
not permanent.

## Unit economics and digital ordering

For every company-operated cohort, calculate:

`traffic × average ticket - food - labor - occupancy - other store costs = store contribution`

Then subtract build cost, pre-opening cost, maintenance, technology, taxes,
and the time value of capital. For franchise cohorts, calculate the same store
contribution for the operator and then the royalty, rent, and support cash for
the parent.

Digital ordering, loyalty, delivery, and pickup lanes can improve frequency,
throughput, and customer data. They can also add commissions, discounts,
kitchen complexity, labor, equipment, waiting time, and order errors. The
correct test is incremental contribution after all channel costs, not digital
sales percentage.

## Earnings-quality and possible financial-engineering tests

The main forensic risk is treating systemwide sales, royalty growth, adjusted
restaurant margin, or refranchising gains as parent owner cash. Test:

- traffic and frequency separately from ticket, price, mix, delivery, and
  inflation;
- systemwide sales separately from parent revenue and parent cash;
- franchisee sales and openings against franchisee margin, debt, closures,
  remodels, and financial health;
- company-operated store margin against food, labor, occupancy, pre-opening,
  maintenance, and technology cost;
- new units against mature-store cohorts, build cost, payback, and
  cannibalization;
- loyalty and digital activity against incremental visits, retention,
  discounting, commissions, and profit per order;
- advertising-fund inflows and outflows separately from corporate economics;
- acquired or refranchised restaurants against purchase price, support,
  integration, and post-transaction cash; and
- parent dividends and repurchases against cash needed to preserve the full
  system and franchisee support.

These are testable system-health questions, not accusations of misconduct.

## Management incentives and capital allocation

The proxy should identify whether management is rewarded for system sales,
traffic, comparable sales, restaurant margin, royalty revenue, unit growth,
franchisee profitability, free cash flow, return on invested capital, share
price, or per-share cash. Record definitions, weights, threshold/target/maximum
levels, and exclusions for refranchising, acquisitions, restructuring,
commodity costs, and unusual items.

A system-sales target can reward price increases or franchisee expansion while
traffic and operator returns weaken. A royalty target can reward collection
from a franchisee that is underinvesting. An adjusted store-margin target can
exclude recurring technology, labor, remodel, or pre-opening costs. A
franchisee-return and parent-cash-per-share measure is more complete only if it
includes the support needed to keep the system healthy.

For each major promotion, compare traffic, ticket, repeat behavior, store
contribution, and franchisee return. For each new unit or acquisition, compare
capital, mature sales, cannibalization, operator financing, integration, and
cash per share.

## Lyn Alden and Damodaran application

The Alden lens asks how household liquidity, wages, rent, food, fuel, debt
payments, employment, and consumer confidence affect visits and ticket. Higher
costs can support nominal sales while weakening traffic and restaurant margin.
Franchise systems can protect the parent from direct costs, but not from
franchisee failure or delayed reinvestment.

The Damodaran lens values company-operated stores, franchise royalties and
rent, advertising systems, real estate, new-unit growth, and corporate support
separately. The forecast should specify traffic, ticket, store margin, mature
cohort curves, build cost, payback, cannibalization, franchisee health, parent
capital, debt, taxes, and diluted shares.

The lifecycle question is whether new units create incremental customer
occasions or merely divide existing visits. A high royalty margin is valuable
only if the underlying stores remain profitable and investable.

## Cohort comparison and filing-based falsifiers

Chipotle and CAVA retain direct control and direct burden. McDonald's, YUM,
Restaurant Brands, and Wingstop transfer more store labor and capital to
franchisees while retaining brand, contracts, real estate, marketing, and
royalty economics. The parent controls customer access and standards; the
operator carries much of the labor, food, rent, remodel, and local financing
burden; customers control frequency and price tolerance.

The thesis weakens if traffic falls while sales depend on price or promotions;
restaurant-level margin declines; franchisee closures, distress, or deferred
remodels rise; royalties grow while franchisee returns fall; new units
cannibalize existing stores; digital sales require increasing discounts,
commissions, or labor; refranchising gains replace recurring cash; or parent
capital returns reduce support for a weakening system.

The next filings should provide traffic, ticket, retention, store margin,
franchisee openings and closures, operator economics, remodels, unit payback,
cannibalization, digital contribution, advertising-fund flows, acquisitions,
debt, parent support, incentive metrics, diluted shares, and repurchase prices.
