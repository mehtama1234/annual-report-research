# Routine consumer brands, bottlers, and owner cash

## The basic economic question

A consumer brand is valuable only when it produces repeat demand without requiring ever-higher promotion, discounting, distribution support, or capital. The annual report must show the path from consumer purchase to owner cash.

The path is: household or personal routine, product choice, package and channel, reported revenue, cost of goods and trade support, marketing and distribution, working capital, capital expenditure, and finally free cash flow. Each step can weaken the claim that the brand is durable.

## Coca-Cola as the worked case

Coca-Cola reported FY2025 revenue of $47.9B and organic revenue growth of 5%, while global unit case volume was even. This means reported growth came from price, mix, geography, and portfolio effects rather than more total cases. Coca-Cola Zero Sugar grew strongly, but that does not by itself prove that the whole system is gaining durable volume.

Reported free cash flow was $5.296B. Excluding a $6.069B fairlife contingent-consideration payment, the company presented $11.365B of free cash flow. The difference matters because an investor must decide whether the payment is an unusual acquisition settlement or part of the real economic cost of building the portfolio. The BODYARMOR impairment is also evidence that a recognized brand can require a later reduction in carrying value.

The bottler structure moves much of the local production, delivery, labor, inventory, and equipment burden outside the parent. That can make the parent appear to need little physical capital. It does not eliminate the cost; it changes where the cost appears. The relevant evidence is therefore not only Coca-Cola's margin. It is also bottler profitability, channel inventory, pricing, package affordability, concentrate sales, and the parent's support obligations.

## Definitions and the full cash chain

**Unit volume** measures physical cases, servings, or equivalent units sold.
**Price/mix** measures the effect of price changes and changes in package,
channel, geography, or product mix. **Organic revenue** removes specified
acquisition, divestiture, and currency effects. These measures answer different
questions and should not be substituted for one another.

The full chain is:

`consumer occasion -> brand and package choice -> retailer / bottler / distributor -> sale and collection -> ingredients, packaging, labor, marketing, and capital -> owner cash`

Owner cash means cash left for common shareholders after the business pays for
the assets and operating work needed to preserve demand. A parent brand owner
may report high margins because bottlers or distributors fund local plants,
trucks, inventory, and route labor. A more integrated food and beverage company
may capture more gross profit but also carries more raw-material, labor, plant,
and working-capital risk.

## Control point versus burden carrier

| Layer | What creates value | Who carries the burden |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Brand owner | Trademark, formula, product design, marketing, and consumer choice | Brand investment, innovation, regulation, acquisitions, and support |
| Bottler or distributor | Production, route delivery, shelf availability, and local execution | Plants, trucks, labor, inventory, equipment, and retailer terms |
| Retailer or food-service channel | Access to the consumer and placement at the purchase occasion | Shelf space, labor, inventory, promotions, and working capital |
| Consumer | Repeat purchase and willingness to pay | Household affordability and substitution risk |
| Common shareholder | Residual cash after all participants and reinvestment | The risk that the brand requires more support than it returns |

This table prevents the parent from receiving credit for economics that are
actually funded by a bottler, retailer, franchisee, or consumer. It also prevents
the analyst from treating high parent margins as proof that the entire system is
healthy.

## Cohort comparison

| Company type | Cleaner economic feature | Main burden or uncertainty |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Concentrate and brand owner | High-margin intellectual property and broad distribution reach | Bottler health, price affordability, acquired-brand write-downs, and support |
| Integrated food and beverage company | More direct control of manufacturing and shelf execution | Ingredients, packaging, labor, plants, retailer power, and debt |
| Focused functional-beverage company | Clearer category and physical case-growth measurement | Promotion, bottler dependence, product regulation, and category substitution |
| Premium spirits company | Brand pricing and long-lived product identity | Aging inventory, cooperage, discretionary demand, and route-to-market cost |

The cleaner parent economics are not automatically the best investment. They may
reflect costs borne by another participant, while an integrated company may earn a
lower margin but retain more control and learning. The comparison must ask who
funds production, inventory, distribution, and demand creation.

## Lyn Alden lens: affordability and channel liquidity

Routine beverages often hold up better than large discretionary purchases, but
defensive demand is not unlimited pricing power. Inflation, household income,
currency, commodity inputs, packaging, retailer concentration, and bottler
liquidity can change the cash outcome. Premium spirits add greater exposure to
travel, hospitality, gifting, and discretionary trade-up.

The stress case should combine price increases with lower unit volume, retailer
concessions, bottler distress, packaging inflation, higher marketing needs, and
working-capital funding. A brand owner may appear resilient while the bottler,
retailer, distributor, or consumer absorbs the adjustment.

## Damodaran lens: convert the brand story into numbers

The valuation model should separately forecast volume, price/mix, gross margin,
marketing, bottler and distributor economics, retailer terms, working capital,
capex, acquisitions, contingent payments, impairment, taxes, debt, and terminal
growth.

For Coca-Cola, the central question is whether flat volume can become durable
cash without permanent affordability damage. For PepsiCo, it is whether broader
categories justify the larger capital and retailer burden. For Monster, it is
whether case growth produces cash after promotion and route cost. For
Brown-Forman, it is whether brand relevance and price/mix cover aging inventory
and discretionary-category risk.

## Earnings-quality and incentive questions

The main concern is usually not fabricated revenue. It is presenting price-led
growth, favorable channel timing, an unusual acquisition payment, or adjusted
profit without showing the cost required to preserve the brand.

- Reconcile revenue to volume, price/mix, currency, acquisitions, and
  divestitures.
- Track receivables, inventory, payables, promotional accruals, and bottler
  support rather than accepting operating cash at face value.
- Treat impairments as evidence that earlier expected cash flows or brand values
  were revised, not as proof of misconduct.
- Separate recurring capex and brand investment from acquisition cash,
  contingent consideration, asset sales, and working-capital releases.
- Read the proxy for incentives tied to volume, price, organic revenue, adjusted
  EPS, free cash flow, return on invested capital, dilution, and bottler health.
  Price-led or acquisition-led targets can reward growth before its durability is
  known.

The valuation denominator should be normalized owner cash after these costs. The
next filing should show whether the cash remains after the channel and brand
system are fully funded.

## General tests for a routine brand

- Does volume hold after price increases?
- Does market share hold against private label and cheaper substitutes?
- Are distributors and retailers carrying healthy inventory and earning acceptable returns?
- Does gross margin remain after marketing, trade spending, and input costs?
- Does operating cash flow convert to recurring free cash flow after capex, acquisitions, contingent payments, and working-capital changes?
- Are impairments, restructuring, or “temporary” costs recurring?
- Does management reinvest enough to preserve the brand without hiding weak returns?

## Valuation discipline

The valuation denominator should be normalized owner cash, not the most favorable adjusted number. For Coca-Cola, a $7B/$10B/$13B cash range at 28x/32x/36x produces implied equity values of $196B/$320B/$468B. These are scenario outputs, not price targets. They make the required assumptions visible: volume, price/mix, bottler health, marketing, brand economics, unusual payments, capex, debt, and shareholder distributions.

The thesis is falsified if price continues to rise while volume, market share, bottler health, or cash conversion weakens; if brand acquisitions repeatedly require impairment; or if shareholder returns depend on nonrecurring cash sources.
