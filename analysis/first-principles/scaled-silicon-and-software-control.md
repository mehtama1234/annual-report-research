# Scaled silicon and software control

Broadcom shows why “technology company” is not a sufficient economic category. Its semiconductor and infrastructure-software businesses share customers and technical relationships, but their cash risks are different.

**Design-in** means a customer has selected a component for a system and tested
it sufficiently that replacing it requires meaningful engineering and approval
work. **Owner cash** means cash left for common shareholders after manufacturing,
research, support, acquisitions, interest, debt, and dilution are funded.

## The control point

An infrastructure component becomes valuable when it is designed into a customer’s system, performs a necessary function, and is expensive or risky to replace. A software product becomes valuable when it remains embedded in daily operations, holds configuration and process knowledge, and continues to receive customer funding through renewals.

Broadcom can hold both types of control. Custom AI accelerators and networking silicon influence system performance and power use. VMware and other infrastructure software influence how workloads are deployed, secured, and managed. The common question is not whether the technology is important. It is whether Broadcom can keep a meaningful share of the value after customers, foundries, cloud operators, employees, and capital providers receive their claims.

## Who controls the relationship and who carries the burden?

| Business area | What creates value | Main burden |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Custom silicon | Customer-specific performance, power, and system integration | Design cycles, concentration, foundry, and packaging dependence |
| Networking components | Bandwidth, connectivity, and qualification in a system | Product transitions, supply commitments, and customer capex |
| Infrastructure software | Workload management, configuration, security, and renewal | Support, development, retention, acquisition, and debt cost |
| Manufacturing partners | Physical production and packaging capacity | Capacity, yield, delivery, and geopolitical constraints |
| Common shareholder | Residual cash from all businesses | Concentration, integration, interest, stock compensation, and cycle risk |

Broadcom controls design choices and software relationships more directly than it
controls factories or customer budgets. It can report high margins while
suppliers, customers, and capital providers carry other parts of the economic
burden. That does not make the economics false; it identifies what the owner-cash
calculation must include.

## Why the two businesses cannot be combined casually

Semiconductor revenue is tied to designs, production, customer programs, and equipment spending. It can grow very quickly and then slow when customers digest capacity or change architecture. Software revenue can renew over many years, but it carries retention, pricing, product-support, and acquisition-return risk.

A consolidated adjusted margin can conceal this difference. If semiconductor growth lifts the whole company, software durability may look stronger than it is. If VMware adds recurring revenue, the company may appear less cyclical while acquisition amortization, debt, and integration costs remain significant. The analysis must maintain separate denominators before combining them.

## The owner-cash test

Start with reported cash from operations. Then ask four questions:

1. How much cash is supported by collected revenue rather than receivables, supplier terms, or customer prepayments?
2. How much research, product support, equipment, and software investment is required to preserve the control point?
3. What acquisition, integration, interest, and debt costs are required to maintain the portfolio?
4. How much of the remaining cash belongs to each diluted share after stock compensation and repurchases?

This produces a more useful conclusion than labeling Broadcom asset-light or high-margin. The relevant result is cash per share after the costs that keep the semiconductor designs and software relationships alive.

## Earnings-quality and financial-engineering questions

1. Separate AI, networking, wireless, storage, industrial, and other silicon by
   customer, cycle, margin, and design life where the filing permits.
2. Reconcile revenue to shipments, receivables, inventory, supplier commitments,
   customer prepayments, and cash collection.
3. Separate software renewal, price, contract duration, cancellations, and RPO
   from acquired revenue and acquisition-related amortization.
4. Treat stock compensation as an owner claim by comparing it with repurchases,
   diluted shares, and cash per share.
5. Track acquisition cash, integration cost, debt, interest, and the return on
   acquired customer relationships. A non-cash amortization add-back does not
   remove the original purchase price.
6. Review product-transition and inventory-reserve charges. A charge can be
   unusual in amount while the risk remains a recurring part of the business.
7. Read the proxy for incentives tied to revenue, adjusted EBITDA, AI growth,
   free cash flow, acquisitions, EPS, debt reduction, and share count. Incentives
   that reward acquired growth or adjusted profit can obscure owner returns.

## Lyn Alden lens: customer liquidity and semiconductor cycles

Broadcom benefits from infrastructure spending and critical system dependence,
but customers can delay projects, change architecture, negotiate price, or
develop internal alternatives when liquidity tightens. Foundry and packaging
constraints can limit supply in a strong cycle, while excess inventory and
purchase commitments can hurt cash when the cycle turns.

Software support can stabilize the company, but infrastructure-software
businesses introduce debt, renewal, pricing, and integration risk. The resilience
test is whether cash remains adequate for research, support, interest, debt
reduction, and customer retention if AI growth slows and semiconductor orders
normalize.

## Damodaran lens: value the components separately

The narrative combines high-growth AI silicon, mature semiconductor products,
software renewals, and acquisition skill. Model them separately using design
wins, customer concentration, units, pricing, gross margin, foundry and
packaging commitments, software renewal, RPO, support cost, acquisition cash,
debt, interest, R&D, stock compensation, diluted shares, and normalized owner
cash through a semiconductor downcycle.

The valuation should reward a durable software relationship only if it survives
pricing and retention tests. It should reward AI growth only if shipped products
produce cash after concentration, working capital, and supplier obligations.

## Cohort comparison

| Business type | Cleaner economic feature | Main uncertainty |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Design-led chip supplier | Customer design-in with low owned-factory capital | Concentration, foundry dependence, and product transitions |
| Integrated chip manufacturer | Control of design, process, and supply | Factories, yield, utilization, inventory, and capex |
| Infrastructure-software owner | Renewal and support revenue from installed workloads | Retention, acquisition cost, debt, and product investment |
| Semiconductor-equipment supplier | Sells tools needed by many chip manufacturers | Customer capex, export controls, and cycle timing |

The cleaner economics may sit with a design-led supplier or software owner, but
the highest control point is not automatically the best investment. The price
must leave room for concentration, reinvestment, acquisition, and cycle risk.

## Filing-based falsifiers

The conclusion should be revised if AI revenue depends on one customer or
program, design wins fail to become volume shipments, software pricing causes
migration, receivables or inventory rise faster than revenue, supplier
commitments create losses, acquisition cash remains necessary to maintain growth,
stock compensation absorbs cash per share, debt limits flexibility, or adjusted
profit rises while cash after required reinvestment falls.

The next filings should show silicon and software mix, customer concentration,
shipments, gross margin, inventory, supplier commitments, software renewal,
RPO, acquisition cash, debt, stock compensation, diluted shares, and owner cash.

## The valuation consequence

The market can value Broadcom as if current AI growth, software recurrence, and acquisition skill will all persist. That is a compound assumption. A sound valuation separates:

- a mature semiconductor cash stream;
- a high-growth AI design cycle;
- a software renewal stream;
- debt and interest obligations;
- acquisition replacement needs; and
- dilution and required research investment.

The key future evidence is not another large revenue number by itself. It is whether growth remains profitable after customer concentration, working capital, manufacturing commitments, software retention, interest, acquisitions, and dilution are included.
