# Utilities: rate base, merchant power, nuclear scarcity, and the large-load customer

## The first principle

Electricity demand becomes an investment asset only after someone commits to build, finance, operate, and recover the infrastructure. A data center or industrial load is not automatically utility value. It must become:

`large-load demand -> interconnection / contract / rate structure -> generation and grid investment -> reliability and service -> rate recovery, PPA cash, or merchant margin -> debt and capital returns`

The utility cohort differs from equipment makers because the control point can be regulated, contracted, or market-based. That control point determines who pays for the buildout and who bears the risk if load arrives late or never materializes.

## Five utility control points

### 1. Regulated rate base: NextEra and regional utilities such as Alliant

NextEra’s FPL business controls a regulated customer franchise, reliability obligation, and rate-base growth mechanism. Alliant’s packet shows how smaller regional utilities are using customer-specific structures, advance ratemaking, and contracted large-load demand to support a multi-year capital plan without treating every data center as an unprotected merchant bet.

The advantage is visibility: approved investment can earn an allowed return if the regulator accepts the prudence, need, and affordability. The burden is political and capital-intensive. Rate cases, customer affordability, storm costs, construction, debt, and load forecasts all matter. A rate base is not free cash; it is a claim that must be financed and recovered over time.

NextEra’s 2025/2026 evidence makes the distinction clear: FPL is the cleaner regulated engine, while NEER carries development, tax-credit, merchant, project, and capital-market risk. Consolidated operating cash cannot be valued without separating rate-base funding, independent power investments, nuclear-fuel purchases, debt, equity, tax-credit proceeds, and asset sales.

Alliant makes the same principle visible at regional scale. Its FY2025 filing
shows $1.169B of operating cash flow against approximately $2.483B of
construction and other productive-asset spending. The resulting negative
$1.314B diagnostic residual is not automatically destruction of value: it is a
bet that approved rate-base additions will earn a return above funding cost and
be recovered without breaking affordability. The future filing proof is
earned return and customer-funded recovery, not positive near-term FCF.

### 2. Scarce clean baseload: Constellation

Constellation controls a large nuclear and clean-generation fleet whose value rises when reliable, carbon-free power is scarce. Long-dated PPAs with commercial customers and data centers can convert that scarcity into contracted cash flow. License extensions, plant restarts, and site-specific interconnection create a hard-to-replicate asset base.

The burden is regulatory approval, outage and operating risk, fuel and maintenance, plant life-extension capital, customer concentration, and the possibility that contracted prices or policy assumptions change. Nuclear scarcity is valuable, but the shareholder owns a complex regulated and operational liability system, not simply a fixed stream of megawatt-hours.

### 3. Merchant fleet and hedging: Vistra

Vistra controls dispatchable generation, retail load, hedging, and market position rather than a classic rate base. Its value rises when capacity, reliability, and power prices tighten. Nuclear PPAs with AWS and Meta and the Helix relationship show how merchant assets can become more contracted and infrastructure-like.

The burden is volatility. Hedge marks can dominate GAAP earnings even when they settle later. Availability, fuel, heat rates, weather, transmission constraints, retail customer behavior, and acquisition integration all matter. Adjusted EBITDA and FCF before growth can be useful, but they must be reconciled to hedge settlements, maintenance, debt, and capital returns.

Vistra's filed FY2025 numbers make the distinction concrete: $4.070B of
operating cash flow less $2.752B of capital expenditures (including nuclear
fuel and long-term service prepayments) and $1.140B of Lotus acquisition cash
left approximately $178M before financing. Dividends and repurchases totaled
about $1.336B. The merchant model may still be attractive, but its durability
must be proven through normalized cash and balance-sheet resilience rather than
management's adjusted FCF label alone.

### 4. Customer-backed generation and retail stack: NRG

NRG’s Bring Your Own Power framing represents a different control point: large-load customers help support the generation investment, while NRG combines retail, supply, generation, demand response, and smart-home relationships. The model attempts to align new power infrastructure with the customer creating the load rather than socializing all costs across the rate base.

The burden is execution and acquisition. NRG’s LS Power transaction materially changed the platform, adding generation and demand-response capacity. The model must prove that customer contracts, retail margins, generation cash, integration, and leverage produce durable cash after the acquisition—not merely a larger EBITDA figure.

The filed FY2025 baseline is deliberately less dramatic: $1.913B of operating
cash flow less $1.147B of PP&E capex left $766M before the LS Power platform,
while dividends and repurchases totaled approximately $1.722B. That is the
right starting point for judging whether customer-backed growth is genuinely
self-funding after integration and debt.

### 5. Water rate base: American Water Works

American Water shows that the regulated-infrastructure model does not require
electricity scarcity. Water and wastewater systems need continuous renewal,
treatment, resilience, and compliance capital. In 2025, American Water generated
$2.059B of operating cash flow against $3.126B of capital expenditures. The gap
is not automatically a failure; it is the amount that must be financed and
recovered through rates, customer contributions, acquisitions, and future
operating cash.

The proof is stricter than connection growth. The investor must track what
entered rate base, what return was allowed and earned, what customers actually
paid, which capital was funded by customers, and whether acquired systems
produced cash after repair, quality, and integration costs. Average bill
affordability can support the regulatory compact while still hiding local
arrears or pressure from cumulative rate increases.

## Who pays for the AI load?

### 3. Large regulated scale: Duke Energy

Duke shows the same rate-base mechanism at a much larger multi-state scale. Its
2025 operating cash flow of $12.330B was below $14.024B of capital expenditures,
so current common-owner cash was negative after the build. The investment case
depends on regulatory recovery, earned returns, and customer-funded load growth,
not on current free cash flow alone. Its data-center service agreements and $103B
capital plan are evidence of potential demand, but they remain claims until load
is energized, billed, collected, and recovered without excessive affordability or
credit damage.

The utility cohort offers five answers:

- Ratepayers fund regulated rate base through approved tariffs.
- Corporate customers fund contracted generation through PPAs, energy-service agreements, or customer-specific structures.
- Merchant markets fund capacity through prices, hedges, and scarcity rents.
- Equipment and contractors fund construction upfront and recover through product margins, project payments, or service relationships.

The social and investment question is whether the growth customer pays enough to cover incremental generation, transmission, reliability, and stranded-capital risk. If not, the utility may earn growth while customers absorb affordability pressure or shareholders absorb under-recovery.

## The forensic bridge

Every utility should be tested through:

`load forecast -> signed commitment -> interconnection / regulatory approval -> capex -> energized capacity -> billable rate / PPA / merchant margin -> cash -> debt, maintenance, and capital returns`

Company-specific weak links:

- NextEra: FPL rate-case recovery versus NEER project and financing burden.
- Alliant: contracted large-load realization, affordability, advance ratemaking, and capital-plan execution.
- Duke: rate-base conversion, regulatory lag, large-load credit support, capital funding, and customer affordability.
- Constellation: nuclear availability, license renewal, outage/capex, PPA economics, and merger integration.
- Vistra: hedge marks versus settled cash, fleet availability, merchant prices, retail weather, and acquisitions.
- NRG: customer-backed generation, LS Power integration, leverage, retail margin, and project completion.
- American Water: rate-case recovery, water-quality capital, customer affordability, acquisition integration, receivables, and debt-funded renewal.

The main category errors are treating contracted demand as energized load, rate-base growth as free cash, hedge marks as current earnings, adjusted FCF as unrestricted cash, or a large-load agreement as proof that all required grid investment will earn an acceptable return.

## Lyn Alden lens

Utilities sit at the intersection of inflation, rates, energy security, industrial policy, and local affordability. The demand for reliable power can be secular while the financing of that power is cyclical. Higher rates increase the cost of debt and reduce the present value of long-lived projects. Inflation raises replacement cost and household bills. Regulatory protection improves visibility but not necessarily real returns if political pressure constrains recovery.

The most durable control points combine customer necessity, regulatory or contractual protection, and disciplined construction. Merchant platforms can earn higher upside during scarcity but require stronger risk management. Customer-backed projects may be the cleanest growth if the customer truly funds the incremental burden.

## Damodaran lens

Value utilities by economic engine:

- Regulated rate base: allowed ROE, capital plan, customer growth, regulatory lag, financing, and affordability.
- Nuclear/contracted generation: availability, contract duration, fuel and maintenance, relicensing, outage, and counterparty quality.
- Merchant power: power and capacity prices, hedge profile, heat rates, fleet availability, fuel, and required capital.
- Customer-backed generation: contract structure, project IRR, customer credit, construction, and stranded-capital protection.

The narrative only becomes value when a megawatt of demand becomes a financed, energized, and remunerated asset. A backlog of load is an option; cash arrives after permits, interconnection, construction, commissioning, and billing.

## Valuation: rate base and large load must earn the financing burden

Value each utility engine separately. Regulated rate base depends on the
allowed return, the portion of capital approved for recovery, regulatory lag,
construction timing, financing cost, and customer affordability. Contracted or
nuclear generation depends on availability, contract price, fuel and outage
costs, counterparty credit, relicensing, and replacement capital. Merchant
power depends on settled prices, hedge results, fleet availability, fuel, and
the capital needed to remain reliable.

Large-load demand should enter the model only after the customer commitment,
interconnection, construction, energization, billing, and recovery structure
are clear. Use normal, delayed-load, cost-overrun, and lower-price cases. The
valuation is fragile if it assumes rapid load growth, full rate recovery, low
financing cost, and no affordability or stranded-capital response at the same
time. The relevant output is owner cash per share after debt, maintenance,
construction, reliability, and regulatory requirements—not announced megawatts.

## Cohort conclusion

Regulated utilities may own the cleanest relationship but carry political and financing burdens. Nuclear owners control scarce clean reliability but carry operating and regulatory risk. Merchant generators capture scarcity but bear price and hedge volatility. Customer-backed platforms may align cost and demand best but depend on contract execution and acquisition discipline.

The cleanest utility economics belong to the company that makes the new load pay for the infrastructure it requires, preserves reliability, and earns a return without shifting excessive cost to ratepayers or common shareholders. Future filings should prove energized load, collected cash, capital recovery, debt capacity, and the economics of the customer-specific contract—not merely announce another gigawatt of demand.
