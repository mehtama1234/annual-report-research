# Who controls the bottleneck—and who funds it?

## The cross-sector first principle

Every durable business sits inside a system that solves a customer problem. The company may own a scarce resource, workflow, standard, network, brand, regulated permission, or installed base. But control is only half the investment question.

The other half is burden:

`customer problem -> control point -> revenue -> required renewal / risk capital -> burden carrier -> common-owner cash`

The best businesses control a difficult-to-replace relationship while requiring relatively little incremental capital to preserve it. The most misleading businesses control something valuable but must continuously fund inventory, labor, acquisitions, debt, regulation, claims, or capacity just to remain in place.

## The control-point map

### Physical scarcity and infrastructure

Eaton controls electrical distribution and specification. Vertiv controls integrated power-and-cooling deployment. Cummins and Generac control generation and resilience equipment. Caterpillar controls an installed machine, dealer, finance, parts, and service network. Equinix and Digital Realty control powered capacity and interconnection. EMCOR, Quanta, MasTec, Comfort Systems, and Sterling control scarce field execution.

These businesses benefit from electrification, AI load growth, grid constraints, and infrastructure policy. Their durability comes from qualification, location, uptime, labor scarcity, dealer density, or project execution. Their burden is physical: factories, land, power, inventory, receivables, labor, warranty, debt, and construction capital.

The cohort lesson is that the closest company to the demand headline is not necessarily the cleanest owner. Eaton may control the electrical interface, but its valuation and acquisition burden are extreme. Caterpillar has a deeper installed base, but Cat Financial and dealer inventory add cyclicality. Contractors may capture labor scarcity while carrying project-estimate and working-capital risk.

### Resource, corridor, and conversion control

ExxonMobil and ConocoPhillips control reserves and production capability. Cheniere controls LNG conversion and export infrastructure. ONEOK and Energy Transfer control integrated corridors. Nucor controls steel capacity, scrap access, and downstream production. These companies monetize energy security, infrastructure scarcity, industrial demand, and geopolitical redundancy.

Their burden is renewal. Reserves deplete; pipelines and terminals require expansion; steel mills require modernization; contracts and affiliates create claims; commodity prices and spreads fluctuate. “Fee-based” reduces some risk but does not eliminate utilization, leverage, affiliate, or capital requirements.

### Workflow and relationship control

Veralto, Ecolab, Motorola, F5, S&P Global, McDonald’s, Wingstop, Henry Schein, Aon, and Gallagher control embedded workflows, standards, distribution, or customer occasions. Their assets may be less visible than a plant or mine, but the relationship can be more durable because switching creates operational, reputational, or financial cost.

The burden is displaced rather than absent: field labor, producer compensation, franchisee health, technology support, acquisitions, cyber, customer retention, and working capital. A workflow moat is economically strong only if the relationship produces cash after the people and systems required to keep it embedded.

### Capital and risk intermediation

CME, BlackRock, T. Rowe Price, Ares, Blackstone, KKR, Chubb, and the insurance-broker platforms control access to liquidity, capital, risk transfer, or institutional trust. These can be among the highest-return businesses because the client or policyholder often supplies the capital.

But the denominator must be precise. Client AUM is not company cash. Performance income is not a perpetual fee. Collateral is not surplus liquidity. Insurance float carries claims and regulatory capital. Private-capital platforms carry partner, NCI, realization, valuation, and statutory-capital claims. Brokerage growth may require enormous acquisition spending.

## The hidden-burden taxonomy

Across sectors, recurring risks fall into six categories:

1. **Renewal capital** — reserve replacement, data-center development, steel modernization, manufacturing capacity, restaurant openings, and equipment fleets.
2. **Working capital** — receivables, inventory, unbilled revenue, contract assets, payables, and dealer or franchisee financing.
3. **Human execution** — technicians, producers, restaurant workers, construction crews, salesforces, and specialized support labor.
4. **Liability and quality** — warranty, emissions, product recalls, cyber incidents, clinical quality, claims, reserve development, and regulatory capital.
5. **Acquisition dependence** — goodwill, purchase accounting, integration, earnouts, amortization, dilution, and the need to buy future growth.
6. **Financial claims** — debt, NCI, preferred capital, partners, franchisees, policyholders, affiliates, and restricted customer collateral.

The forensic framework asks which category is growing faster than the revenue narrative. That is where apparent earnings quality begins to weaken.

## The universal cash-conversion test

The same sequence applies to a generator, an LNG train, a surgical robot, a restaurant, an asset manager, or a data center:

`demand -> committed order / adoption -> delivery or utilization -> billing -> collection -> renewal investment and risk provision -> distributable owner cash`

The accounting label changes by sector, but the economic test does not. Backlog, AUM, system sales, premiums, revenue, adjusted EBITDA, and operating cash are intermediate observations. They become investment evidence only after the company funds the assets and liabilities needed to preserve the relationship.

## Recent worked cases: the same question in different businesses

| Company | Control point | Burden funded by the company | Evidence that must be followed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| PepsiCo | Branded food and beverage choice and distribution | Ingredients, packaging, plants, labor, retailer support, inventory, acquisitions, and debt | Volume, price/mix, working capital, capital spending, acquisition returns, and cash after normal investment |
| American Express | Card-member and merchant relationship | Rewards, receivables, credit losses, funding, liquidity, and regulatory capital | Billed-business conversion, activation, retention, rewards liability, write-offs, and required capital |
| Eaton | Electrical distribution and protection interface | Factories, inventory, unbilled receivables, capacity, acquisitions, debt, and separation costs | Backlog margin, billing and collection, capacity utilization, acquisition cash, and per-share returns |
| ServiceNow | Accepted workflow record and routing system | Cloud service, support, R&D, deferred commissions, acquisitions, stock compensation, and dilution | Renewal, module expansion, AI contract value, subscription margin, and cash per diluted share |
| Intel | Integrated design and advanced manufacturing platform | Fabs, equipment, yield, labor, inventory, process R&D, commitments, and financing | Qualified external-foundry volume, utilization, capex, commitments, dilution, and cash after the next process generation |

These cases show why control and burden must be analyzed together. PepsiCo can
control product choice while retailers and consumers negotiate the economic
terms. American Express controls a payment relationship but carries the
financial asset and future credit claims. Eaton has valuable backlog but funds
capacity and acquisitions before orders become owner cash. ServiceNow controls a
workflow with less physical capital but still has dilution and cloud-service
cost. Intel may have strategic manufacturing importance while shareholders fund
the longest and most uncertain path to cash.

The comparison is not a ranking. It is a way to ask the same five questions in
plain language: what is controlled, what must be funded, when cash arrives, who
absorbs a shock, and what price already assumes success.

## How the three analytical lenses fit together

### Lyn Alden: durability inside liquidity and real-economy cycles

The macro question is whether the demand is non-discretionary, supply-constrained, inflation-linked, or dependent on abundant credit. Energy, infrastructure, healthcare, and essential access can be durable; restaurants, construction, semiconductors, asset management, and data-center development remain sensitive to household, corporate, or financial liquidity.

Durability is not immunity. Higher rates can delay projects, reduce asset values, raise financing costs, pressure customers, and expose leverage. Inflation can strengthen nominal revenue while raising replacement capital and labor costs.

### Damodaran: narrative to numbers through the lifecycle

The narrative must become explicit drivers: volume, price, retention, utilization, fee rate, margin, reinvestment, terminal growth, and cost of capital. Each cohort requires a different lifecycle model:

- Resource companies: depletion and reserve replacement
- Infrastructure: maintenance versus growth capital and utilization
- Workflow companies: retention, pricing, labor, and support productivity
- Franchise systems: franchisee return and system expansion
- Asset managers: fee-paying AUM, fee rates, and performance separation
- Insurers: underwriting, float, claims, and capital requirements
- Technology: R&D, SBC, customer retention, obsolescence, and capacity

The valuation mistake is to assign a high-quality multiple to a business whose future cash depends on unproven reinvestment or on capital supplied by someone else.

### Forensic framework: look for category errors

The most important “shenanigans” screen is often category discipline rather than an accusation of misconduct. Ask whether the analysis is confusing:

- backlog with collected cash;
- system sales with parent revenue;
- AUM with fee-paying AUM;
- adjusted EBITDA with owner cash;
- acquisition-adjusted EPS with organic growth;
- collateral or policyholder float with excess liquidity;
- non-cash SBC with costless compensation;
- one-time restructuring or quality costs with truly nonrecurring items.

Repeated “one-time” charges, rising working capital, aggressive buybacks, and acquisition-heavy growth are the recurring patterns that deserve deeper filing review.

## The hierarchy of clean economics

There is no universal ranking, but a useful heuristic is:

1. A trusted, specified, or regulated workflow with recurring use and low renewal capital.
2. A dense network or installed base with high switching costs and measurable service cash.
3. A scarce physical interface with pricing power but meaningful capacity investment.
4. A franchised or asset-light platform whose cash depends on the health of another operator.
5. A commodity or project business where scarcity is real but renewal capital and cycle exposure consume the return.

Valuation can reverse this order. A cleaner business at 80x normalized owner cash can be less attractive than a more cyclical business at 15x mid-cycle cash.

## What future filings should prove

The archive’s watchlists should converge on five evidence demands:

1. **Conversion:** Does backlog, AUM, system sales, or revenue become collected cash?
2. **Renewal:** What capital is required to preserve or grow the control point?
3. **Burden:** Who pays for labor, inventory, claims, quality, regulation, and debt?
4. **Incentives:** Are managers rewarded for organic cash returns, or for scale, adjusted EPS, AUM, backlog, and acquisitions?
5. **Price:** What operating outcome is already embedded in the market value?

The best future evidence is not another record quarter. It is stable cash conversion through a weaker demand period, funded reinvestment, lower leverage, controlled liabilities, and capital returns that remain covered without financial engineering.

## Final synthesis

Cross-sector investing is the study of control and burden. Every company wants to describe the control point; the annual report reveals the burden. The research system is strongest when it connects those two facts and refuses to capitalize one without the other.

The durable compounder is not simply the company with the largest market, highest margin, or strongest narrative. It is the company that controls a valuable relationship, renews that control at attractive returns, absorbs shocks without transferring them to common shareholders, and trades at a price that leaves room for the thesis to be merely good rather than perfect.
