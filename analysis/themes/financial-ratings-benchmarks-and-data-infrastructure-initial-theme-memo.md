## Theme

- Name: Ratings, benchmarks, and data-workflow earnings are being driven by embedded decision infrastructure and high-value recurring information
- Type: financial / data / ratings

## Core claim

S&P Global shows that another major part of the Financial sector is really a decision-infrastructure business. In this model, earnings depend on recurring ratings revenue, benchmark and index licensing, commodity pricing and information, market-intelligence subscriptions, workflow software, and analytics embedded in how capital gets raised, benchmarked, and monitored. The current `2025` annual and `2026` trailing-quarter materials suggest the business is still delivering strong operating leverage while becoming even more concentrated around its highest-value information and ratings franchises after the Mobility separation. The broader pattern is that S&P monetizes benchmark dependence, regulatory embedment, and information authority. That makes trusted scoring, indexation, and pricing data look less like support functions and more like economic infrastructure.

## Why this matters

- The Financial sector sample now spans seven distinct systems: scale banking, premium closed-loop payments, insurance underwriting plus float, active asset management, custody / servicing, exchange infrastructure, and ratings / benchmark / data infrastructure.
- S&P Global adds another hidden-finance operating system, but one centered on information authority rather than asset servicing or trade execution.
- This evidence set makes ratings, benchmarks, and proprietary information products visible as first-order financial-sector earnings drivers.
- It also sharpens the broader cultural and institutional read in the repo: more of the economy runs through trusted scoring, benchmark, pricing, and workflow systems than through direct asset ownership alone.

## Force-level read

- `money-gets-unbundled`:
  S&P is another clear case where value accrues outside the balance sheet owner. It captures economics from the standards, scores, indices, and data products that organize capital markets rather than from directly holding the financial assets moving through them.

- `the-money-movers`:
  debt issuance, passive-product benchmark usage, commodity-pricing references, and market-intelligence workflows all require trusted data and classification layers. S&P monetizes those coordination points across both issuance and ongoing portfolio management.

- `the-compliance-tax`:
  ratings processes, benchmark governance, and regulated-data workflows become harder to replace as capital-market participants face rising documentation, transparency, and audit expectations. Compliance and trust reinforce the incumbent information franchises.

## Secondary themes already emerging

- Recurring information revenue is economically powerful.
- Ratings and index positions create durable pricing power when they are embedded in market structure.
- Workflow software and analytics reinforce the data moat.
- Portfolio simplification can increase the concentration and clarity of a high-margin information franchise.
- Benchmark dependence and information authority increasingly look like infrastructure assets, not just branded content or analytics features.

## Counter-signals

- Ratings and issuance-linked businesses remain exposed to capital-markets cycles.
- Benchmark and data franchises still face regulatory, competition, and trust-related pressure.
- The post-Mobility shape of the company could change segment growth mix and perceived cyclicality.
- The current archive shows strong information-franchise economics, but it does not yet prove how durable growth remains if issuance, index-linked asset growth, or enterprise workflow budgets soften together.
