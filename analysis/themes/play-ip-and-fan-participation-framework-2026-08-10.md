# Play, IP, and Fan Participation Framework

Date baseline: 2026-08-10

## Purpose

- This memo connects Hasbro's new play-and-IP packet to adjacent archive names that monetize culture, participation, and identity through different control points.
- Core question: when consumers spend on belonging, fandom, ritual, or taste, which layer captures the cleanest economics?

## Companies compared

- franchise and world controllers: Hasbro, Inc.; Mattel, Inc.; Spin Master Corp.; JAKKS Pacific, Inc.; Walt Disney Co.; Take-Two Interactive Software Inc.; Electronic Arts Inc.
- participation platforms, tooling, marketplaces, demand infrastructure, and ritual retail: Roblox Corporation; Unity Software Inc.; AppLovin Corporation; Playtika Holding Corp.; Funko, Inc.; Build-A-Bear Workshop, Inc.; The Trade Desk, Inc.; Etsy, Inc.

## Shared demand signal

- All fifteen companies point to the same top-level social reality through the `2025` annual window and the latest reported quarters now in scope:
  - consumers still spend on meaning, not only utility
  - rituals, fandoms, occasions, and identity still command wallet share
  - recurring participation matters more than one-off transactions
- The companies differ in what they control:
  - Hasbro controls game systems, toy brands, and licensing-rich play worlds
  - Mattel controls toy brands, character systems, vehicle franchises, and an expanding bridge into digital games and family entertainment
  - Spin Master controls toy brands, preschool franchises, content delivery, and a digital-games layer spanning `Toca Boca`, `Piknik`, and related open-ended play systems
  - JAKKS controls licensed-product execution, costumes, and retail expression layers around major entertainment franchises, but with less owned-world control than the larger toy peers
  - Disney controls story universes, streaming surfaces, sports attention, parks, and merchandise flywheels
  - Take-Two controls high-value game worlds, sports franchises, and mobile monetization loops across Rockstar, 2K, and Zynga
  - Electronic Arts controls sports-franchise ecosystems, live-service loops, and annualized participation systems across EA SPORTS FC, Madden, College Football, Apex, and Battlefield
- Roblox controls the creation, discovery, communication, and economy layer for user-generated play worlds
- Unity controls creator tooling, deployment workflows, and a monetization stack that helps other companies build and grow interactive worlds without owning the same user economy as Roblox
- AppLovin controls ad-targeting, auction, and growth-optimization infrastructure that routes consumer attention and advertiser spend across mobile and adjacent digital ecosystems
- Playtika controls a habitual mobile live-ops and direct-billing layer that monetizes repeat participation through player conversion, limited-time offers, and portfolio-level optimization
- Funko controls a collector-expression layer that turns licensed fandom into displayable, giftable, repeat-purchase physical artifacts with its own recognizable aesthetic system
- Build-A-Bear controls a ritualized in-store and gift-driven participation layer that turns personalization and occasion-making into branded physical experiences
- The Trade Desk controls an open-internet demand, identity, and measurement layer that helps advertisers route attention and spending across fragmented media environments
- Etsy controls discovery, matching, and trust inside a marketplace for individualized and occasion-driven goods

## Where the economics diverge

### Controlled IP systems

- Hasbro, Mattel, Spin Master, JAKKS, Disney, Take-Two, and Electronic Arts monetize:
  - owned worlds
  - characters, brands, and narratives
  - repeat engagement loops
  - licensing
  - cross-format expansion
- Their strongest recurring pattern is that a fan relationship can be reopened again and again:
  - new sets
  - new releases
  - new formats
  - new channels
  - new partner products

Within that controlled-IP layer, there is an important split:

- Disney is the broader lifestyle-and-entertainment empire where the same attachment can be monetized through streaming, sports, parks, theatrical releases, and products
- Hasbro is the more compact play-and-fandom system where the same attachment is monetized through tabletop, digital licensing, toys, consumer products, and partner-driven extensions
- Mattel is the broader physical-toy-and-family-entertainment system still in migration, where brands like Barbie, Hot Wheels, UNO, and Masters of the Universe are increasingly being pushed across toys, film, mobile games, and collector demand loops, but with more visible physical-goods friction than Hasbro's strongest profit engines
- Spin Master is the three-creative-centre system where toys, entertainment, and digital games are already formal operating pillars, but where the economics still swing sharply with retailer timing, tariffs, markdowns, content amortization, and trade-policy pressure
- JAKKS is the licensed-product and costume-expression system where participation is monetized mainly through partner properties, seasonal demand, and retailer execution, with much less owned-canon leverage and therefore much more obvious dependence on tariffs, royalty obligations, and sourcing discipline
- Take-Two is the digital-world control model where the attachment is monetized through recurrent consumer spending inside owned game universes, annualized sports ecosystems, and mobile habit loops, but with much greater concentration around tentpole release timing than Disney, Hasbro, or Mattel
- Electronic Arts is the sports-and-live-services control model where the attachment is monetized through repeat competition, seasonal resets, roster-building, and ongoing content inside annualized digital ecosystems, with less single-world concentration than Take-Two but heavy dependence on sports-franchise and Ultimate Team style loops

### Marketplace participation

- Roblox monetizes:
  - user-generated world participation
  - creator economics
  - discovery and recommendation
  - communication and social connection
  - repeat virtual spending inside a platform economy
- Roblox proves that a company does not need to own a fixed character canon to own a world.
- It can also own the infrastructure layer where other people build the worlds.
- Unity monetizes:
  - the software tools used to build worlds
  - deployment and workflow infrastructure
  - monetization and user-acquisition tooling
  - AI-enabled optimization around growth and creation
- Unity proves that a company can control a meaningful part of the participation economy without owning either the canon or the end-user community.
- It can instead own a large part of the production and monetization stack beneath many outside worlds.
- AppLovin monetizes:
  - recommendation and auction infrastructure
  - advertiser demand routing
  - user-acquisition optimization
  - monetization intelligence across publisher inventory
- AppLovin proves that a company can control a major economic choke point in participation systems without owning either the worlds or the creation tools.
- It can instead own the algorithmic marketplace that decides which offers, apps, and experiences get efficiently distributed and monetized.
- Playtika monetizes:
  - repeat mobile engagement loops
  - payer conversion
  - direct-to-consumer billing
  - live-ops optimization
  - licensed-IP overlays inside habitual game systems
- Playtika proves that a company can own an economically meaningful slice of the participation economy without controlling the largest prestige worlds or the ad-market infrastructure.
- It can instead own the repeat-spend habit layer where engagement, offers, and payment optimization get tuned quarter after quarter.
- Funko monetizes:
  - collector identity
  - shelf-display fandom
  - gifting and affordable collectibles
  - licensing breadth across many entertainment universes
  - repeat product refresh and treasure-hunt retail behavior
- Funko proves that a company can create a meaningful control point in the participation economy without owning the worlds, the platform rails, or the creation stack.
- It can instead own a recognizable aesthetic and merchandising system that lets fans carry fragments of many worlds home with them.
- Build-A-Bear monetizes:
  - personalized physical creation
  - gifting and milestone occasions
  - in-store ceremony and memory formation
  - pop-culture licensing with direct guest participation
  - asset-light partner-operated and franchise expansion around the same ritual
- Build-A-Bear proves that a company can create a meaningful control point in the participation economy without owning the deepest canon or the broadest toy shelf.
- It can instead own the branded ceremony through which a consumer helps make the object emotionally meaningful.
- The Trade Desk monetizes:
  - media-buying decisioning
  - privacy-aware identity and measurement
  - advertiser demand routing across fragmented channels
  - `CTV`, retail-data, and supply-path access
  - AI-enhanced optimization across the open internet
- The Trade Desk proves that a company can capture a meaningful share of the participation economy without owning the world, the marketplace, or the app itself.
- It can instead own the commercial attention infrastructure that helps determine which experiences, products, and messages get efficiently surfaced and measured.
- Etsy monetizes:
  - discovery
  - trust
  - individualized taste
  - gifting and ritual occasions
  - buyer-seller matching
- Etsy proves that cultural spending does not require owned franchises to be durable.
- But the profit logic is different:
  - Etsy earns from facilitating meaning-rich commerce
  - Disney, Hasbro, Mattel, Spin Master, Take-Two, and Electronic Arts earn more directly from owning the worlds people want to revisit
  - JAKKS earns more from merchandise and costume expression around worlds that are usually controlled by other companies
  - Unity earns from supplying the development and monetization infrastructure used by many world owners and creators
  - Playtika earns from operating habitual mobile-spend loops with tighter direct billing and live-ops control than the larger console-first publishers
  - Funko earns from a collector-heavy expression layer where licensed IP gets converted into highly repeatable, display-oriented physical artifacts
  - Build-A-Bear earns from branded ritual retail where customization, gifting, and experiential participation increase the emotional value of the product
  - The Trade Desk earns from providing the buying, identity, measurement, and optimization rails that help advertisers navigate fragmented media and shift budgets toward open-internet channels
  - Roblox earns from operating the rails on which many user-generated worlds compete, socialize, and monetize

## The main conclusion

- The common pattern is that consumers repeatedly pay for entry into systems of meaning.
- The important difference is who owns the system.
- Disney, Hasbro, Mattel, Spin Master, Take-Two, and Electronic Arts own the worlds.
- JAKKS usually does not own the deepest world layer.
- Roblox owns the participation infrastructure across many worlds.
- Unity owns a creator-and-growth tooling layer beneath many worlds.
- AppLovin owns a monetization-and-demand-routing layer across many worlds.
- Playtika owns a habitual live-ops and direct-billing participation layer inside mobile games.
- Funko owns a collector-expression and shelf-presence layer across many worlds.
- Build-A-Bear owns a ritualized occasion and personalized physical-participation layer.
- The Trade Desk owns an open-internet demand and measurement layer across many worlds and channels.
- Etsy owns the interface and discovery layer around many sellers' worlds.
- Put differently:
  - Hasbro monetizes play participation through franchise control
  - Mattel monetizes play participation through brand-system expansion from shelves into screens and entertainment formats
  - Spin Master monetizes play participation through a more explicit toy-entertainment-digital stack spanning preschool franchises, content delivery, subscriptions, and open-ended digital play
  - JAKKS monetizes play participation through licensed-product execution, costumes, and consumer-goods expression around franchises it does not fully control
  - Disney monetizes fan participation through universe control
  - Take-Two monetizes play participation through direct ownership of game worlds, sports loops, and recurrent digital spending systems
  - Electronic Arts monetizes play participation through sports-franchise control, live services, and annualized competitive ecosystems
  - Roblox monetizes play participation through platform control over creation, discovery, communication, and virtual spending
  - Unity monetizes play participation through creator tooling, deployment infrastructure, ad and monetization software, and AI-enhanced growth systems
  - AppLovin monetizes play and consumer participation through ad auctions, recommendation systems, measurement, and AI-enhanced user-acquisition and monetization infrastructure
  - Playtika monetizes play participation through habit loops, payer conversion, direct billing, and live-service offer design inside mobile portfolios
  - Funko monetizes fan participation through licensed collectibles, shelf display, gifting, and repeat physical artifact purchases
  - Build-A-Bear monetizes fan and family participation through in-store creation, gifting, memory-making, and customized branded keepsakes
  - The Trade Desk monetizes consumer participation indirectly through advertiser decisioning, media-buying control, identity resolution, and cross-channel measurement across the open internet
  - Etsy monetizes ritual and taste participation through marketplace control

## Cultural and societal read-through

- Hasbro adds a crucial signal the archive needed:
  - adult fandom and hobbyist identity are no longer side categories
  - they are core economic engines
- Mattel adds the adjacent mass-market signal:
  - mainstream toy demand is also being reorganized around repeatable brands, collector behavior, theatrical reinforcement, digital play, and cross-age participation
  - not every franchise benefits equally, which makes the category split between Hot Wheels and games versus Barbie and Fisher-Price analytically important
- Spin Master adds the hybrid-family-entertainment signal:
  - children’s franchises now need to travel through shelves, movies, streaming-style content delivery, subscriptions, and in-game engagement to stay culturally relevant
  - the same company may own strong repeatable brands and still show volatile reported results because tariff policy, retailer order timing, markdowns, and acquisition integration can disrupt the physical layer
- JAKKS adds the licensed-expression signal:
  - not every company in the participation economy owns the world itself
  - some monetize the costume, role-play, collectible, and occasion layer around stronger outside franchises
  - that model can still work, but it is much more visibly exposed to tariffs, retailer caution, China sourcing, and royalty commitments
- Roblox adds the digital-native participation signal:
  - younger and older users increasingly want to enter, revisit, and help shape the worlds themselves rather than only consume finished content
  - creator labor, identity, communication, moderation, and recommendation systems are now part of the consumer product
- Unity adds the creator-infrastructure signal:
  - a growing share of the participation economy is controlled before the user ever arrives
  - the tools used to build, ship, optimize, and monetize interactive worlds are becoming strategic bottlenecks in their own right
  - AI is increasingly part of that infrastructure layer rather than a separate adjacent category
- AppLovin adds the demand-routing signal:
  - another growing share of the participation economy is controlled at the point where attention gets allocated and converted
  - algorithmic recommendation, ad auctions, and performance measurement are becoming economic toll points underneath digital participation
  - consumer culture is increasingly shaped not just by the world people enter, but by the optimization systems that decide what gets surfaced and scaled
- Playtika adds the habitual-spend signal:
  - not all participation economics depend on giant worlds, creator ecosystems, or ad exchanges
  - a large share of digital participation is monetized through repeated low-ticket purchases, live-ops cadence, and billing optimization inside routines that become part of users' daily behavior
  - licensed IP can be used not only to build whole universes, but also to refresh and strengthen already-optimized monetization loops
- Funko adds the collector-expression signal:
  - a large share of cultural participation happens through visible ownership, gifting, shelf display, and affordable collecting rather than through gameplay or media access alone
  - adult fandom is not just a media-consumption pattern, but a recurring consumer-goods engine
  - the company shows that aesthetic system control can matter even when the deepest canon belongs to someone else
- Build-A-Bear adds the ritual-retail signal:
  - a large share of physical participation demand is not about finished products, but about ceremonies consumers help complete themselves
  - personalization, gifting, birthdays, and memory-making can be more economically important than toy utility alone
  - the company shows that branded retail experiences can function as fandom, occasion, and family-participation systems at the same time
- The Trade Desk adds the fragmented-attention signal:
  - a growing share of modern consumer culture is mediated by infrastructure that consumers never see directly
  - the shift from linear television and broad-reach media toward streaming, commerce, mobile, and cross-device fragmentation increases the value of software that can reconstruct audiences and prove outcomes
  - the company shows that participation economics are no longer only about owning worlds or marketplaces, but also about owning the measurement and demand-routing layer beneath them
- Take-Two adds the owned-digital-worlds signal:
  - consumers increasingly pay not just for launch access, but for repeated participation inside sports ecosystems, catalog worlds, and mobile loops
  - recurrent consumer spending is the most useful common metric because it makes game-world economics comparable to broader fandom and participation systems
- Electronic Arts adds the repeat-competition signal:
  - digital sports and shooter communities are not just game audiences, but recurring social and spending systems
  - live services and annualized sports cycles can support a major participation economy even without a single dominant open world
- Disney reinforces the idea that fandom now stretches across digital, physical, and destination formats.
- Etsy shows that the same desire for meaning can appear outside formal franchises in gifts, weddings, milestones, home expression, and personalization.
- Together they suggest that modern consumer culture is not just shifting toward experiences.
- It is shifting toward repeatable participation in worlds, rituals, and identity systems.

  - licensing timing
  - physical-product seasonality
- repeated Mattel pressures:
  - tariffs
  - inflation
  - higher royalties
  - foreign-exchange pressure
  - advertising intensity
  - cost of building digital and entertainment capability on top of a global physical-products base
- repeated Spin Master pressures:
  - tariff volatility and refund timing
  - retailer order pull-forwards and slowdowns
  - markdown and promotional support
  - inventory and warehouse optimization demands
  - content amortization and digital-asset impairment risk
  - Melissa & Doug integration and trade-policy sensitivity
- repeated JAKKS pressures:
  - China sourcing concentration
  - tariff shocks and refund timing
  - seasonal working-capital swings
  - retailer order reductions and timing distortions
  - minimum royalty guarantees
  - theatrical and franchise-release dependence
- repeated Roblox pressures:
  - trust and safety expense
  - age-check and policy friction
  - discovery-algorithm tradeoffs between retention and monetization
  - creator payout economics
  - AI and infrastructure intensity
  - deferred-revenue accounting that can obscure near-term platform demand shifts
- repeated Unity pressures:
  - platform-policy dependence
  - ad-market and publisher volatility
  - debt-maturity and capital-structure pressure
  - portfolio-reset and restructuring noise
  - AI investment demands
  - customer migration risk during product and monetization transitions
- repeated AppLovin pressures:
  - mobile-gaming concentration
  - dependence on Apple, Google, and Meta ecosystem rules
  - pressure to prove e-commerce expansion is durable
  - heavy reliance on Axon AI model quality and continued recommendation superiority
  - share-repurchase intensity versus balance-sheet flexibility
  - regulatory scrutiny around privacy, ad targeting, consumer protection, and AI
- repeated Playtika pressures:
  - title concentration
  - dependence on Apple and Google platform rules outside its direct-to-consumer channel
  - contingent-consideration volatility and cash obligations tied to SuperPlay
  - large debt balances and revolver-maturity discipline
  - live-ops dependence on sustained payer conversion
  - litigation and regulatory pressure around social-casino-style monetization and in-game offer practices
- repeated Funko pressures:
  - tariffs and sourcing concentration across Vietnam, Cambodia, China, and Mexico
  - royalty minimums and audit exposure
  - debt covenants and limited capital-structure flexibility
  - retailer restocking caution and channel volatility
  - product-mix risk between Core Collectibles, Loungefly, and emerging brands
  - consumer-discretionary softness that can hit collectible spending even when fandom remains culturally active
- repeated Build-A-Bear pressures:
  - tariffs, freight, and sourcing exposure across China and Vietnam
  - mall and store traffic sensitivity despite stronger brand affinity
  - occupancy and lease deleverage when direct-to-consumer demand softens
  - channel-mix volatility between stores, e-commerce, commercial, and franchising
  - dependence on licensing freshness, gifting occasions, and seasonal relevance
  - pressure to expand asset-light formats without weakening the core in-store ritual
- repeated The Trade Desk pressures:
  - ad-market and macro sensitivity
  - pressure to prove `CTV`, retail-data, and open-internet share gains are durable
  - dependence on inventory access, data partners, and identity cooperation across the ecosystem
  - rising hosting and AI-infrastructure cost intensity
  - closed-platform and regulatory pressure around privacy, data use, and market structure
  - reputational risk if execution stumbles weaken the claim that superior software can keep offsetting fragmentation
- repeated Disney pressures:
  - content cost
  - portfolio complexity
  - advertising cyclicality
  - capital intensity in physical destinations
- repeated Take-Two pressures:
  - launch concentration around major tentpoles, especially GTA VI
  - mobile user-acquisition efficiency and mature-title lapping risk
  - higher marketing and R&D intensity around release clusters
  - the need to balance recurrent consumer spending durability against slate volatility
- repeated Electronic Arts pressures:
  - franchise concentration, especially around EA SPORTS FC and Ultimate Team style economics
  - licensing leverage and annualized sports-content expectations
  - deferred-revenue mechanics that can blur the simple launch-versus-service read
  - large transaction, governance, and capital-structure disruptions, as shown by the `2025-2026` merger process
- repeated Etsy pressures:
  - trust and fraud
  - discovery quality
  - cross-border logistics
  - tariff and de minimis exposure
- The broader pattern is that cultural demand may be durable, but the monetization layer still depends on operational control and infrastructure discipline.

## Implication for the archive

- Hasbro opens a genuinely useful new frontier because it proves that franchise/IP monetization is not only a media-company story.
- It also belongs in consumer-goods and play systems.
- Mattel strengthens that frontier by showing a second route through the same terrain:
  - a company can have the right brands and still show much messier economics while it tries to convert toy demand into a fuller family-entertainment and digital-games flywheel
- Spin Master sharpens the frontier further:
  - it shows a company already structurally organized around toys, entertainment, and digital games, but still exposed to the hard operational physics of manufacturing, trade policy, retail timing, and impairment risk
  - that makes it a better bridge between physical franchise owners and digital participation platforms than either Hasbro or Mattel alone
- JAKKS extends the frontier to the outer licensed ring:
  - it shows how much value can still be captured in the participation economy without owning the canon itself
  - it also shows how quickly that value becomes operationally fragile when tariffs, retailer caution, royalty obligations, and sourcing concentration intensify
- Roblox stretches the frontier further:
  - the archive can now compare controlled-IP play systems with an open-ended participation platform where the company owns the economy, discovery, and safety layer instead of the character canon
- Unity extends the frontier downward into tooling:
  - the archive can now separate owning the user economy from owning the creation and monetization stack underneath the economy
  - that matters because Unity's control point is less socially visible than Roblox's, but potentially broader across the total universe of creators
- AppLovin extends the frontier sideways into monetization infrastructure:
  - the archive can now separate owning the creation stack from owning the demand-allocation and auction layer
  - that matters because AppLovin's control point is even less consumer-visible, yet it may capture a large share of economics when consumer attention gets converted into measurable spend
- Playtika extends the frontier back toward the player wallet:
  - the archive can now separate ad-routing infrastructure from the direct habit-and-billing layer where repeated player spending actually gets harvested
  - that matters because Playtika's control point is less about owning the biggest cultural universe and more about owning the routine monetization loop
- Funko extends the frontier into collector shelves and gifting behavior:
  - the archive can now separate licensed costume execution from a more collector-heavy, design-system-driven fandom artifact model
  - that matters because Funko's control point is not ownership of canon, but repeated conversion of cultural moments into affordable physical tokens of identity
- Take-Two sharpens the comparison:
  - the archive can now distinguish between owning the participation rails and owning the worlds themselves
  - that matters because Take-Two's economics are cleaner than physical toy systems but more concentrated than Roblox's platform model
- Electronic Arts sharpens it further:
  - the archive can now compare two different owned-digital-participation models inside games
  - Take-Two is more dependent on prestige worlds and tentpole events, while EA is more dependent on annualized sports loops and service recurrence
- That matters for the broader archive because similar patterns should appear elsewhere:
  - toys versus fandom platforms
  - character brands versus open marketplaces
  - owned universes versus distribution interfaces
  - collectible communities versus generalized retail
