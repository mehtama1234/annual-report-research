# CEMEX Packet

Date baseline: 2026-08-10

## Snapshot

- Company: Cemex, S.A.B. de C.V.
- Sector: Basic Materials
- Industry: General Building Materials
- Ticker: `CX`
- Fiscal year-end: December
- Target annual report year: 2025
- Target quarter window:
  - quarter 1: Q2 2026
  - quarter 2: Q1 2026
  - quarter 3: Q4 2025

## Why this name matters

- CEMEX is one of the clearest global cement-and-urbanization names for reading how physical infrastructure, housing demand, industrial policy, carbon regulation, and logistics pressure interact in one operating system.
- It also fits the infrastructure layer behind software and AI better than a simple cement label suggests. The quarter chain ties demand not just to roads and buildings, but to social housing, rural-road programs, IIJA projects, AI-related industrial construction, and the operating cost base needed to move heavy material through local networks.
- This makes CEMEX a useful comparison against `CRH plc`, `Martin Marietta Materials, Inc.`, and `Vulcan Materials Company`. It is more internationally mixed, more exposed to public-program housing and urbanization in Mexico, and more explicit about carbon-policy pass-through in Europe.

## Annual report takeaways

- The audited `2025` statements show a business that was still grinding through cyclical and portfolio noise while protecting cash flow and operating discipline. Revenue was `$16.132B`, controlling-interest net income was `$960M`, operating earnings before other expenses were `$1.789B`, and full-year operating cash flow after interest and income taxes was `$1.975B`.
- The annual statements also show why the year cannot be read as a clean demand boom. Gross profit fell to `$5.311B` from `$5.408B`, operating earnings fell to `$1.005B` from `$1.822B`, and the company recorded `2025` impairment losses of `$538M`.
- The key structural annual signal is cost and balance-sheet management. Cash and cash equivalents rose to `$1.822B` at December 31, 2025, total current assets reached `$6.097B`, and contractual debt and lease obligations totaled about `$7.163B`.
- The most important annual risk signal is goodwill concentration in the United States. The audited report says goodwill was `$7.170B` at December 31, 2025, including `$5.894B` tied to the U.S. cash-generating-unit group, and the company recognized a `$307M` goodwill impairment related to that U.S. group during 2025.
- The annual read also shows active portfolio reshaping rather than passive cycle exposure. The statements discuss the sale of Panama and the Dominican Republic operations and a larger stake in Couch Aggregates in the southeastern United States, showing that CEMEX is reallocating capital around operating density and return quality.

## Quarter-by-quarter takeaways

### Most recent quarter: Q2 2026

- Second-quarter `2026` sales were `$4.593B`, operating EBITDA was `$1.018B`, operating EBITDA margin was `22.2%`, and controlling-interest net income was `$347M`.
- The quality signal was structural cost improvement plus targeted pricing rather than raw volume alone. Management said the quarter benefited from Project Cutting Edge efficiencies, overhead savings, pricing discipline, operating leverage as Mexico volumes recovered, and a favorable energy environment.
- Mexico was the strongest thematic region. Management said self-construction and government-backed programs such as Rural Roads and housing continued to support bagged cement demand, while residential demand drove bulk cement volumes. The company said the social housing program continued to scale and was a meaningful lever of growth in the quarter.
- The U.S. read was more mixed. Management described demand as resilient and led mainly by infrastructure from the ongoing rollout of IIJA projects, while industrial demand related to the AI environment continued to grow. But weather disruptions in Texas and parts of the Mid-South, rising materials and freight costs, and residential affordability constraints weighed on EBITDA and margin.
- Europe reinforced the carbon-policy theme. Management said the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism and the gradual reduction of free EU ETS allowances were supportive of higher prices, while Middle East and Africa results stayed strong despite regional tensions.

### Quarter minus 1: Q1 2026

- First-quarter `2026` sales were `$4.019B`, operating EBITDA was `$794M`, operating EBITDA margin was `19.8%`, and controlling-interest net income was `$228M`.
- The first-quarter message was that a leaner cost base was beginning to show up as structural margin expansion. Management said a large part of the margin gain was sustainable and driven by improved operating efficiency, a leaner cost base, disciplined pricing, and operating leverage.
- Mexico again mattered more than simple macro commentary would imply. Management said public spending on social programs such as Rural Road projects and the government housing program drove demand and that, for the first time in six quarters, year-over-year cement volumes turned positive.
- The U.S. pattern stayed consistent: infrastructure and some industrial demand were firm, but residential stayed soft. Management also said ready-mix volumes had their first year-over-year increase since mid-2022 and aggregates benefited from the consolidation of Couch Aggregates and other investments coming online.
- Europe in the first quarter showed how regulation and pricing are becoming linked. The company said cement prices in Europe increased sequentially, supported by the implementation of the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism and tighter free CO2 allowances, with additional price increases and fuel surcharges used to offset energy inflation.

### Quarter minus 2: Q4 2025

- Fourth-quarter `2025` sales were `$4.180B`, operating EBITDA was `$781M`, operating EBITDA margin was `18.7%`, and controlling-interest net income was a loss of `($356M)` because of goodwill impairment and asset write-downs.
- The stronger underlying signal was not the GAAP loss but the second-half margin recovery. Management said cost efficiencies from Project Cutting Edge began to materialize, all regions reported relatively flat to improved EBITDA margin in `2025`, and fourth-quarter EBITDA increased `9%` on a like-to-like basis.
- Mexico showed the clearest demand inflection. Management said public spending on social programs and infrastructure was beginning to gain momentum, Rural Road projects were showing early signs of increased activity, and the federal government had established a goal of building `1.8M` houses during the administration.
- The United States already displayed the same barbell that carried into 2026: infrastructure strength and industrial bright spots offset by continued softness in residential. Management also noted competitive pressure in select cement markets while aggregates volumes rose with Couch Aggregates and other investments.
- EMEA added another durable pattern. Management said infrastructure projects in Eastern Europe and sustained housing activity and infrastructure investment in Spain supported volume growth, while carbon-regulation changes should support cement pricing into `2026` and beyond.

## Consumer, cultural, and societal interpretation

- CEMEX is not a consumer brand in the usual sense, but it sits directly under mass social needs: housing affordability, road access, urbanization, local employment, climate adaptation, and the reliability of public works. When those needs move, CEMEX’s volumes and pricing power move with them.
- Mexico is the clearest societal story in the packet. The recurring references to Rural Roads, social programs, self-construction, and government housing show a business tied to the informal and semi-formal mechanics of how households and local governments actually expand shelter and basic infrastructure.
- The company also reflects a broader cultural shift in infrastructure politics. Demand is not just coming from abstract GDP growth. It is increasingly tied to visible state-backed buildout, repair backlogs, national industrial policy, and politically legible construction programs.
- In the United States, the contrast is sharper: AI and industrial expansion can boost heavy-material demand even while households remain constrained by affordability and higher housing inventories. That split matters because it shows how physical AI demand is monetized through older industrial supply chains rather than only through software and chips.
- Europe adds a different social layer: decarbonization policy is no longer just compliance overhead. It is becoming part of market structure, pricing power, and competitive position.

## Industrial and operating pressures

- Pricing still has to outrun cost inflation. Across the quarter chain, management keeps pointing to fuel surcharges, higher cement pricing, and mix-adjusted aggregates pricing as necessary responses to freight, energy, and input-cost pressure.
- Project Cutting Edge is central to the current margin story. The quarter materials repeatedly attribute better gross margin and operating expense ratios to savings from that program, which means future execution risk sits partly in how repeatable those savings are.
- The U.S. business is still fighting a two-speed market. Infrastructure and industrial categories are stronger, but residential remains pressured by affordability constraints and elevated housing inventories, while freight and weather can still hit margins quickly.
- Europe faces a different pressure stack: weather variability, energy inflation, and geopolitics are real, but regulation can also improve pricing conditions through carbon-cost pass-through and tighter supply economics.
- Balance-sheet and portfolio pressure remain present in the annual read. Debt, lease commitments, pension obligations, carbon-allowance commitments, and the size of U.S. goodwill all mean CEMEX is still a highly managed industrial platform rather than a low-maintenance compounder.

## Repeated higher-order patterns

- `Infrastructure demand is no longer a single-cycle story.` CEMEX keeps showing separate demand engines in social housing, public roads, U.S. federal infrastructure, AI-linked industrial buildout, and selected European projects.
- `Operating leverage now depends as much on cost-system design as on volume.` Project Cutting Edge is a reminder that margin recovery in heavy industry can come from labor, process, energy, and overhead redesign, not only from a stronger macro cycle.
- `Residential weakness does not kill the materials cycle if public and industrial demand stay firm.` The U.S. business demonstrates that pattern repeatedly across `Q4 2025`, `Q1 2026`, and `Q2 2026`.
- `Carbon policy is becoming part of pricing architecture.` Europe is a direct example of how regulation can tighten economics and help support prices rather than just adding cost.
- `The physical layer behind AI monetizes through old-economy channels.` CEMEX’s references to AI-related industrial demand show that heavy materials still sit underneath modern compute expansion, even if investors usually focus elsewhere.

## Best comparison uses

- Against `CRH plc`: CRH is the broader North American infrastructure allocator; CEMEX is the more internationally mixed cement, aggregates, and urbanization operator with heavier Mexico and carbon-policy sensitivity.
- Against `Vulcan Materials Company`: Vulcan is the cleaner U.S. aggregates and local-scarcity story; CEMEX adds global cement exposure, social-program demand, and a more complex portfolio and policy mix.
- Against `Martin Marietta Materials, Inc.`: Martin is the more focused reserve-and-pricing read; CEMEX is the better cross-border housing, infrastructure, and regulatory comparison.
- Against `Knife River Corporation`: Knife River is a more domestic public-works and vertically integrated construction-materials read; CEMEX is the larger international platform with a stronger cement and policy-driven pricing angle.
