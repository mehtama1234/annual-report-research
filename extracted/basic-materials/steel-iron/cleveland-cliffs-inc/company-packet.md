## Annual report takeaways

- Cleveland-Cliffs is the strongest steel-and-heavy-input addition for this batch because the `2025` annual chain shows a vertically integrated industrial conversion system rather than a simple commodity-price proxy. The business runs from iron ore mining, pellets, direct reduced iron, and scrap processing into primary steelmaking, downstream finishing, and automotive-focused value-added flat-rolled products.
- AnnualReports confirms the `Basic Materials` / `Steel & Iron` taxonomy and, unlike several other names in this batch, already showed the `2025 Annual Report and Form 10-K` as of `2026-08-10`. The authoritative annual chain still comes from the company-hosted `2025` annual filing materials and the SEC `10-K`.
- The `2025` annual materials show a company under real cyclical pressure. Cleveland-Cliffs reported full-year `2025` consolidated revenues of `$18.6 billion`, a GAAP net loss of `$1.4 billion`, an adjusted net loss of `$2.48` per diluted share, and adjusted EBITDA of only `$37 million`.
- The filings make clear that the core `2025` problem was not a collapse in physical scale but a combination of weak automotive production, lagging fixed annual contract pricing, and an expiring slab contract that management described as value-destructive in its final year.
- Even with those pressures, the annual chain still shows why the company matters as a flagship packet. Cleveland-Cliffs ended `2025` with `$3.3 billion` of liquidity, maintained a large North American manufacturing footprint, employed approximately `25,000` people in the United States and Canada, and preserved a system built around automotive steel, coated products, cold-rolled sheet, plate, stainless and electrical output, and downstream conversion capabilities.
- The business model matters for the archive because roughly `35%` to `40%` of flat-rolled steel shipments are sold under fixed-price contracts, while the rest is exposed to spot or variable and index-linked pricing. That means earnings do not move in a clean one-to-one line with headline steel prices; contract lag and product mix shape how quickly market changes flow into results.
- This packet also sharpens the difference between extraction businesses and heavy industrial conversion businesses. In gold or silver, the central questions are reserves, grades, and mine sequencing. In Cleveland-Cliffs, the central questions are utilization, energy and labor costs, contract structure, automotive demand, downstream value-add, and whether a high-fixed-cost asset base can recover margin.

## Quarter-by-quarter takeaways

### Most recent quarter

- Results: `Q2 2026` revenues were `$5.2 billion`, GAAP net loss was `$134 million` or `$0.25` per diluted share, adjusted net loss was `$115 million` or `$0.20` per diluted share, and adjusted EBITDA was `$286 million`.
- Cash flow and liquidity: operating cash flow was `$230 million`, and liquidity remained `$3.1 billion` as of `2026-06-30`.
- Operations: steel product sales volumes were `4.0 million` net tons. Mix was `45%` hot-rolled, `31%` coated, `15%` cold-rolled, `4%` plate, `4%` stainless and electrical, and `1%` other.
- End-market mix: steelmaking revenues were `$5.1 billion`, with about `33%` from distributors and converters, `29%` from direct automotive, `28%` from infrastructure and manufacturing, and `10%` from steel producers.
- Management message: the quarter showed sequential recovery from the weather-hit first quarter, with EBITDA up by `$191 million` and pricing plus operating normalization helping margins improve.
- Structural themes: `Q2 2026` shows Cleveland-Cliffs as a throughput-and-mix recovery story, not a clean commodity rally story. The important signal is that manufacturing demand, automotive volumes, and contract realization can lift profits even while the company still reports GAAP losses.

### Quarter minus 1

- Results: `Q1 2026` revenues were `$4.9 billion`, GAAP net loss was `$229 million` or `$0.42` per diluted share, and adjusted EBITDA was `$95 million`.
- Costs and disruption: management said adjusted EBITDA included an approximately `$80 million` one-time energy cost impact from extreme cold weather.
- Operations: steel shipments were `4.1 million` net tons, up `338,000` tons from the prior quarter. Mix was `44%` hot-rolled, `29%` coated, `15%` cold-rolled, `5%` plate, `3%` stainless and electrical, and `4%` other including slabs.
- End-market mix: steelmaking revenues were `$4.8 billion`, with about `31%` from distributors and converters, `29%` from infrastructure and manufacturing, `29%` from direct automotive, and `11%` from steel producers.
- Liquidity and outlook: liquidity was `$3.1 billion` at `2026-03-31`, and management maintained full-year `2026` guidance for `16.5` to `17.0 million` net tons of shipments, about `$700 million` of capex, about `$575 million` of SG&A, about `$1.1 billion` of depreciation, depletion, and amortization, and roughly `$125 million` of cash pension and OPEB payments.
- Structural themes: `Q1 2026` is a good reminder that asset-heavy industrial systems are exposed to real-world operating friction. Weather, energy costs, and plant-level execution still matter materially even before broader pricing and demand questions are resolved.

### Quarter minus 2

- Results: `Q4 2025` revenues were `$4.3 billion`, GAAP net loss was `$235 million` or `$0.44` per diluted share, adjusted net loss was `$0.43` per diluted share, and adjusted EBITDA loss was `$21 million`.
- Shipments and liquidity: fourth-quarter steel shipments were `3.8 million` net tons, and liquidity stood at `$3.3 billion`.
- Full-year context: the same release framed full-year `2025` revenues at `$18.6 billion`, GAAP net loss at `$1.4 billion`, adjusted net loss at `$2.48` per diluted share, and adjusted EBITDA at `$37 million`.
- Management message: management said the year was weighed down by persistently weak automotive production, lagging fixed annual contract pricing, and the final year of a slab contract that had become economically harmful. At the same time, it emphasized progress on higher-value products, multi-year automotive contracts, lower unit costs, reduced capex, and extended debt maturities.
- Structural themes: `Q4 2025` closes the annual story around contract friction and industrial dependence. Cleveland-Cliffs did not fail because it lacked volume; it struggled because a large integrated steel system can still be pinned down by poor contract carryover, weak customer production schedules, and legacy commitments.

## Signals to feed into higher-level analysis

- Sector: Cleveland-Cliffs adds the steel-and-iron conversion layer that neither the hydrocarbon names nor the precious-metals names cover. This is heavy-input exposure expressed through industrial throughput and manufacturing dependence.
- Commodity exposure: the company is tied to steel pricing, but the earnings path is filtered through fixed-price contracts, downstream processing, and end-market mix. That makes realized economics slower and more operationally mediated than in spot-heavy extraction names.
- Capital intensity: this is one of the clearest examples in the batch of a business where blast furnaces, finishing assets, tooling, tubing, DRI capacity, mining assets, and downstream conversion all create a very high fixed-cost base.
- Automotive dependence: Cleveland-Cliffs makes the North American auto system visible inside the materials lane. Weak vehicle production was a major drag in `2025`, which means the company becomes a useful bridge between heavy inputs and the broader mobility or manufacturing cycle.
- Cost and infrastructure dependence: the `Q1 2026` weather impact and annual commentary underline how energy, logistics, labor, and operating continuity can move results materially in an industrial asset base.
- Contract structure: fixed-price automotive and annual contracts can stabilize relationships but also create lag when market pricing changes quickly. That is a distinct risk from the direct commodity exposure seen in independent oil and gas or precious-metals mining.
- Vertical integration: iron ore, pellets, direct reduced iron, scrap processing, steelmaking, and downstream finishing give Cleveland-Cliffs more internal control than a simple reroller or service center, but they also make the system more complex and harder to shrink quickly in weak demand periods.
- Bigger-picture theme: this packet expands the archive from extraction into transformation. The heavy-input economy is not just mines and wells; it also includes hard-asset industrial systems that turn raw materials into strategically important manufacturing inputs under tight cost, demand, and contract constraints.
