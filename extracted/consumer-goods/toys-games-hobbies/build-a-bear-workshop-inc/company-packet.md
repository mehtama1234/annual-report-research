# Build-A-Bear Workshop, Inc. packet

Date baseline: `2026-08-10`

## Snapshot

- Build-A-Bear is not just a toy retailer. It is a branded, in-person participation system built around customization, commemoration, gifting, and emotional attachment.
- The key `2025-2026` story is a split between:
  - a still-strong brand platform that keeps widening into partner-operated stores, franchising, wholesale, collectibles, and adult gifting
  - a more fragile direct-to-consumer traffic picture that became visible in `Q1 2026`
- That makes Build-A-Bear a strong archive case for how a company can monetize play and fandom through ritualized physical participation rather than by owning the deepest entertainment canon or the broadest toy shelf.

Covered scale:

- full-year `2025` total revenues: `$529.8 million`
- full-year `2025` pre-tax income: `$67.2 million`
- `Q1 2026` total revenues: `$125.3 million`
- `Q3 2025` total revenues: `$122.7 million`

## Filing coverage

- `2025 10-K` filed `2026-04-16`
- `Q3 2025` earnings `8-K` and `EX-99.1` filed `2025-12-04`; `10-Q` filed `2025-12-11`
- `Q4 2025` earnings `8-K` and `EX-99.1` filed `2026-03-12`
- `Q1 2026` earnings `8-K` and `EX-99.1` filed `2026-05-28`; `10-Q` filed `2026-06-11`

## Annual takeaways from `2025`

### 1. Build-A-Bear is monetizing ceremony and memory, not just product

- The `2025 10-K` says guests create personalized furry friends through a stuffing, dressing, accessorizing, and naming process centered on the `Heart Ceremony`.
- The filing repeatedly frames the company as a `retailtainment` brand and a multi-generational phenomenon rather than a standard toy store.
- This matters because Build-A-Bear's core product is not a plush unit by itself.
- It is a purchased moment:
  - a visit
  - a ritual
  - a gift
  - a commemoration
  - a token of relationship or memory
- That gives it a distinctive place in the archive.
- It is one of the cleanest examples of a consumer-goods company whose moat includes a ceremony.

### 2. The addressable market has widened beyond children into fandom, gifting, and adult affinity

- The `10-K` says Build-A-Bear sits at the intersection of pop-culture trends and that its e-commerce sites over-index with teen and adult gifting and collectible consumers.
- It also points to:
  - the adult-focused `Bear Cave` microsite
  - sports, entertainment, art, and gaming licenses
  - pre-stuffed plush and non-plush categories
  - content through Build-A-Bear Entertainment
- In archive terms, this means the brand is no longer constrained to mall-based children's traffic.
- It is attempting to widen from child's play into broader occasion-driven and fandom-driven demand.

### 3. The business model is increasingly diversified and more asset-light than it first appears

- As of `2025` year-end, Build-A-Bear had `662` global locations:
  - `375` corporately-managed
  - `178` partner-operated
  - `109` franchise
- Net new unit growth in `2025` was `64` experience locations.
- The filing says the company operates through three reportable segments:
  - direct-to-consumer
  - commercial
  - international franchising
- That is a crucial structural point.
- The company is no longer only monetizing owned-store sales.
- It is increasingly monetizing the brand through:
  - wholesale
  - partner-operated experiential stores
  - royalties
  - franchise economics
  - entertainment and outbound licensing

### 4. Tariffs and sourcing pressure were major `2025` headwinds, but the company still held profit at record levels

- Full-year `2025` revenue was a record `$529.8 million`.
- Full-year pre-tax income was also a record `$67.2 million`, essentially flat versus `$67.1 million` in `2024`, despite about `$11 million` of tariff and related cost impact.
- The `10-K` says plush and clothing are sourced primarily from China and Vietnam.
- It also says production had shifted primarily to Vietnam in `2025`, while the company still remained meaningfully exposed to tariff volatility and international trade uncertainty.
- This is an important archive signal:
  - the brand and operating model were strong enough to offset serious cost pressure
  - but the physical-goods layer still imposed hard limits on how much of that demand could convert cleanly into margin

### 5. Capital allocation remained aggressive despite the operating friction

- The `10-K` says Build-A-Bear ended fiscal `2025` with about `$26.8 million` in cash, cash equivalents, and restricted cash and no outstanding borrowings on its credit facility.
- During fiscal `2025`, the company used:
  - `$27.5 million` for share repurchases
  - `$11.5 million` for dividends
- It also increased the quarterly dividend again in March `2026`.
- This matters because management is not behaving like a fragile turnaround team.
- It is behaving like a company that believes the evolved model can continue producing cash even while it navigates traffic pressure and tariffs.

## Quarter-by-quarter read

### `Q3 2025`

- Total revenues: `$122.7 million`, up `2.7%`
- Net retail sales: `$112.3 million`, up `2.5%`
- Consolidated e-commerce demand: down `10.8%`
- Commercial and international franchising revenues: `$10.4 million`, up `4.9%`
- Pre-tax income: `$10.7 million` versus `$13.1 million`
- Diluted EPS: `$0.62` versus `$0.73`
- EBITDA: `$14.2 million`

Additional management signals:

- management called out record third-quarter and first-nine-month revenue
- it emphasized more than `50` net new units year to date and entry into `seven` additional countries
- it reaffirmed annual guidance even while flagging meaningful tariff expense
- it said the elevated tariff impact would continue through `Q4 2025` and into the next fiscal year

Interpretation:

- `Q3 2025` showed that demand and expansion were still healthy, but tariff costs had started to bite more visibly.
- The quarter is important because it shows the business widening internationally and commercially even while the classic direct-to-consumer margin profile came under pressure.

### `Q4 2025`

- Total revenues: `$154.5 million`, up `2.7%`
- Full-year `2025` total revenues: `$529.8 million`
- Fourth-quarter pre-tax income: `$21.5 million` versus `$27.5 million`
- Full-year pre-tax income: `$67.2 million` versus `$67.1 million`
- Fourth-quarter diluted EPS: `$1.26` versus `$1.62`
- Full-year diluted EPS: `$3.99` versus `$3.80`
- Fiscal `2025` capital returned to shareholders: `$39.0 million`

Additional management signals:

- management described `2025` as another year of revenue growth across all three operating segments
- it highlighted more than `60` net new units across multiple geographies for the second straight year
- it increased the quarterly dividend to `$0.23` per share
- it introduced `2026` guidance calling for total revenue growth in the mid-single digits and pre-tax income ranging from a mid-single-digit decline to low single-digit growth, inclusive of tariff costs and longer-range investments

Interpretation:

- `Q4 2025` confirms that Build-A-Bear can still post record annual results even when quarter-level profit gets hit by tariff costs.
- The more important strategic signal is that growth was broad-based across direct-to-consumer, commercial, and franchising.
- That tells the archive this is no longer a one-channel mall specialty retailer story.

- Net retail sales: `$113.5 million`, down `5.1%`
- Consolidated e-commerce demand: down `26.1%`
- Commercial and international franchise revenues: `$11.8 million`, up `34.1%`

Liquidity and operating detail:

- cash and cash equivalents at quarter end: `$26.2 million`

- no borrowings under the revolver
- net new unit growth: `7` global experience locations
- total global locations: `669`
- first-quarter capital return: `$14.2 million`

Important qualification:

- the quarter included a `$7 million` benefit from an `IEEPA` tariff refund tied to prior fiscal-year costs
- the `10-Q` says total recorded tariff-related benefit in the quarter was about `$10.4 million`, with additional inventory-value benefit

Additional management signals:

- management said results were lower than expected because of weaker consumer traffic
- it lowered `2026` revenue guidance while still keeping the range above the prior year's record level
- it raised pre-tax outlook because of the tariff refund
- it emphasized Orlando expansion, global experience-location growth, and wholesale expansion

Interpretation:

- `Q1 2026` is the key stress-test quarter in this packet.
- It shows the split between:
  - a direct-to-consumer business seeing weaker traffic and much softer e-commerce demand
  - an adjacent-channel strategy that is still growing quickly
- It also shows why filing interpretation matters:
  - the reported profit improvement looks strong
  - but a significant part of it came from tariff-refund accounting rather than from a clean acceleration in underlying store demand

## Repeating operating signals

### Consumer behavior

- Build-A-Bear monetizes participation through a personalized act of creation.
- The purchase is part toy, part gift, part event, part memory marker.
- The products function as:
  - customized keepsakes
  - birthday and party artifacts
  - fandom and pop-culture tokens
  - romantic and friendship gifts
  - family leisure experiences
- The brand is therefore less about toy utility and more about emotionally coded ownership.

### Cultural and societal read-through

- Build-A-Bear is one of the clearest archive examples of a physical retail concept turning into a ritual system.
- The filing language is explicit about:
  - meaningful moments
  - emotional connection
  - personalization
  - DIY participation
  - multi-generational appeal
- This matters because it shows how modern consumer culture keeps shifting from passive consumption toward participatory and identity-laden formats.
- Build-A-Bear is not selling a finished character alone.
- It is selling a moment in which the consumer helps make the object meaningful.
- That makes it a strong contrast with `Funko`:
  - Funko monetizes fandom display after the cultural attachment exists
  - Build-A-Bear monetizes the act of making, gifting, and commemorating in real time

### Industrial and operating pressures

- repeated tariff pressure:
  - the company sources mainly from China and Vietnam
  - margin remains exposed to trade policy and shipping-cost swings
- repeated traffic pressure:
  - mall and store traffic softness can quickly hit direct-to-consumer performance
  - `Q1 2026` made that risk visible
- repeated channel-mix pressure:
  - e-commerce can weaken even when commercial and franchise channels grow
  - the business is becoming more diversified, but also more mix-sensitive
- repeated lease and occupancy pressure:
  - most stores are leased
  - occupancy deleverage mattered in `Q1 2026`
- repeated expansion-execution pressure:
  - the company is opening more partner-operated, franchise, and destination formats
  - scale helps, but store economics still require careful execution
- repeated supply-chain pressure:
  - inventory, freight, warehousing, and sourcing discipline remain critical in a business that depends on seasonal and licensed product turns

## Cross-company interpretation

- Compared with `Funko`, Build-A-Bear is more experiential, more occasion-driven, and more directly tied to in-person ritual.
- Compared with `Hasbro` or `Mattel`, it owns less of the underlying franchise canon but has a more distinctive branded participation ceremony at the point of sale.
- Compared with `JAKKS`, it is less about licensed-product execution at scale and more about branded memory-making and personalization.
- Compared with `Etsy`, it shares the meaning-rich and gift-oriented demand logic, but through a controlled retail experience rather than a decentralized marketplace.

That gives the archive another useful distinction:

- owned-world and play-system operators: `Hasbro`, `Mattel`, `Spin Master`
- licensed expression and shelf-based fandom: `JAKKS`, `Funko`
- ritualized physical participation and personalized occasion retail: `Build-A-Bear`
- marketplaces for personalized meaning-rich goods: `Etsy`

## Bottom line

- Build-A-Bear is a strong archive case for how physical retail can become a participation business rather than just a merchandise business.
- The most important signal is that the company monetizes ceremony, customization, gifting, and fandom through a branded experience that consumers actively help complete.
- The main tension is also clear:
  - the broader platform is diversifying into partner-operated, wholesale, and franchise growth
  - but the direct-to-consumer core can still get hit by traffic softness, tariff costs, and e-commerce weakness
- That combination makes the name especially valuable for the archive, because it shows how emotionally strong consumer demand still has to pass through a very physical operating system.
