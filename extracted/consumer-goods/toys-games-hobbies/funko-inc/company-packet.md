# Funko, Inc. packet

Date baseline: `2026-08-10`

## Snapshot

- Funko is a fandom-expression and collector-merchandise company built around licensed pop-culture properties, repeat shelf refresh, and a recognizable visual system rather than ownership of the underlying worlds.
- The key `2025-2026` story is a tension between two realities:
  - the business was hit hard by tariffs, retail caution, and debt constraints in `2025`
  - the company entered `2026` with improving `Core Collectibles` momentum, stronger international demand, and sharply better gross margins
- That makes Funko a strong archive case for how cultural demand can stay alive even while the physical-goods and balance-sheet layer remains stressed.

Covered scale:

- full-year `2025` net sales: `$908.2 million`
- full-year `2025` adjusted EBITDA: `$26.6 million`
- `Q1 2026` net sales: `$200.9 million`
- `Q2 2026` net sales: `$207.7 million`

## Filing coverage

- `2025 10-K` filed `2026-03-12`
- `Q4 2025` earnings `8-K` and `EX-99.1` filed `2026-03-12`
- `Q1 2026 10-Q` and earnings `8-K` filed `2026-05-07`
- `Q2 2026 10-Q` and earnings `8-K` filed `2026-08-06`

## Annual takeaways from `2025`

### 1. Funko is selling belonging through a collector aesthetic, not through world ownership

- The `2025 10-K` says Funko's business is built on the idea that everyone is a fan of something.
- It frames the company as a way for fans to display, wear, or carry affiliation with movies, television, video games, music, sports, and other cultural properties.
- The filing says Funko sits at the nexus of pop culture and the growing `kidult` segment.
- This matters because the company is not primarily monetizing utility or even children's toy cycles.
- It is monetizing identity display and repeat fandom participation in physical form.

### 2. The licensing engine is massive and central

- The `2025 10-K` says Funko had license agreements with over `250` content providers covering about `800` active licensed properties in `2025`.
- That scale is a core part of the model:
  - broad property access
  - rapid response to cultural moments
  - evergreen catalog support
  - the ability to spread risk across many fandoms
- But it also creates obligation pressure.
- The filing says Funko expected to incur `$128.2 million` in minimum guaranteed royalty payments, including:
  - `$40.6 million` for `2026`
  - `$49.9 million` for `2027`
  - `$37.7 million` for `2028`
- It also carried a `$29.6 million` royalty-audit accrual at `2025-12-31`.

### 3. Direct-to-consumer and international reach are strategic growth levers

- The `2025 10-K` says direct-to-consumer sales were about `24%` of sales in both `2025` and `2024`, up from `21%` in `2023`.
- The direct-to-consumer channel includes owned e-commerce sites in the U.S., Mexico, and Europe plus three flagship stores in the U.S.
- The filing also says non-U.S. sales represented about `40%` of `2025` sales, up from `35%` in `2024` and `31%` in `2023`.
- International expansion and direct relationships with fans are therefore not side projects.
- They are central to how Funko is trying to build more resilient economics around fandom demand.

### 4. Tariffs and retail caution were the dominant `2025` headwinds

- Full-year `2025` net sales fell `13.5%` to `$908.2 million`.
- The `10-K` says the decline came across all distribution channels because of tariff disruption and macro uncertainty.
- Cost of sales fell in absolute dollars but still absorbed higher duty and tariff costs.
- The filing says shipping, freight, duty and tariff costs increased by `$9.5 million`, while cost pressure also came through inventory reserves and a challenging retail environment.
- Management also says retailers slowed restocking, prioritized lower inventory levels, negotiated additional discounting, or canceled orders.

### 5. The balance sheet was still under strain even as operations began to stabilize

- `Q4 2025` debt stood at `$225.3 million`, up from `$182.8 million` a year earlier.
- The annual filing and later quarters show that the company needed a `Fifth Amendment` to its credit agreement in `February 2026`.
- That amendment:
  - extended maturity to `December 31, 2027`
  - waived or loosened several covenant tests across `2025-2027`
  - required mandatory prepayment with cash and cash equivalents above `$50 million`
  - maintained a minimum qualified cash covenant of `$10 million`
- So Funko's improvement story is real, but it is happening under lender supervision rather than from a position of full balance-sheet freedom.

## Quarter-by-quarter read

### `Q4 2025`

- Net sales: `$273.1 million` versus `$293.7 million`
- Gross profit: `$111.6 million`
- Gross margin: `40.9%`
- Adjusted net income: `$2.5 million`
- Adjusted EBITDA: `$23.3 million`
- Cash at `2025-12-31`: `$42.1 million`
- Total debt at `2025-12-31`: `$225.3 million`

Additional management signals:

- strong sales of entertainment properties including `KPop Demon Hunters` and `Stranger Things`
- continued strength in `Bitty Pop!`
- launch of `Pop! Yourself` in Europe
- inventory reduction and `$16 million` of debt paydown in the quarter

Interpretation:

- `Q4 2025` showed that the company could still win when it connected quickly to cultural moments and collector formats.
- But the annual result still looked weak because the full system remained burdened by tariffs, retail caution, and financing pressure.

### `Q1 2026`

- Net sales: `$200.9 million` versus `$190.7 million`
- Gross profit: `$88.8 million`
- Gross margin: `44.2%`
- Net loss from the `10-Q`: `$(18.1) million`
- Adjusted net loss: `$(6.3) million`
- Adjusted EBITDA: `$11.3 million`
- Cash at `2026-03-31`: `$34.3 million`
- Total debt at `2026-03-31`: `$215.9 million`

Additional operating detail:

- management said `Core Collectibles` increased `17%` year over year
- international sales were described as robust, particularly in Europe
- the quarter included non-recurring third-party debt amendment fees tied to the credit amendment
- the `10-Q` says the company had no remaining borrowing availability under the revolver

Interpretation:

- `Q1 2026` is important because it showed that Funko's demand problem was not a simple collapse in fan interest.
- The business could still generate stronger margins and positive adjusted EBITDA when the product mix and channel conditions improved.
- The weak point was financing flexibility, not lack of cultural relevance.

### `Q2 2026`

- Net sales: `$207.7 million`, up `7%`
- Gross profit: `$117.6 million`
- Gross margin: `56.6%`
- Net income: `$15.4 million`
- Adjusted net income: `$15.0 million`
- Adjusted EBITDA: `$40.9 million`
- Cash at `2026-06-30`: `$40.7 million`
- Total debt at `2026-06-30`: `$201.1 million`

Important qualification:

- `Q2 2026` included a pre-tax benefit of `$25.4 million` from expected tariff refunds and the release of accrued tariffs.
- The company also executed a participation sale of `$22.1 million` in tariff claims for `$19.2 million`, using half the proceeds to pay down term debt.

Additional management signals:

- `Core Collectibles` grew `9%`
- the company reiterated full-year net sales guidance of flat to up `3%`
- it raised adjusted EBITDA guidance to `$100 million-$110 million`, including the tariff-related benefit
- management said it would rationalize `Loungefly` SKU count and focus the assortment on stronger-demand, better-return products

Interpretation:

- `Q2 2026` was genuinely strong, but not clean.
- It proves that Funko can generate much better profitability when demand, mix, and tariff mechanics all line up.
- It does not prove that the business has fully escaped structural volatility.

## Repeating operating signals

### Consumer behavior

- Funko is selling tangible proof of affiliation.
- The products function as:
  - identity markers
  - gifting objects
  - collector completions
  - fandom souvenirs
  - social-display goods
- The filing language around `treasure hunt` shopping, limited-time products, shelf refresh, and repeat visits shows that this is a recurring participation system in physical retail form.
- Consumers are not simply buying a toy.
- They are buying a token of membership in a fandom.

### Cultural and societal read-through

- Funko is one of the clearest archive examples of how fandom has become an everyday consumer identity system.
- The `kidult` framing matters:
  - adult collectors are not a side market
  - they are a core economic constituency
- Unlike Disney or Take-Two, Funko usually does not own the underlying universe.
- Unlike JAKKS, it is less about costumes or broad seasonal licensed execution.
- Its core role is to turn cultural attachment into collectible, repeatable, affordable physical artifacts.
- That makes it a strong signal that modern consumer culture is not just about access to worlds.
- It is also about owning visible, displayable fragments of those worlds.

### Industrial and operating pressures

- repeated tariff pressure:
  - sourcing remains concentrated in Vietnam, Cambodia, China, and Mexico
  - tariffs affect demand, margins, and cash flow simultaneously
- repeated licensing pressure:
  - the company depends on outside content providers
  - minimum guarantees and royalty audits create fixed obligations
- repeated retail pressure:
  - mass and specialty retail partners still matter heavily even as direct-to-consumer grows
  - retailer restocking caution can hit the business quickly
- repeated capital-structure pressure:
  - debt amendments, covenant waivers, and cash-prepayment rules limit flexibility
- repeated product-mix pressure:
  - `Core Collectibles` is strengthening while `Loungefly` needs SKU discipline
  - this is a reminder that not all fandom-expression formats compound equally

## Cross-company interpretation

- Compared with `JAKKS`, Funko is more collector-heavy, more aesthetic-system driven, and more direct-to-consumer aware.
- Compared with `Hasbro` and `Mattel`, it has far less canon control and less play-system depth.
- Compared with `Etsy`, it is more licensed, more retailer-dependent, and more tied to formal entertainment IP.
- Compared with Disney, it captures only a thin slice of the world economics, but it can still participate broadly across many universes at once.

That gives the archive a useful additional distinction:

- owned-world operators: `Disney`, `Take-Two`, `Electronic Arts`
- broad play-and-brand systems: `Hasbro`, `Mattel`, `Spin Master`
- licensed expression and retail execution: `JAKKS`
- collector-expression and shelf-based fandom artifacts: `Funko`
- ritual-and-taste marketplaces: `Etsy`

## Bottom line

- Funko is a strong archive case for consumer participation that happens through collecting, gifting, display, and identity signaling rather than through direct ownership of the world itself.
- The most important signal is that the company still sits on top of real fandom demand.
- The main problem is not whether the product means something to consumers.
- It is whether Funko can convert that demand into durable economics despite tariffs, royalty obligations, retailer volatility, and a still-constrained capital structure.
