## What changed

- Cheniere is a cleaner next addition than another conventional pipeline or producer because it adds the liquefaction-and-export bottleneck layer. The company is not mainly a molecule owner; it is a system that converts U.S. gas into contracted and marketed global LNG flows.
- The annual and quarter materials all reinforce the same structure: Sabine Pass and Corpus Christi are increasingly a combined export machine, while Corpus Christi Stage 3 is still actively expanding capacity. This makes the packet more about project completion, cargo throughput, and global gas arbitrage than about domestic tariff rates alone.
- The quarter chain also shows why Cheniere cannot be read through GAAP net income alone. Derivative marks can swing reported net income sharply, including a large `Q1 2026` net loss, while management emphasizes Consolidated Adjusted EBITDA and Distributable Cash Flow as the steadier operating measures tied to LNG delivered and cash deployment.
- The AnnualReports nuance matters here. AnnualReports confirmed the company taxonomy and displayed a `2025` annual-report label, but the saved page still said reports were not currently hosted there. The official IR annual-reports page and the filed SEC `10-K` therefore serve as the authoritative annual chain.

## Open questions

- How durable is the current mix of LNG margin strength, optimization activity, and project-completion momentum once Corpus Christi Stage 3 is largely complete and derivative noise normalizes?
- Does Cheniere remain best understood as quasi-infrastructure with long-duration cash visibility, or does the increasing role of integrated production marketing and global portfolio optimization make the earnings model more merchant-sensitive than a typical pipeline packet?
- If the next brownfield FIDs at Sabine Pass and Corpus Christi move forward, how much incremental value comes from locked-in infrastructure scarcity versus continued dependence on global LNG trade policy, contracting appetite, and feedgas economics?
