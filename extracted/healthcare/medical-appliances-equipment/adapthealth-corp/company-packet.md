## Why this name matters

- AdaptHealth is one of the clearest direct healthcare-at-home middle-layer names available because it monetizes recurring equipment access, medical-supply replenishment, discharge follow-through, referral relationships, and payor routing rather than a single product breakthrough.
- It matters for CLI 8 because it extends healthcare coverage beyond drug wholesalers, office procurement, hospital products, and procedure-adjacent equipment into the literal post-acute and home-based supply chain where institutional demand still controls the flow.
- It also makes channel control visible in a different way: not through branch inventory alone, but through referral-source density, payor relationships, patient resupply habits, capitated contracts, and field execution in the home.

## Annual report takeaways

- AnnualReports was current to the `2025` package on Monday, `2026-08-10`, and classified AdaptHealth as `Healthcare / Medical Appliances & Equipment`, so the taxonomy anchor is clean for this packet.
- The `2025` annual filing says the company serviced approximately `4.3 million` patients annually in all `50` states through approximately `640` locations in `48` states as of Wednesday, `2025-12-31`.
- The annual and year-end materials show a business built around four reportable segments during `2025`: Sleep Health, Respiratory Health, Diabetes Health, and Wellness at Home.
- The operating logic is exactly right for CLI 8. Sleep Health covers `CPAP` and `BiLevel` supply and resupply. Respiratory Health covers oxygen and home mechanical ventilation. Wellness at Home covers post-acute equipment, supplies, and support in the home. Diabetes Health still existed during `2025`, but later became the business the company chose to sell.
- Full-year `2025` net revenue was `$3.2449B`, down `0.5%`, while organic revenue still increased `1.7%`. Full-year net loss attributable to AdaptHealth was `$70.8M` versus net income of `$90.4M` in `2024`, largely because the year included a non-cash goodwill impairment charge of `$128.0M`.
- Full-year `2025` adjusted EBITDA was `$616.7M`, down `10.5%`, but cash flow from operations improved to `$601.8M` from `$541.8M`, and debt reduction reached `$250M` for the year.
- The annual filing is especially useful because it shows the company as a literal home-based institutional extension of the healthcare system. The company depends on acute-care hospitals, sleep labs, pulmonologists, skilled nursing facilities, clinics, and public and private payors rather than on direct consumer merchandising alone.

## Quarter-by-quarter takeaways

### Most recent reported quarter as of 2026-08-10: Q2 2026

- Results: net revenue increased `12.7%` to `$740.3M`, and organic revenue growth reached `15.9%`, with growth across all continuing reportable segments.
- Profitability read: net loss attributable to AdaptHealth was `$145.3M` compared to net income of `$4.2M`, largely due to a `$144.2M` pre-tax goodwill write-down.
- Cash-flow read: adjusted EBITDA fell `3.2%` to `$132.0M`, while year-to-date cash flow from operations declined to `$239.0M` from `$257.5M`, and year-to-date free cash flow turned negative `$48.4M` versus positive `$73.3M`.
- Strategic read: after quarter-end the company agreed to sell Diabetes Health for `$235.0M` in cash, signed a joint venture combining its eCommerce asset with a sleep retailer plus home sleep testing capability, and expanded its digital layer to more than `512,000` registered `myAPP` users, up `56%` from year-end `2025`.
- Operating-pressure read: management said the West Coast capitated partnership reached full scale, but the complexity of the rollout and an unexpected manufacturer price increase pressured margins and forced a reset of full-year `2026` guidance.
- Forward read: revised continuing-operations guidance moved to net revenue of `$2.85B` to `$2.89B`, adjusted EBITDA of `$490M` to `$520M`, and free cash flow of `$80M` to `$120M`.

### Quarter minus 1: Q1 2026

- Results: net revenue increased `5.4%` to `$819.8M`, and organic revenue growth reached `9.1%`, with growth across each of the company’s then-four reportable segments.
- Profitability read: net loss attributable to AdaptHealth widened to `$16.0M` from a net loss of `$7.2M`, while adjusted EBITDA declined `5.3%` to `$121.2M`.
- Cash-flow read: cash flow from operations was `$93.7M`, slightly below `$95.5M`, and free cash flow was negative `$27.5M` versus roughly break-even in the prior-year quarter.
- Operating read: management described the quarter as the largest de novo expansion in the history of the home medical equipment industry, reflecting how much capacity and execution were tied to the new capitated rollout.
- Forward read: at that point management raised `2026` revenue guidance to `$3.45B` to `$3.52B` and maintained adjusted EBITDA guidance of `$680M` to `$730M` and free cash flow guidance of `$175M` to `$225M`, which makes the later `Q2` reset analytically important.

### Quarter minus 2: Q4 2025

- Results: full-year `2025` net revenue was `$3.2449B` compared to `$3.2610B`, a decline of `0.5%`, while fourth-quarter revenue was `$846.3M` compared to `$856.6M`, a decline of `1.2%`.
- Demand read: organic revenue growth still reached `1.7%` for both the full year and the fourth quarter, and management said the company set patient census records for Sleep Health, Respiratory Health, and Wellness at Home while also posting a patient-retention record in Diabetes Health.
- Profitability read: fourth-quarter net loss attributable to AdaptHealth was `$102.8M` versus net income of `$50.3M`, with the same quarter absorbing part of the `$128.0M` non-cash goodwill impairment charge.
- Cash-flow and balance-sheet read: full-year cash flow from operations improved to `$601.8M`, full-year free cash flow reached `$219.4M`, and debt reduction totaled `$250M` in `2025`.
- Strategic read: management framed `2025` as a transition year in which the company rebuilt its operating model, hired heavily to support a major new capitated contract, expanded digital self-service, and positioned the business for a larger `2026` growth step.

## Consumer, cultural, and societal interpretation

- AdaptHealth is not retail-consumer demand in the ordinary sense, but it is heavily shaped by a broader societal shift toward more care delivered outside the hospital and more chronic-condition management handled in the home.
- The company shows how aging populations, chronic disease prevalence, sleep-apnea diagnosis, respiratory illness burden, and pressure on inpatient capacity all create recurring demand for a middle layer that keeps patients supplied after discharge or between clinical encounters.
- It also captures a cultural shift toward self-service and digital care coordination. `myAPP` growth, online resupply, and home-sleep-testing adjacency show that even a regulated home-medical operator is becoming more digitally mediated and behavior-driven.
- The Diabetes sale is also culturally revealing: the company is narrowing itself around the categories where it believes its home-service platform and payer-channel position are strongest, rather than trying to hold every adjacent home-medical vertical.

## Industrial and operating pressures

- This is a healthcare replenishment and routing business, not a simple product seller. The key operating levers are referral-source access, payer reimbursement, field-service execution, equipment utilization, recurring consumable resupply, and patient retention.
- Capitated contracts can drive large growth bursts, but they also create onboarding complexity, staffing strain, and temporary margin compression when volume ramps faster than process maturity.
- Manufacturer pricing still matters. `Q2 2026` showed that an unexpected supplier price increase can hit margins even in a recurring healthcare-access model.
- Working capital and free cash flow matter more than surface growth. `Q1 2026` and `Q2 2026` both showed stronger top-line performance than free-cash-flow performance, which means the channel can grow faster than cash conversion if rollout intensity rises.
- Regulation and reimbursement are structural constraints. The annual filing repeatedly points back to Medicare, Medicaid, commercial payors, and healthcare-compliance risk, which makes policy and coding discipline part of the core economics.

## Repeated higher-order patterns

- `Home care is still institutional demand.` AdaptHealth shows that once a patient leaves the hospital, the spend does not become purely consumer. It remains routed through referral systems, payors, and recurrent clinical need.
- `Recurring supplies matter as much as installed equipment.` Sleep, respiratory, and home-medical categories all produce ongoing replenishment economics rather than one-time device revenue.
- `Channel ownership can be built through care coordination, not just inventory.` The moat here is referral density, payor positioning, and home-service execution as much as warehousing or branch count.
- `Growth can expose the cost of complexity.` The quarter chain shows a company that can generate strong organic growth and still disappoint on EBITDA and cash flow when a major capitated rollout and portfolio reset collide.
- `Healthcare middle layers are being reshaped by portfolio discipline.` The move to sell Diabetes Health shows management treating category focus itself as a strategic operating lever.

## Best comparison uses

- Against `Henry Schein, Inc.`: Henry Schein shows office-based practitioner procurement, while AdaptHealth shows the home-based fulfillment and recurrent resupply layer after the clinical encounter.
- Against `Cardinal Health, Inc.`, `McKesson Corporation`, and `Cencora`: those names show large institutional drug and provider throughput, while AdaptHealth shows a lower-dollar but deeply recurring home-medical continuation layer.
- Against `Option Care Health, Inc.`: Option Care shows clinician-supported specialty-therapy delivery outside the hospital, while AdaptHealth shows broader home-equipment and supply continuity across chronic conditions.
- Against `Baxter International Inc.` and `West Pharmaceutical Services, Inc.`: those names show therapy-enabling product and hospital-use dependence, while AdaptHealth shows where the recurring home-side service and replenishment loop takes over.

## Working interpretation

- AdaptHealth materially strengthens CLI 8 because it makes healthcare replenishment in the home visible as a real middle layer with payor dependence, recurring supply economics, field execution, and embedded referral channels.
- The bigger pattern is that the healthcare system increasingly outsources continuity, not just treatment. AdaptHealth sits in that continuity layer, where the real product is not a single device but the ongoing ability to keep a chronic patient supplied, serviced, reimbursed, and out of the hospital.
