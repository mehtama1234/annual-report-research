## Annual report takeaways

- Addus extends the healthcare-frontier archive into the everyday home-care layer that sits below specialty therapy and below hospital care, but above simple consumer wellness.
- The `2025` annual record matters because it frames home-based care as a lower-cost substitute for institutional care, a managed-care partner, and a consolidating local-service market rather than as a product or device story.
- This packet is especially useful because Addus operates across personal care, hospice, and home health, which makes it a better continuum-of-care comparison than a single-service home-health operator.
- The annual evidence sharpens a recurring archive pattern: durable healthcare demand often comes from services that are socially necessary but operationally messy, with labor, state reimbursement, route-to-market density, and local execution doing as much work as science or IP.
- Addus is one of the clearest packets in the archive for understanding how aging-in-place demand gets monetized through recurring labor hours, rate support, and acquisition-built local density.

## Quarter-by-quarter takeaways

### Most recent quarter

- Results: Q2 `2026` revenue was `$377.4 million`; net income was `$27.6 million`; adjusted EBITDA was `$49.2 million`; cash flow from operations was `$40.0 million`.
- Guidance: the quarter supported the view that Addus could still grow profitably in a steadier post-acquisition phase through organic demand, state rate support, and disciplined execution rather than through one large one-time step-up.
- Management message: management emphasized continued demand for cost-effective home care, strong execution across service lines, and ongoing leverage reduction.
- Structural themes: personal care remained `78.4%` of revenue, hospice `17.0%`, and home health `4.6%`, which makes the business a recurring home-based support network first and a clinical-acuity expansion story second.

### Quarter minus 1

- Results: Q1 `2026` revenue was `$363.6 million`; net income was `$25.1 million`; adjusted EBITDA was `$44.5 million`; cash flow from operations was `$52.4 million`.
- Guidance: Q1 showed stable hiring, healthy organic demand, and enough balance-sheet flexibility to keep expanding through acquisitions.
- Management message: management highlighted personal care as the main driver, supported by state rate increases in Texas and Illinois, while hospice stayed strong and home health remained a smaller complement.
- Structural themes: the quarter is the clearest recent example of Addus as a density-builder. The HomeCourt acquisition in Indiana was not about entering a glamorous category. It was about adding recurring local scale in a state where the company can build more coverage over time.

### Quarter minus 2

- Results: Q4 `2025` revenue was `$373.1 million`; net income was `$29.8 million`; adjusted EBITDA was `$50.3 million`; cash flow from operations was `$18.8 million`.
- Guidance: the company exited `2025` with a stronger base following a year of acquisition integration and service-line expansion.
- Management message: the year-end message focused on record annual revenue of `$1.42 billion`, continued demand for home-based care, and the ability to keep compounding across the personal-care and hospice footprint.
- Structural themes: Q4 `2025` is the bridge quarter where Addus most clearly looks like home-based recurring-care infrastructure rather than a narrow home-health provider.

## Signals to feed into higher-level analysis

- Sector: Addus is one of the best current packets for understanding recurring healthcare services that are highly operational, lower-cost than facility care, and tightly tied to demographic demand.
- Consumer: the consumer trend is not discretionary health spending. It is the preference of older, chronically ill, or disabled patients and families to remain at home if the care model is affordable and reliable.
- Cultural: the company reflects the normalization of aging in place, home as the preferred setting of care, and a broader expectation that healthcare systems should reduce family and patient friction rather than route everyone into institutions.
- Societal: Addus sits directly inside aging, long-term-services-and-supports demand, dual-eligible complexity, and the pressure to manage chronic need without moving more patients into higher-cost settings.
- Regulatory: reimbursement rates, managed-care contracts, electronic visit verification, Medicaid long-term services, and state budget decisions remain central to value capture.
- Technology: the edge is not frontier science. It is workflow, electronic visit records, compliance systems, referral coordination, and operating density that make managed-care partners more willing to narrow networks.
- Industrial: caregiver hiring, retention, wage pass-through, local branch density, acquisition integration, state concentration, and rate support matter more than simple headline revenue.
- Capital allocation: Addus is a strong example of an acquisition-led service compounding model where balance-sheet flexibility is used to add local density and adjacent capabilities rather than to chase one transformative bet.

## Thematic interpretation

- Addus helps explain a different recurring-care pattern than the rest of the healthcare batch. `Option Care` monetizes complex specialty therapy outside the hospital. `DaVita` monetizes mandatory chronic treatment frequency. Addus monetizes long-duration daily-living support, hospice, and selective home-health capacity.
- That distinction matters because it shows how much healthcare value can come from reducing the total cost of care rather than from owning a therapy, device, or high-acuity care site. The economic pitch is simple: keep people at home longer, intervene earlier, and avoid costlier settings.
- The company also strengthens the archive’s cultural and societal read. Aging in place is not just a demographic fact. It is becoming an organizing principle for care delivery, reimbursement design, and family expectations.
- A second repeated pattern is that home-based care is recurring but fragile. Revenue can be durable, yet still exposed to labor availability, state rate decisions, managed-care bargaining power, and local execution quality.
- Addus also adds a useful industrial comparison to the archive because it shows how fragmented service markets often become consolidation lanes once payors prefer scaled partners with compliance systems, technology, and broader care-continuum capabilities.
- The cross-company lesson is that modern healthcare is increasingly built around recurring infrastructure in the home, not just in clinics, labs, and infusion centers. Addus is the cleanest current packet for the lower-acuity, labor-intensive end of that home-based continuum.
