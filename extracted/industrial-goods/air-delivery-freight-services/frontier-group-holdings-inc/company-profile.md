## What changed

- Frontier is the archive's missing ultra-low-cost airline case. Delta shows the loyalty-currency model. United shows the premium-network and reliability model. Southwest shows the merchandising-reset model. Frontier shows a thinner consumer relationship layer where fare stimulation, ancillary extraction, and aircraft-productivity discipline do more of the work.
- The annual filing makes the business model concrete. Frontier describes itself as an `ultra low-cost carrier`, had `176` Airbus single-aisle aircraft as of `2025-12-31`, served about `100` airports during `2025`, and generated about `$3.724B` of revenue while posting a net loss of about `$137M`.
- The loyalty and interface layer still exists, but it is different from Delta, United, or even Southwest. The annual materials emphasize `FRONTIER Miles`, the co-brand card, `Discount Den`, `GoWild!`, first-class seating by the end of `2026`, and onboard Wi-Fi by the end of `2027`, but the economics still look much more price-led and ancillary-led than premium-status-led.
- The `2026` quarter trail is dominated by two non-routine forces: the TSA-fee reserve and the early-return agreement for `24` A320neo aircraft. That makes Frontier a good operator for studying not only consumer demand, but also fleet flexibility and balance-sheet repair inside a fragile airline model.

## Open questions

- Is Frontier best read as an airline relationship system that is still being thickened, or as a lower-quality transport interface where the customer connection remains intentionally thin because price and ancillary design matter more than deep loyalty?
- How much of the Q2 `2026` improvement reflects a durable change in demand and revenue management versus short-cycle supply tightening and one-time reset effects?
- What does it mean for the broader archive that AnnualReports classifies a consumer airline under `Industrial Goods` while the actual business is one of the clearest travel-interface cases in the repo?
