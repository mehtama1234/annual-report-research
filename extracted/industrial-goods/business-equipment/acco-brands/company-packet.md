## Annual report takeaways

- ACCO is a strong `Business Equipment` packet because it captures the low-growth, everyday office-edge economy where work, school, and household routines still require physical tools, but replacement and replenishment demand are uneven and increasingly contested.
- The `2025` annual and year-end release show a company managing contraction rather than riding clean category growth. Full-year `2025` net sales were about `$1.525B`, down `8.5%` from `2024`, while adjusted operating income fell to about `$153.3M` from `$189.7M` and adjusted EPS declined to about `$0.84` from `$1.02`.
- The strategic response is the core signal. Management emphasized a pivot toward higher-growth technology peripherals, highlighted by the `EPOS` acquisition closed on `2026-01-30`, while still squeezing a mature installed base of branded office, workspace, learning, and gaming-accessory categories.
- Cost discipline is doing a large share of the work. ACCO said its multi-year cost reduction program had produced more than `$60M` of cumulative savings and was on track to reach `$100M` by the end of `2026`. That tells the frontier something important about mature device and office-edge categories: management execution increasingly matters more than top-line expansion.
- The annual chain also gives a broader consumer and industrial read. Soft global demand, tariff-related disruptions, FX volatility, and pressure in core office categories all show up directly in results. This is a useful packet for understanding how hybrid work normalization and weaker office-product intensity keep squeezing legacy demand even when branded products remain broadly distributed.

## Quarter-by-quarter takeaways

### Most recent quarter

- Results: Q2 `2026` net sales were about `$415.1M`, up `5.1%` year-on-year; net income was about `$14.1M`; GAAP EPS was about `$0.15`; adjusted EPS was about `$0.29`.
- Segment and demand signals: Americas segment sales were about `$262.9M`, up `5.8%`, with comparable sales up `1.8%`; International segment sales were about `$152.2M`, up `4.0%`, but comparable sales were down `9.3%`.
- Operating signals: comparable company-wide sales declined `2.3%`; EPOS added `5.7%` to revenue growth and favorable FX added `1.7%`; adjusted operating income increased to about `$48.1M` from `$47.1M`.
- Management signal: the company said the Americas business benefited from strong back-to-school sell-in and better-than-expected performance in Mexico, while International faced softness and shipment disruption from a planned systems upgrade at its largest EMEA distribution center.
- Interpretation: Q2 shows ACCO as a routine-economy operator. Results improved not because office-product demand broadly recovered, but because seasonal school demand, acquisition help, and cost savings offset structural weakness in workspace and technology-peripheral demand.
- Bigger-picture read: the quarter is a useful cultural signal that everyday edge categories still move with school calendars, retailer restocking, and household routine, but those supports now coexist with weaker office intensity, geographic softness, and more fragile channel demand than the category once enjoyed.

### Quarter minus 1

- Results: Q1 `2026` net sales were about `$343.7M`, up `8.3%`; net income was about `$19.4M`; GAAP EPS was about `$0.20`; adjusted EPS was about `$0.02`.
- Segment and demand signals: Americas sales were about `$178.5M`, up `2.6%`, with comparable sales down `2.3%`; International sales were about `$165.2M`, up `15.1%`, with comparable sales down `2.8%`.
- Operating signals: adjusted operating income increased to about `$11.7M` from `$6.9M`; leverage remained about `4.1x`; management reaffirmed full-year free cash flow of `$75M` to `$85M` and leverage target of `3.7x` to `3.9x`.
- Accounting and acquisition signal: reported earnings benefited from a bargain purchase gain of about `$37.6M` tied to the preliminary EPOS purchase price allocation, making adjusted earnings the more useful operating measure.
- Interpretation: Q1 is the clearest quarter for separating reported growth from underlying category pressure. FX and acquisition support lifted sales, but comparable demand in office-product categories stayed negative in both major segments.
- Bigger-picture read: this is what mature business-equipment normalization looks like after the pandemic and post-pandemic reshuffling. Demand is no longer anchored by broad-based office expansion; it is patchy, tied to channels and geographies, and increasingly dependent on portfolio repositioning into peripherals and accessories.

### Quarter minus 2

- Results: Q4 `2025` net sales were about `$428.8M`, down `4.3%`; net income was about `$21.3M`; GAAP EPS was about `$0.23`; adjusted EPS was about `$0.38`.
- Full-year `2025` signals: net sales were about `$1.525B`, down `8.5%`; adjusted net income was about `$78.8M`; operating cash flow was about `$68.7M`; adjusted free cash flow was about `$69.5M`; leverage was about `4.1x`.
- Mix signals: management highlighted trend improvement in the Americas segment, with growth in technology accessories and moderating declines in core categories, while the International business remained pressured by softer demand in Europe and other core-product weakness.
- Structural signals: the company paid about `$27.0M` of dividends in `2025`, repurchased about `3.2M` shares for `$15.1M`, and leaned on cost-out and portfolio reshaping rather than broad organic growth.
- Interpretation: Q4/full-year `2025` is the defining transition quarter. It shows ACCO straddling two realities at once: a shrinking legacy office-products base and an attempt to reposition around peripherals, gaming, learning, and adjacent branded productivity tools.
- Bigger-picture read: this quarter makes the frontier link explicit. Everyday device and office-edge categories are no longer straightforward office proxies. They sit at the intersection of hybrid work, school demand, household budgets, retailer discipline, tariff exposure, and the question of which physical tools still earn recurring consumer attention.

## Signals to feed into higher-level analysis

- The office edge is now a routine-demand business, not a broad growth story: ACCO shows that many categories still matter because they sit inside daily work, learning, and household routines, but demand is more seasonal, fragmented, and deferrable than before.
- Hybrid life changes the category mix: learning and creative products, gaming accessories, and peripherals can still grow, while legacy office-product categories keep facing secular pressure from lower paper intensity and less centralized office usage.
- Portfolio shift is a survival strategy: the `EPOS` deal and repeated emphasis on technology peripherals show that mature operators are trying to buy or build their way into adjacent categories with better growth and margin potential.
- Cost-out and cash discipline are central operating levers: a multi-year savings program and leverage management are not side notes here. They are core to how a mature hardware-and-supplies operator preserves earnings when volumes soften.
- Geography and channel execution matter more than category labels: Mexico strength, EMEA softness, a distribution-center systems upgrade, and retailer timing all visibly shape results. That makes this a useful packet for understanding how low-growth physical-product operators are managed through operational variance rather than through simple demand growth.
- Customer concentration and channel power remain important: the `2025` `10-K` says net sales to the top five customers were about `$484.7M`, and Amazon alone represented about `10%` of annual sales, reinforcing how much category economics depend on retailer and channel control.

## Working interpretation

- ACCO materially strengthens the fourth sub-lane because it gives the frontier a true `Business Equipment` packet rather than relying only on cross-category proxies like HP, Apple, or Xerox.
- The key consumer and societal pattern is that work, school, and home routines remain physically mediated even after digital substitution, but the mix of what gets bought is changing. Core office intensity is lower, while school, creative, gaming, and accessories demand carry more of the category's resilience.
- The strongest repeating pattern is that mature everyday-hardware operators increasingly survive through brand portfolios, cost takeout, selective acquisitions, and channel management rather than through broad category growth.
- For this frontier, ACCO also links the everyday-device lane to larger cultural shifts around hybrid work, household budget discipline, and the persistence of school-year seasonality. It is a useful reminder that the edge of the consumer and office interface is still physical, but it now operates under slower replacement logic and tighter margin discipline.
