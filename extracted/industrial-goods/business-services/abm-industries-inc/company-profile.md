## What changed

- Revenue: Q4 `2025` revenue was a record `$2.3 billion`, FY2025 revenue was a record `$8.7 billion`, Q1 `2026` revenue was `$2.2 billion`, and Q2 `2026` revenue returned to a second-quarter record `$2.3 billion`. This is steady operating growth rather than a sharp cyclical swing.
- Earnings and cash flow: Q4 `2025` net income was `$34.8 million`, diluted EPS was `$0.56`, adjusted diluted EPS was `$0.88`, and adjusted EBITDA was `$124.2 million`. Q1 `2026` net income was `$38.8 million`, adjusted diluted EPS was `$0.83`, adjusted EBITDA was `$117.8 million`, and free cash flow was `$48.9 million`. Q2 `2026` net income was `$43.1 million`, adjusted diluted EPS was `$0.90`, adjusted EBITDA was `$131.7 million`, and free cash flow was `$22.4 million`.
- Operating model: the filings describe ABM as a leading provider of facility, engineering, and infrastructure solutions. That matters because the company reads as more than janitorial or outsourced labor. The active demand pockets are airports, warehouses, education facilities, commercial sites, semiconductor fabrication environments, battery-storage systems, and datacenter-related services.
- Segment mix: the `2025` Form `10-K` says ABM has five reportable segments: Business & Industry, Manufacturing & Distribution, Education, Aviation, and Technical Solutions. The quarterlies show the strongest recent growth coming from Technical Solutions, Aviation, and Manufacturing & Distribution, while Education stays steady and Business & Industry is lower growth but still important.
- WGNSTAR and semiconductor exposure: the late-`2025` and `2026` materials make WGNSTAR one of the most important strategic pivots in the file set. Management presented the deal alongside Q4 `2025` results, then said in Q1 `2026` that the business had closed at the start of Q2 and strengthened ABM's position in the semiconductor market and fabrication environments.
- Datacenter and battery-storage demand: Q2 `2026` explicitly ties ATS growth to battery energy storage systems and datacenter-related services. That makes ABM a useful bridge inside the industrial set because it connects technical facilities work to the same AI and power-buildout chain that also benefits Caterpillar, Eaton, and Trane.
- Aviation and transport exposure: Aviation grew `7%` in Q4 `2025`, `10%` in Q1 `2026`, and `20%` in Q2 `2026`. Management tied the Q2 ramp to healthy domestic air travel and new contract wins including London Heathrow. This adds an airport-operations and transportation-infrastructure layer to the archive.
- Management tone: constructive but operationally specific. Management repeatedly points to bookings, backlog, service mix, cost savings, pricing actions, and volume timing rather than broad macro optimism.

## Working interpretation

- ABM is one of the better archive examples of how recurring physical-economy demand often hides in outsourced operations rather than in hardware or construction alone. Airports, logistics sites, schools, semiconductor environments, and datacenter-adjacent facilities all need labor-heavy, always-on support.
- The deeper pattern is that technical complexity is widening what a facilities operator can be. ABM is no longer just a low-end outsourcing read; it is also a window into how mission-critical operating environments create demand for recurring engineering and technical-services capability.

## Open questions

- How durable is the recent Technical Solutions growth once the current semiconductor, datacenter, and battery-storage project mix normalizes?
- Can WGNSTAR lift ABM's growth profile and margin mix enough to make the company read more like mission-critical industrial infrastructure support and less like a lower-multiple facilities-services operator?
