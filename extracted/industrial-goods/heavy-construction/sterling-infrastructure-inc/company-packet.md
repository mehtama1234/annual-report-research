# Sterling Infrastructure, Inc. Packet

Date baseline: 2026-08-10

## Snapshot

- Company: Sterling Infrastructure, Inc.
- Sector: Industrial Goods
- Industry: Heavy Construction
- Ticker: `STRL`
- Fiscal year-end: December
- Target annual report year: 2025
- Target quarter window:
  - quarter 1: Q2 2026
  - quarter 2: Q1 2026
  - quarter 3: Q4 2025

## Why this name matters

- Sterling is the right follow-on after Granite because it shows a different version of the same physical-infrastructure buildout: less public-works breadth, more concentrated exposure to mission-critical site development, electrical services, and the land-preparation layer behind data centers and semiconductor facilities.
- It also adds a sharper cultural and industrial signal. Where Granite is still anchored in public infrastructure conversion, Sterling is the clearest packet in this batch on how AI, cloud capacity, and advanced-manufacturing demand pull through into dirt work, site utilities, electrical integration, and construction-margin mix shift.

## Annual report takeaways

- Sterling's `2025` evidence stack shows a company in the middle of a portfolio transformation. Full-year revenue reached `$2.49B`, net income attributable to common stockholders reached `$290.2M`, adjusted net income reached `$336.7M`, and adjusted EBITDA reached `$503.8M`.
- Management's year-end framing is more important than the income statement alone. Sterling said adjusted EBITDA margin exceeded `20%` for the first time in company history in `2025`, which signals a real quality shift rather than just revenue growth.
- The annual and year-end materials define the business through three segments: `E-Infrastructure Solutions`, `Transportation Solutions`, and `Building Solutions`. That structure matters because the growth and margin engine is increasingly concentrated in E-Infrastructure rather than spread evenly across all three.
- End-of-year demand visibility was unusually strong. Signed backlog reached `$3.01B`, combined backlog reached `$3.31B`, and management said signed backlog, unsigned awards, and future-phase opportunities together pointed to a pool of work approaching `$4.5B`.
- The annual lane is also acquisition-shaped. `CEC` became a major contributor to both revenue and backlog in late `2025`, reinforcing that Sterling is building scale and capability around site development plus mission-critical electrical work rather than staying a simpler regional contractor.

## Quarter-by-quarter takeaways

### Most recent quarter: Q2 2026

- Revenue reached `$1.17B`, net income reached `$155.8M`, adjusted net income reached `$180.8M`, and adjusted EBITDA reached `$256.7M`.
- Backlog reached `$4.33B` and combined backlog reached `$5.62B`, while management said pipeline of high-probability future phase work exceeded `$1.4B`. That pushes Sterling's visibility stack above `$7.0B` when signed backlog, unsigned awards, and future phase opportunities are considered together.
- The latest quarter makes the strategic shift explicit. Management said `Transportation Solutions` revenue declined `20%` while adjusted operating income still increased `8%`, because resources are being reallocated away from transportation projects and toward higher-margin E-Infrastructure opportunities.
- E-Infrastructure is now the center of gravity. Management said mission-critical projects including data centers, manufacturing, and semiconductor facilities represented `92%` of E-Infrastructure backlog at quarter end.
- Sterling also raised full-year `2026` guidance again. Updated ranges call for revenue of `$4.00B` to `$4.15B`, adjusted diluted EPS of `$19.70` to `$20.30`, and adjusted EBITDA of `$891M` to `$916M`.

### Quarter minus 1: Q1 2026

- Revenue reached `$825.7M`, net income reached `$96.0M`, adjusted net income reached `$111.3M`, and adjusted EBITDA reached `$166.6M`.
- Signed backlog reached `$3.80B` and combined backlog reached `$5.15B`, showing that the demand build was not a one-quarter Q2 jump but already visible entering the year.
- The key operating signal was not just growth but composition. Sterling said mission-critical projects represented over `90%` of E-Infrastructure backlog at quarter end and highlighted an initial phase award for a large multi-year semiconductor fabrication campus.
- Management also said the company was already delivering both site and electrical services in integrated fashion on two data-center campuses. That matters because it shows cross-selling between legacy site development and acquired electrical capabilities turning into actual project execution rather than just acquisition theory.
- Building Solutions remained the weak spot. Revenue increased only `3%`, and management explicitly tied the soft near-term outlook to housing-affordability pressure on prospective homebuyers.

### Quarter minus 2: Q4 2025

- Fourth-quarter revenue reached `$755.6M`, net income reached `$87.6M`, adjusted net income reached `$96.0M`, and adjusted EBITDA reached `$142.1M`.
- Full-year `2025` revenue reached `$2.49B`, adjusted diluted EPS reached `$10.88`, and adjusted EBITDA reached `$503.8M`.
- Signed backlog ended the year at `$3.01B` and combined backlog at `$3.31B`, while management said the broader pool of visible work was approaching `$4.5B`.
- The segment commentary in Q4 is one of the cleanest strategic reads in the packet. E-Infrastructure posted `123%` revenue growth and `91%` adjusted operating income growth, while mission-critical work represented `84%` of E-Infrastructure backlog at year end.
- Transportation still performed well in Q4, but management was already talking about downsizing the low-bid Texas heavy-highway business to improve margin mix, which set up the more explicit resource reallocation discussed in `2026`.

## Consumer, cultural, and societal interpretation

- Sterling is far from the end consumer, but it sits very close to some of the biggest societal shifts in the archive. Data-center expansion, semiconductor reshoring, manufacturing-site buildout, power-intensive logistics facilities, and continued digital-capacity demand all eventually require site work, utilities, electrical services, and local execution.
- This packet is one of the archive's clearest demonstrations that AI is not only a software-and-chip story. Sterling monetizes the dirt, trenching, grading, electrical, and site-preparation layer that must exist before servers or fabs can operate.
- The contrast between segments is culturally important too. `Building Solutions` remains tied to the affordability-constrained housing consumer, while `E-Infrastructure` is tied to enterprise, cloud, and industrial capex. That means the same company contains both a stressed consumer-adjacent lane and a structurally stronger digital-industrial lane.
- There is also a regional-growth and industrial-policy signal here. Semiconductor campuses, manufacturing facilities, and data-center clusters are long-duration projects tied to land availability, power access, and public-private investment priorities, which makes Sterling a useful read on where physical U.S. growth is actually getting funded and built.

## Industrial and operating pressures

- Sterling's current strength is concentrated, not evenly diversified. If mission-critical site development and electrical demand slow, the company has more exposure to a mix reversal than a contractor with steadier public-infrastructure dependence.
- Acquisitions are helping growth, but they also complicate analysis. `CEC` clearly lifted revenue and backlog in `2025` and early `2026`, and `Stone Ridge` contributed to the Q2 `2026` setup, which means investors have to separate true organic demand from acquired scale.
- Transportation is no longer simply a growth lane. Management is intentionally reallocating resources away from lower-margin transportation work, which can lift mix quality but also reduces diversification if the E-Infrastructure boom becomes less favorable.
- Building remains exposed to housing affordability and homebuilder hesitation. That pressure did not disappear even while the rest of the company accelerated.
- Sterling's visibility framework is powerful, but it still requires discipline. Signed backlog, combined backlog, and future phase opportunities are useful leading indicators, yet they are not the same as recognized revenue or guaranteed margin conversion.

## Repeated higher-order patterns

- `AI demand monetizes through site work before it monetizes through finished buildings.` Sterling shows that data-center and semiconductor buildouts start with land, grading, utilities, and mission-critical electrical infrastructure.
- `Backlog is becoming a layered visibility system.` Sterling does not just talk about backlog; it now uses signed backlog, combined backlog, and future phase opportunities as stacked indicators of demand depth.
- `Margin quality can rise even while legacy lanes shrink.` Transportation revenue fell in Q2 `2026`, but adjusted operating income still rose because management is actively moving resources toward higher-margin work.
- `Consumer weakness and enterprise infrastructure strength can coexist inside one company.` Building Solutions is pressured by affordability, while E-Infrastructure is being pulled forward by hyperscale, manufacturing, and industrial-capacity investment.
- `Physical-layer winners are becoming more integrated.` Sterling is increasingly pairing site development with mission-critical electrical services, which is a more valuable position than selling only one slice of the project scope.

## Best comparison uses

- Against `Granite Construction Incorporated`: Granite is the broader public-works and civil-conversion read, while Sterling is the more concentrated mission-critical site-development and electrical-infrastructure read.
- Against `MasTec, Inc.`: MasTec is the wider power, pipeline, telecom, and utility execution platform; Sterling is more narrowly exposed to the site-development and mission-critical electrical layer behind data centers, semiconductor, and manufacturing campuses.
- Against `Knife River Corporation`: Knife River shows materials and public-works pull-through, while Sterling shows how digital and industrial capex increasingly drives local heavy-construction activity without needing the same aggregates-ownership model.
