## Why this name matters

- DNOW is the right next flagship after Fastenal because it extends the industrial-supply lane into a more energy-, utilities-, flow-control-, and instrumentation-heavy operating layer.
- It also turns the rejected `MRC Global` idea into a stronger packet. Instead of trying to force a dead standalone company after its November 6, 2025 acquisition, DNOW captures the actual combined platform that now intermediates PVF, gas products, pumps, fabricated equipment, measurement, instrumentation, and digital sourcing across upstream, midstream, gas utilities, downstream, industrial, and energy-transition-adjacent markets.

## Annual report takeaways

- The `2025` annual baseline is the Form `10-K`, not a separate annual report. DNOW’s own investor FAQ says the company does not publish one, which matters because it keeps the evidence chain clean and avoids pretending a missing IR PDF exists.
- The merged business is materially larger and broader. In the annual filing and first-half `2026` materials, DNOW says it operates under the `DNOW` and `MRC Global` brands and serves customers through approximately `300` locations in the U.S., Canada, and select international markets.
- The end-market frame widened meaningfully after the merger. Management now describes the portfolio across upstream, gas utilities, downstream and industrial, and midstream, with explicit references to water handling, petrochemical and chemical processing, mining, water and wastewater treatment, data centers, LNG terminals, and RNG facilities.
- The product story is also broader than plain pipe and fittings. The filing calls out PVF, gas products, pumps, fabricated equipment, oilfield supply, valve automation and modification, measurement, instrumentation, and other specialty products, plus the `DigitalNOW` and `MRCGO` e-commerce platforms.
- Full-year `2025` revenue was `$2,820M`, gross profit was `$478M`, adjusted gross profit was `$651M`, net loss attributable to DNOW was `$89M`, adjusted net income attributable to DNOW was `$104M`, adjusted EBITDA was `$209M`, and cash provided by operating activities was `$155M`.
- The merger with `MRC Global` is the central annual event. DNOW closed the acquisition on `2025-11-06` for about `$1,763M` net of cash acquired, recorded provisional goodwill of `$383M`, and said first-year synergy savings were already expected at `$23M`, `35%` above target, while preserving the `$70M` three-year synergy commitment.

## Quarter-by-quarter takeaways

### Most recent quarter: Q2 2026

- Revenue was `$1,307M`.
- Gross profit was `$243M`, or `18.6%` of revenue, and adjusted gross profit was `$272M`, or `20.8%`.
- Net loss attributable to DNOW was `$21M`, while adjusted net income attributable to DNOW was `$21M`, or `$0.12` per diluted share.
- Adjusted EBITDA was `$60M`, or `4.6%` of revenue.
- Cash flow from operating activities was `$133M`, cash was `$114M`, total long-term debt was `$474M`, and net debt leverage improved to `1.7x`.
- This is the first quarter where the merger story starts to look operationally credible rather than just strategic. Management described strong collections, inventory streamlining, better U.S. segment growth, and materially higher EBITDA, with second-quarter U.S. midstream revenue surpassing a `$1B` annualized run rate for the first time.
- Capital allocation also matters here. DNOW repurchased `$25M` in the quarter and `$75M` year to date under the `$160M` authorization, signaling confidence that the integration is stabilizing enough to support aggressive repurchases.

### Quarter minus 1: Q1 2026

- Revenue was `$1,183M`.
- Gross profit was `$193M`, or `16.3%` of revenue, and adjusted gross profit was `$256M`, or `21.6%`.
- Net loss attributable to DNOW was `$44M`, while adjusted net income attributable to DNOW was `$3M`, or `$0.01` per diluted share.
- Adjusted EBITDA was `$39M`, or `3.3%` of revenue.
- Cash used in operating activities was `$95M`, with cash of `$116M`, long-term debt of `$571M`, and total liquidity of about `$379M`.
- This was the first full quarter with `MRC Global` and the pressure showed. Management talked about integration progress, but also about the need to address ERP-transition issues and to improve execution. That makes `Q1` important as the “messy absorption” quarter before the `Q2` rebound.
- The company also bought `Edge Controls` for `$46M` in February `2026`, expanding automation and controls capabilities within U.S. Process Solutions. That is a useful signal that DNOW is not only integrating a giant merger; it is also continuing to push into higher-value technical categories.

### Quarter minus 2: Q4 2025

- Full-year `2025` revenue was `$2,820M`, and fourth-quarter revenue was `$959M`.
- Full-year adjusted EBITDA was `$209M`, or `7.4%` of revenue, while fourth-quarter adjusted EBITDA was `$61M`, or `6.4%`.
- Full-year operating cash flow was `$155M`, and fourth-quarter operating cash flow was `$83M`.
- The quarter and year were dominated by the `MRC Global` close. Management described the merger as strategically transformative, emphasized that cost synergies were ahead of plan, and said `2025` would still have been DNOW’s fifth consecutive year of revenue growth and highest adjusted EBITDA year ever even excluding the fourth-quarter MRC contribution.
- The hard part is visible too. Management explicitly flagged persistent challenges tied to the U.S. `MRC Global` ERP transition that went live in the third quarter of `2025`, making this quarter a clean read on why the combined company still faced near-term execution friction heading into `2026`.

## Consumer, cultural, and societal interpretation

- DNOW is not consumer-facing, but it sits in the social substrate of industrial continuity. Utilities, upstream fields, chemical plants, LNG terminals, water systems, and data-center builds all depend on the quiet availability of valves, fittings, pumps, controls, and instrumentation. This is recurring infrastructure demand disguised as distribution.
- The merger also reflects a broader cultural shift inside industrial procurement: customers increasingly prefer fewer, larger, technically capable vendors that can handle sourcing, stocking, logistics, and product expertise across multiple sites and categories.
- There is a labor and skill shortage theme here too. The more complex the installed base becomes, and the harder it is for customers to staff technical purchasing and maintenance internally, the more value shifts to distributors that combine local inventory with engineering support, automation capability, and digital ordering infrastructure.
- DNOW also shows how “energy” and “industrial” are blending. Management now talks in one breath about gas utilities, midstream, data centers, RNG, water treatment, and industrial process demand. That is a structural clue that physical-infrastructure spending is converging around reliability, electrification, fluid movement, and process control rather than staying in neat sector boxes.

## Industrial and operating pressures

- Integration is the main pressure. The `MRC Global` merger expands reach and category depth, but it also adds ERP, working-capital, service-level, and cultural-integration risk.
- Working capital matters more here than in lighter models. Receivables quality, inventory positioning, and cash conversion all directly affect whether revenue growth turns into balance-sheet strength or merely more operational sprawl.
- End-market diversification helps, but the company is still exposed to project timing and energy-cycle variability. Upstream, midstream, gas utility, industrial, and process markets are more balanced together than apart, but they are not immune to capex pauses or commodity-linked caution.
- Margin quality remains sensitive to execution. Adjusted gross profit strips out real merger and inventory effects, so the gap between GAAP and adjusted results has to be interpreted carefully while the combined platform normalizes.
- Capital allocation is both a strength and a test. Aggressive repurchases can be smart when the integration is working, but they also raise the bar for management to prove that leverage, inventory, and systems risk are genuinely under control.

## Repeated higher-order patterns

- `Industrial distribution is consolidating into multi-layer infrastructure platforms.` DNOW is no longer just a distributor of parts; it is becoming a broader control point for product flow, technical support, and customer workflow across energy and industrial networks.
- `Recurring infrastructure demand often hides inside project-looking categories.` Gas utilities, midstream maintenance, water systems, and industrial process operations still need constant replacement, service, and supply-chain support even when headline capex slows.
- `AI and software demand still ride on fluid and power hardware underneath.` DNOW’s explicit references to data centers, controls, measurement, and process equipment are a reminder that digital growth still depends on pipes, valves, pumps, utilities, and operating hardware.
- `Big mergers promise diversification but first create operating fragility.` `Q1 2026` and `Q2 2026` are a good paired case study: first the combined company absorbs ERP and working-capital strain, then it starts to show whether the broader platform can produce better cash flow and more durable end-market balance.
- `Capital allocation has become part of the operating signal.` Buybacks here are not just financial engineering. They are management’s claim that inventory, leverage, and synergy capture are under enough control to return cash while still integrating a large transaction.

## Best comparison uses

- Against `Fastenal Company`: Fastenal shows high-frequency consumables, vending, and onsite procurement control; DNOW shows more project-linked, energy-and-process-heavy PVF and instrumentation distribution.
- Against `Ferguson Enterprises`: Ferguson is tied more to contractor, plumbing, HVAC, waterworks, and commercial-build channels; DNOW is tied more to energy networks, gas utilities, midstream, process plants, and controls.
- Against `WESCO International`: WESCO has broader electrical, communications, and utility distribution exposure; DNOW is a better read on fluid movement, gas infrastructure, process hardware, and instrumentation.
