# Ferguson Packet

Date baseline: 2026-08-10

## Snapshot

- Company: Ferguson Enterprises Inc.
- Sector: Industrial Goods
- Industry: Plumbing / HVAC Distribution
- Ticker: `FERG`
- Fiscal year-end: December
- Assigned CLI 8 sub-lane: wholesale and distribution infrastructure
- Target annual report year: 2025
- Target quarter window:
  - quarter 1: Q2 2026
  - quarter 2: Q1 2026
  - quarter 3: Q4 calendar 2025 / transition-period quarter ended 2025-12-31

## Why this name matters

- Ferguson is the strongest broad-line plumbing, HVAC, PVF, and waterworks distribution flagship available for this batch. It sits between fragmented suppliers and fragmented specialized contractors, which makes it one of the clearest ways to read the recurring physical-service layer behind housing, water systems, comfort systems, and commercial project execution.
- It also gives the archive a better read on the difference between upstream product manufacturing and downstream channel control. Ferguson is not primarily paid for invention. It is paid for availability, local density, inventory, fleet, branch relationships, technical support, and its ability to simplify complex jobs for contractors.
- That makes it a strong comparison against `Watsco, Inc.`, `Core & Main, Inc.`, and `Applied Industrial Technologies, Inc.`. Watsco is the narrower HVAC-distribution specialist, Core & Main is the waterworks and fire-protection channel specialist, and Applied is the industrial-MRO specialist. Ferguson shows what a broader contractor-facing infrastructure distributor looks like when water, HVAC, residential repair, non-residential projects, and bolt-on M&A are all under one roof.

## Annual report takeaways

- The annual filing chain shows a large, asset-light but operationally dense channel business. The fiscal-`2025` `10-K` said Ferguson served a `$340B` North American construction market, worked with about `37,000` suppliers, operated `1,746` branches as of `2025-07-31`, and employed about `35,000` associates. The transition-period `10-KT` updated that to `1,729` branches, about `6,200` fleet vehicles, and the same approximate associate count as of `2025-12-31`.
- The basic business model matters because it explains why this name belongs in an infrastructure batch. Ferguson says residential and non-residential each account for roughly half of net sales, while roughly two-thirds of net sales tie to repair, maintenance and improvement rather than new construction. That means a large share of demand is recurring and failure-driven rather than purely cyclical new-build demand.
- The transition-period and calendar-`2025` chain also shows how much this company depends on market fragmentation. Ferguson explicitly frames itself as bridging a fragmented supplier base and an even more fragmented customer base. That is the core moat: local inventory, product breadth, service, and workflow simplification in markets where many contractors still operate close to home and need rapid fulfillment.
- The most important annual signal is mix quality. The `2026-02-24` calendar-`2025` results release reported sales of `$31.3B`, gross margin of `31.0%`, adjusted operating margin of `9.6%`, operating cash flow of `$2.2B`, eight acquisitions with annualized revenue above `$300M`, and net debt to adjusted EBITDA of `1.1x`. Management emphasized double-digit non-residential growth and continued market-share gains despite a weak residential backdrop.
- The category mix inside the `10-KT` is also useful for comparison work. As of the transition-period disclosure, Waterworks was `24%` of U.S. sales, Ferguson Home was `21%`, Commercial/Mechanical `15%`, Residential Trade Plumbing `15%`, HVAC `11%`, Industrial `7%`, Facilities Supply `4%`, and Fire & Fabrication `3%`. That is a wide contractor-economy cross section rather than a single-product vertical.

## Quarter-by-quarter takeaways

### Most recent quarter: Q2 2026

- Ferguson reported results on Monday, August 10, 2026. Sales increased `4.6%` to `$8.751B`, reported operating profit increased to `$893M`, adjusted operating profit reached `$932M`, operating margin improved to `10.2%`, adjusted operating margin was `10.7%`, and diluted EPS increased `6.9%` to `$3.43`.
- The most important change was not just the headline growth rate. Management said residential growth returned even though the residential market backdrop remained challenging, while non-residential revenue increased `8%` and large capital project activity stayed healthy. That is a strong signal that Ferguson was still taking share rather than just floating on macro demand.
- Guidance also improved. On August 10, 2026 management moved `2026` net-sales guidance from low-to-mid-single-digit growth to mid-single-digit growth and raised the low end of adjusted operating-margin guidance from `9.4%` to `9.5%`, while keeping the top end at `9.8%`. Capital-expenditure guidance also increased to `$375M-$425M`.
- The operating message is that pricing is normalizing but not collapsing. Price inflation was in the low single digits and gross margin slipped `20` basis points because the prior-year quarter had been temporarily elevated by supplier-price timing. Even with that, operating leverage stayed positive because service density and cost discipline held.
- The strategic layer sharpened again with M&A. Ferguson completed five acquisitions in the quarter and signed the deal for FloWorks, a distributor of highly technical valves and flow-control solutions. That pushes the company further into industrial-flow and higher-complexity channels rather than only ordinary plumbing and HVAC distribution.

### Quarter minus 1: Q1 2026

- On `2026-05-05`, Ferguson reported sales of `$7.472B`, gross margin of `31.0%`, reported operating profit of `$612M`, adjusted operating profit of `$647M`, operating margin of `8.2%`, adjusted operating margin of `8.7%`, and diluted EPS of `$2.13`.
- The quarter showed the same pattern that later strengthened in `Q2`: weak residential conditions but resilient share-taking. Management said price inflation was in the mid-single digits, residential revenue was down `1%`, and non-residential revenue increased about `8%`, helped by commercial/mechanical, industrial, waterworks, and large capital project activity.
- Guidance was unchanged at this stage, which matters. Management was constructive enough to keep the annual outlook intact but not yet strong enough to raise it. That suggests the August guidance increase was earned by actual year-to-date performance rather than by optimism alone.
- Capital allocation remained active. Ferguson completed two acquisitions during the quarter, one additional deal after quarter-end, signed definitive agreements on another three, repurchased `$236M` of stock, authorized a new `$2B` repurchase program, and kept leverage near `1.0x` net debt to adjusted EBITDA.
- The cultural and societal read here is that deferred residential work still existed, but had not disappeared. Repair, maintenance and improvement activity remained soft, which is consistent with households delaying nonessential work under affordability pressure while still addressing failures and complex jobs when necessary.

### Quarter minus 2: Q4 calendar 2025

- The `2026-02-24` results chain is the correct quarter-minus-two read because it covers both full calendar `2025` and the quarter ended `2025-12-31` after the fiscal-year shift. In that quarter, sales increased `3.6%` to `$7.495B`, gross margin reached `30.6%`, reported operating profit reached `$596M`, adjusted operating profit reached `$625M`, and diluted EPS rose to `$1.99`.
- The strongest pattern was a split market. Management said residential revenue was down `2%` in the quarter because new housing starts, permit activity, and RMI work stayed soft, while non-residential revenue increased `10%`, driven by waterworks and commercial / mechanical activity including large capital projects.
- The full-year result matters because it shows the broader operating backdrop before `2026` began. Ferguson reported calendar-`2025` sales of `$31.3B`, price inflation in the low single digits, adjusted operating profit of `$3.011B`, and eight acquisitions with annualized revenue above `$300M`. That gives the packet a stronger footing than relying only on the five-month transition filing.
- This quarter also locks in the broader growth-driver framework management carried into `2026`: water infrastructure, large capital projects, climate and comfort, and aging and underbuilt housing. Those phrases recur because they describe Ferguson’s real demand stack better than a simple "construction recovery" narrative does.

## Consumer, cultural, and societal interpretation

- Ferguson is not a consumer brand in the ordinary sense, but it still sits under consumer behavior. When housing affordability is poor, households delay discretionary remodeling and some replacement work. That softness shows up in Ferguson’s residential RMI commentary before it shows up in more obvious retail channels.
- The company also sits inside a broader cultural move toward resilience and reliability. Water systems, HVAC systems, and commercial mechanical systems are not aspirational purchases. They are failure-response and uptime purchases. That makes Ferguson a good read on how cost-of-living pressure and infrastructure neglect shift spending from optional upgrades toward essential fixes.
- There is also a labor-and-complexity pattern here. Specialized contractors increasingly need product availability, technical support, and simplified procurement because jobs are more fragmented and labor remains constrained. Ferguson’s value proposition is built around reducing job friction rather than merely shipping boxes.
- Water and climate comfort are becoming more socially visible categories. Aging pipe networks, wastewater needs, heat stress, comfort expectations, and building-system complexity all create recurring demand even when housing sentiment is weak. Ferguson’s mix makes those pressures monetizable through distribution rather than through direct utility ownership or heavy manufacturing.

## Industrial and operating pressures

- Residential weakness remains a real drag. Across the quarter chain, Ferguson repeatedly ties softness to weak new residential activity, weak permits, and soft RMI. The `Q2 2026` improvement did not remove that pressure; it only showed that Ferguson could outperform it.
- Supplier pricing still matters, but the quality of margin matters more. Gross margin around `31%` was supported by disciplined execution, while some quarter-to-quarter movement came from the timing and extent of supplier price increases. That means simple margin comparisons can overstate or understate underlying operating strength.
- Large capital projects and non-residential bidding are now central to the growth story. Waterworks and commercial/mechanical exposure repeatedly drove the stronger side of the business, which makes Ferguson more tied to infrastructure modernization and industrial project flow than a surface read of "plumbing distributor" would suggest.
- Acquisitions are part of the moat but also part of the risk. Ferguson uses bolt-on M&A to deepen density and capability, but the pace of deals means systems integration, local execution, and culture remain important risk points.
- The company is also exposed to trade policy and input-cost volatility. The filings explicitly discuss tariffs, sanctions, retaliatory measures, pricing swings, and supply-chain disruption risk. That matters because Ferguson sits in the pass-through channel where supplier cost moves have to be translated into customer pricing without losing share.

## Repeated higher-order patterns

- `Repair demand is infrastructure demand.` Ferguson shows that a major share of infrastructure monetization runs through repair, maintenance, and replacement rather than through headline megaproject announcements alone.
- `The local branch and service layer is a moat.` In fragmented contractor markets, distribution power comes from proximity, availability, and technical support, not from consumer mindshare.
- `Residential weakness and infrastructure strength can coexist.` Ferguson repeatedly shows that housing softness does not prevent strong growth in waterworks, commercial/mechanical, and large capital project channels.
- `Channel businesses can compound through mix, density, and bolt-on M&A.` Ferguson is a case study in how recurring service revenue, acquisition discipline, and branch-scale economics can matter as much as any one product category.
- `The physical layer behind AI and software often hides in ordinary categories.` Water systems, flow control, HVAC, and mechanical infrastructure do not look like technology narratives on the surface, but they are part of the uptime, cooling, and building-service stack that modern digital infrastructure still depends on.

## Best comparison uses

- Against `Watsco, Inc.`: Watsco is the cleaner HVAC replacement and contractor-tech specialist; Ferguson is the broader contractor-infrastructure distributor spanning waterworks, plumbing, PVF, HVAC, commercial/mechanical, and more.
- Against `Core & Main, Inc.`: Core & Main is the narrower aging-water and fire-protection channel specialist; Ferguson is the wider branch-density platform where water is important but not the whole story.
- Against `Applied Industrial Technologies, Inc.`: Applied monetizes industrial uptime and engineered MRO; Ferguson monetizes contractor complexity, branch convenience, and recurring repair across construction-adjacent and civil-infrastructure markets.
- Against `WESCO International, Inc.`: WESCO is more exposed to utility, communications, and data-center electrical distribution; Ferguson is more exposed to water, mechanical, comfort, and contractor-field execution.
