# United Rentals, Inc. Packet

Date baseline: 2026-08-10

## Snapshot

- Company: United Rentals, Inc.
- Sector: Industrial Goods
- Industry: Rental & Leasing Services
- Ticker: URI
- Fiscal year-end: December
- Target annual report year: 2025
- Target quarter window:
  - quarter 1: Q2 2026
  - quarter 2: Q1 2026
  - quarter 3: Q4 2025

## Why this name matters

- United Rentals is the right rental-and-leasing flagship for this batch because it captures the access model behind physical infrastructure growth: customers do not need to own everything if they can rent scale, specialty capability, logistics, and service from the market leader.
- It also closes another important layer in the archive. Core & Main shows distribution and channel power. Mueller shows fabricated installed systems. CECO shows environmental process equipment. United Rentals adds fleet economics, utilization, capital intensity, and customer outsourcing behavior.

## Annual report takeaways

- The `2025` annual report and `10-K` present United Rentals as the largest equipment rental company in the world, built around a one-stop-shop model spanning general rentals, specialty products, technology, and service.
- The annual report makes clear that this is not a simple construction-cycle proxy. It says industrial and other non-construction rentals and commercial construction rentals each accounted for about `48%` of rental revenue in `2025`, reflecting exposure to manufacturers, energy, chemicals, utilities, retail, infrastructure, and commercial project work.
- `2025` was another record year. The annual report says total revenue reached `$16.1B`, GAAP diluted EPS was `$38.61`, adjusted EPS was `$42.06`, net income was `$2.5B`, adjusted EBITDA was `$7.3B`, and net cash from operating activities was `$5.2B`.
- The shareholder letter is especially useful for theme extraction. Management says large projects drove most of `2025` growth, with strong demand across non-residential construction and infrastructure, while industrial end markets showed particular strength in the power vertical.
- Specialty continues to reshape the business. The annual materials say specialty again posted double-digit growth and note that specialty rentals increased `13.6%` year over year, supported by all seven specialty businesses.
- The annual report also flags the new pressure point: growth has not removed cost friction. Inflation, delivery costs, ancillary revenue mix, and re-rent revenue mix now matter more to margin interpretation than sheer demand alone.

## Quarter-by-quarter takeaways

### Most recent quarter: Q2 2026

- Total revenue was `$4.410B`, rental revenue was `$3.849B`, net income was `$753M`, GAAP diluted EPS was `$12.03`, adjusted EPS was `$12.76`, adjusted EBITDA was `$2.056B`, and fleet productivity increased `3.4%`.
- Management raised `2026` guidance again to total revenue of `$17.5B-$17.8B` and adjusted EBITDA of `$7.975B-$8.125B`, while keeping free-cash-flow expectations intact.
- The quarter shows why this name matters thematically. Management said customers remain optimistic, especially around large projects, and linked the better outlook to customer backlogs and year-to-date momentum.
- The operating details are mixed in the right way. General rentals margin improved, but specialty rental margin softened because of lower-margin ancillary and re-rent mix. That is a more sophisticated signal than just “demand is strong.”
- Capital intensity remained high but controlled. Year-to-date gross rental capex was `$2.931B`, while the balance sheet still carried a `1.8x` net leverage ratio and nearly `$3.0B` of liquidity.

### Quarter minus 1: Q1 2026

- Total revenue was `$3.985B`, rental revenue was `$3.419B`, net income was `$531M`, adjusted EBITDA was `$1.759B`, fleet productivity was up `2.3%`, and operating cash flow was `$1.514B`.
- Guidance was raised here as well, with management citing momentum into the busy season and growth opportunities within large projects and key verticals.
- The margin story is more nuanced than the headline. Excluding the prior-year H&E merger termination benefit, first-quarter adjusted EBITDA margin improved, but specialty margin still took pressure from depreciation, delivery costs, and ancillary mix.
- The quarter also introduced restructuring as a live issue. Results included `$45M` of restructuring charges tied to branch consolidations and other cost-reduction measures initiated in the fourth quarter of `2025`.
- This is important analytically because United Rentals is showing both operating leverage and active cost redesign at the same time, rather than relying on market growth alone.

### Quarter minus 2: Q4 2025

- Quarter revenue was `$4.208B`, rental revenue was `$3.581B`, net income was `$653M`, adjusted EBITDA was `$1.901B`, and fourth-quarter fleet productivity increased `0.5%`, while full-year fleet productivity increased `2.2%`.
- Full-year operating cash flow was `$5.190B` and free cash flow was `$2.181B` after gross rental capex of `$4.189B`, which is a clean demonstration of the business model’s cash-generation power even during aggressive fleet investment.
- Management said `2026` would likely resemble `2025`, with large projects and dispersed geographic demand driving most of growth. That is a useful read on how broad-based project activity remains despite macro uncertainty.
- Margin pressure was visible here too. General rentals margin and specialty rentals margin both faced inflation, depreciation, and delivery-cost pressure, with specialty also hit by lower-margin ancillary growth.
- The quarter also sharpened capital-allocation discipline: `2025` ended with `$2.364B` returned to shareholders, including `$1.9B` in repurchases and `$464M` in dividends, while leverage remained within target.

## Consumer, cultural, and societal interpretation

- United Rentals is not consumer-facing in the usual sense, but it reflects a broader economic behavior shift: more customers prefer access over ownership when equipment is expensive, specialized, seasonal, or operationally complex.
- This is a physical-economy analogue to platform usage. Customers outsource fleet ownership, maintenance, telematics, logistics, and availability risk to the rental platform rather than carrying that burden themselves.
- The annual and quarter materials also show the social and industrial weight of large projects. Infrastructure, power, utilities, industrial construction, and specialty site needs are increasingly consuming rental capacity, which means physical project complexity is becoming a recurring source of demand.
- There is also a labor-and-productivity angle. The one-stop-shop framing is partly about helping customers work around labor scarcity, site complexity, and the need for fast deployment without building permanent internal equipment fleets.

## Industrial and operating pressures

- United Rentals remains highly capital intensive. Strong free cash flow is real, but it sits alongside multi-billion-dollar gross rental capex requirements and constant fleet refresh decisions.
- Fleet economics matter at every level. Growth has to be read through OEC expansion, fleet productivity, utilization, depreciation, resale recovery, and mix between owned equipment, ancillary revenue, and re-rent.
- Delivery costs and specialty mix are real margin constraints. Both `Q4 2025` and `Q2 2026` show that strong specialty growth can still pressure rental gross margin if the mix skews toward lower-margin ancillary or re-rent revenue.
- Inflation, tariffs, and other macro input shocks remain named risks in the `10-K`, but the more operationally immediate pressures are delivery, depreciation, labor, and the challenge of repositioning fleet to match geographically dispersed project demand.
- Restructuring is now part of the operating story. The `Q1 2026` materials show branch and function consolidation work underway, which implies management is actively defending efficiency while preserving growth capacity.

## Repeated higher-order patterns

- `Access models can be as economically powerful as ownership models.` United Rentals shows how renting can create recurring revenue and high returns in heavy physical infrastructure.
- `Large projects increasingly shape fleet and margin behavior.` Project concentration can drive growth, but it also forces more fleet repositioning, delivery costs, and specialty-mix variability.
- `Specialty expansion improves relevance but complicates margin reading.` Higher specialty revenue does not automatically mean higher margins if ancillary and re-rent mix expands faster than owned-rental economics.
- `Physical-layer AI and infrastructure demand is broader than digital vendors.` Power, utility, and infrastructure work all require rented equipment, site services, and specialty capability to get built.
- `Capital allocation discipline is part of the product.` United Rentals’ value proposition to shareholders depends not only on growth, but on balancing fleet investment, leverage, repurchases, dividends, and restructuring at the right points in the cycle.

## Best comparison uses

- Against `Core & Main`: Core & Main earns through distribution density and product/channel mix; United Rentals earns through fleet productivity, utilization, service, and capital deployment.
- Against `Mueller Industries`: Mueller reflects installed-system manufacturing economics; United Rentals reflects the outsourced access model used to build and service those systems.
- Against a future `WESCO` packet: United Rentals is not a stocked-inventory distributor, but both names help explain how scale, customer workflow integration, and service depth become moats in physical infrastructure categories.
