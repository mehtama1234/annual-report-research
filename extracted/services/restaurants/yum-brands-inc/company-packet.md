## Annual report takeaways

- YUM describes a global franchise system of approximately 1,500 franchisees,
  more than 63,000 restaurants, and operations across 155 countries and
  territories (p. 3).
- 2025 financial highlights include company sales of $2.745 billion, franchise
  and property revenues of $3.473 billion, total revenues of $8.214 billion,
  and operating profit of $2.574 billion (p. 2).
- Taco Bell reported 7% same-store sales growth for 2025, while the report
  emphasizes innovation and consumer relevance (p. 4).
- Byte by Yum! processed more than 370 million digital transactions, up more
  than 60% year over year; KFC digital sales rose more than 20%, and U.S. Taco
  Bell loyalty membership grew more than 20% (p. 5).
- The 10-K describes company sales, franchise and property revenue, company
  restaurant expenses, franchisee financing, labor and commodity inflation,
  digital ordering, supply-chain, privacy, and regulatory risks (pp. 102-120,
  200).

## Seven research themes

### Premium-value bifurcation

Reported evidence: YUM describes consumer relevance, value, product innovation,
same-store performance, and discretionary-spending sensitivity (pp. 4, 120).
The report does not isolate household income cohorts, price elasticity, or a
causal value-to-traffic bridge.

### Restaurant margin vise

Reported evidence: the 10-K reports company sales, franchise/property revenue,
and company restaurant expenses by division, including 2025 company restaurant
expense totals (p. 200). This is useful margin context but does not establish
franchisee unit economics, labor-only pressure, or the cause of changes.

### Health-conscious consumption

The annual materials discuss menu innovation, food quality, food safety,
consumer preferences, and brand relevance (pp. 4, 109). They do not provide a
dated health-oriented demand or menu-mix measure. Theme presence remains
contextual and mechanism evidence is absent.

### Labor and automation

Reported evidence: YUM describes approximately 49,000 employees, digital and
technology platforms, Byte, digital ordering, and labor/wage pressures (pp. 5,
105, 113). No labor-hour, throughput, service-time, or automation attribution
is established.

### Supply-chain and geopolitical volatility

Reported evidence: the 10-K identifies suppliers, distributors, raw-material
and commodity costs, supply interruption, currency, and international-market
risks (pp. 109-116). These are exposure statements, not a realized shock
estimate or company-specific causal result.

### Regulatory fragmentation

Reported evidence: YUM discusses food safety, privacy and data transfer,
consumer protection, labor, tax, local and international regulatory exposure
(pp. 111-114, 119). No measured regulatory cost or mechanism outcome is
established.

### Capital concentration and ownership

Reported evidence: the company is organized around franchisees, franchisee
capitalization and financing, company restaurants, refranchising, debt, and
capital allocation (pp. 3, 102, 107-108, 200). Ownership and leverage context
do not establish superior economics or causality.

## Open evidence boundary

The report provides strong contextual coverage and selected company-reported
facts. It does not provide a complete like-period traffic/mix decomposition,
labor-throughput bridge, franchisee margin measure, or falsifier for the
observatory's signal-to-company mechanisms. Those remain open for LLM review.
