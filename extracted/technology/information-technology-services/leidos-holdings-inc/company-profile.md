## What changed

- Leidos is increasingly legible as a mission-critical infrastructure-software company rather than as a generic services contractor. The annual report centers digital modernization, cyber, mission software, AI-enabled multi-domain operations, and managed health services.
- The segment map sharpened the story. As of the `2025` annual report the company was organized around `National Security & Digital`, `Health & Civil`, `Commercial & International`, and `Defense Systems`, showing a portfolio built around federal workflow depth rather than around one narrow product category.
- Revenue concentration remained the defining structural fact. Leidos said `87%` of fiscal `2025` revenue came from U.S. government contracts, which means program alignment, appropriations timing, and procurement structure matter more here than classic enterprise-software seat growth.
- Backlog stayed large and resilient. Total backlog moved from roughly `$49.0 billion` at fiscal year-end `2025` to `$48.4 billion` in `Q1 2026` and `$48.7 billion` in `Q2 2026`, with Defense and Intelligence & Digital remaining the biggest visibility anchors.
- The balance sheet was used to deepen capability. Debt rose from `$4.6 billion` at `2025` year-end to `$6.3 billion` in `Q1 2026` after the Entrust transaction, then eased to `$6.0 billion` by `Q2 2026`.

## Working interpretation

- Leidos belongs in the archive because it fills the layer between raw communications infrastructure and the institutions that consume data, bandwidth, and software to run real-world missions. Towers, satellites, backup power, and test gear explain where capacity lives; Leidos explains how governments operationalize that capacity.
- The most important read is that recurring infrastructure-like economics can exist inside a contractor frame when the work is mission-critical, clearance-heavy, program-entrenched, and aligned with federal modernization priorities.

## Open questions

- How durable is Leidos’s current segment and backlog mix if federal priorities shift away from present intelligence, defense, homeland, and health workflow spending?
- Does the rising debt and acquisition activity produce real capability densification, or does it mainly add integration burden inside an already complex program portfolio?
