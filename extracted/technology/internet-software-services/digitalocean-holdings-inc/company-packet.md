# DigitalOcean Holdings, Inc. Company Packet

## Why this company belongs in CLI 5

DigitalOcean belongs in CLI 5 because it adds a lightweight builder-first infrastructure layer that sits between consumer-internet software and the heavier cloud, telecom, and datacenter stacks. It is not just a small cloud vendor. The filings increasingly frame DigitalOcean as an `agentic inference cloud` or `AI-Native Cloud` aimed at developers, startups, SMBs, digital businesses, and AI-native customers that want usable infrastructure without hyperscaler complexity. That makes it one of the clearest tests of whether a simpler, developer-centric infrastructure platform can capture a real toll as AI adoption broadens beyond the largest enterprises.

## Annual anchor: year ended `2025-12-31`

DigitalOcean's calendar fiscal year means the freshest annual available by `2026-08-10` is the `2025` annual package, filed on `2026-02-24` with the annual-report PDF following on `2026-04-24`. The annual filing shows a company that was already scaling profitably before the later `2026` acceleration became obvious. Revenue reached roughly `$901.4M`. Gross profit reached about `$539.6M`, operating income reached about `$157.0M`, net income reached about `$259.3M`, and net cash provided by operating activities reached about `$309.6M`. Cash and cash equivalents ended the year at roughly `$254.5M`.

The annual plus `Q4 2025` materials show why the company matters in CLI 5. This is a cloud platform trying to monetize easier adoption, developer reach, and smaller-customer usability rather than the deepest enterprise-control stack. Even before the full AI-native pivot was spelled out, management was already tying the model to top-customer growth, rising AI traction, record incremental ARR, and stronger monetization from larger customers.

That shift is important because it changes the category read. DigitalOcean is no longer just a long-tail SMB host. The later filings show a platform trying to serve a broader ladder: small builders at the low end, but increasingly AI-native and higher-spending customers at the high end. In lane terms, it is a lightweight infrastructure-control and capacity-access layer rather than a pure app surface or a physical chokepoint.

## What this company helps prove

- Sector role: DigitalOcean is a lightweight infrastructure-access and developer-control layer that monetizes simpler cloud adoption and increasingly AI-native infrastructure use.
- Consumer signal: more digital participation keeps producing new small businesses, creator businesses, software tools, and AI-native applications that need real infrastructure but cannot justify the full complexity of hyperscaler stacks.
- Cultural and societal signal: the infrastructure market is becoming more polarized between giant enterprise platforms and easier-to-use systems for smaller builders; DigitalOcean is trying to monetize the second path.
- Institutional signal: AI adoption is not staying inside the largest enterprises. Smaller teams and AI-native companies are also demanding inference, agents, managed infrastructure, and simpler workflows.
- Technology signal: the filings suggest a new infrastructure edge can form around inference services, integrated cloud tooling, and easier deployment rather than only around bare-metal GPU resale.
- Industrial signal: DigitalOcean shows that a lighter infrastructure platform can improve economics if it captures higher-spending customers without inheriting the full balance-sheet burden of the largest cloud owners.
- Capital-allocation signal: the company is still much lighter than telecom or datacenter landlords, but it is increasingly taking on capacity commitments, GPU exposure, and infrastructure financing decisions as AI demand rises.

## Working interpretation

DigitalOcean improves CLI 5 because it adds a missing middle layer between software and heavy infrastructure. Cloudflare and Fastly sit at the edge and runtime layer. NetApp sits at governed data persistence. DigitalOcean sits closer to cloud capacity access and developer enablement, but with a much lighter and simpler positioning than the largest public cloud platforms.

The stronger pattern in the filings is not merely that cloud demand exists. It is that a simplified infrastructure experience can itself be a toll. DigitalOcean historically monetized ease of use for developers and smaller businesses. The recent quarter chain suggests management is trying to extend that same advantage into AI-native infrastructure, inference, and agentic workloads. If that works, the company becomes a useful proof that the next infrastructure profit pool is not reserved only for hyperscalers, enterprise incumbents, or raw GPU lessors.

That matters because it maps onto a broader social shift. More people and more organizations can now participate in building software-like products, digital businesses, and AI tools without behaving like classic enterprise IT shops. The infrastructure layer that lets these builders move faster without mastering hyperscaler complexity can become valuable in its own right. That is a real participation and access pattern, not a niche technical detail.

The risk is that DigitalOcean is being pulled into a harder game than the one it originally won. As AI demand grows, the business increasingly depends on larger customers, committed capacity, contract life, and inference economics. That may improve growth, but it also makes the model less purely “simple SMB cloud” and more like a lighter infrastructure allocator. The quarter chain shows both sides at once: stronger growth and larger commitments, but also rising exposure to infrastructure-scale execution.

The open question for CLI 5 is whether DigitalOcean can remain the cleaner builder-first access layer while moving upmarket into AI-native workloads, or whether this path inevitably pushes it toward the heavier burden stack that larger infrastructure providers already carry. That is exactly why the company belongs in the lane.
