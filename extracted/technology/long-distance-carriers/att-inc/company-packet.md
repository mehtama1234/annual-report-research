## Annual report takeaways

- AT&T is a core communications-and-access company because it operates at national connectivity scale across wireless, fiber, and fixed-wireless access while still carrying the burdens of legacy telecom infrastructure, regulation, and capital intensity.
- The `2025` annual report and Q4 material show a decisive strategic reframing. AT&T is not presenting the business primarily as a traditional long-distance or wireline carrier. It is recoding the company around converged fiber and `5G` customer growth, then formalizing that shift in `2026` through `Advanced Connectivity` and `Legacy` segment reporting.
- That matters for this frontier because the key question is no longer just who owns telecom networks. It is who owns the recurring household and small-business connectivity relationship as media fragments, work moves across locations, and customers demand one provider for multiple everyday access needs.
- The annual package also reinforces the heavy industrial reality under the consumer story. AT&T remains a debt-bearing, infrastructure-intensive operator spending tens of billions of dollars on spectrum, fiber, network modernization, and customer acquisition while trying to remove old copper-network costs fast enough to keep the legacy business from dragging the whole system.
- Strategically, the message is blunt: the future carrier is a bundled connectivity utility with wireless, fiber, fixed wireless, and service integration, while the old telecom stack is being harvested, decommissioned, or pushed toward immateriality.

## Quarter-by-quarter takeaways

### Most recent quarter

- Results: Q2 `2026` revenue was about `$31.6B`; adjusted EBITDA was about `$12.3B`; cash from operating activities was about `$10.8B`; capital expenditures were about `$5.7B`; capital investment was about `$6.1B`; and free cash flow was about `$4.7B`.
- Customer metrics: AT&T added more than `1M` advanced-connectivity customers in the quarter, including `646,000` advanced-connectivity internet net adds made up of `367,000` fiber and `279,000` fixed-wireless adds, plus `432,000` postpaid phone net adds with churn of `0.86%`.
- Convergence metrics: about `42.5%` of households with advanced home internet also chose AT&T wireless, showing how the company now measures the customer relationship through bundle attachment rather than through single-product penetration.
- Interpretation: Q2 shows telecom’s new center of gravity. Growth is no longer coming from the historic carrier core. It is coming from a managed connectivity bundle where fiber, wireless, fixed wireless, and billing ownership reinforce each other.
- Bigger-picture read: the quarter also makes the legacy drag explicit. AT&T stated that legacy revenues were down `25.9%`, expects legacy service revenue to fall `20%+` in `2026`, and expects legacy EBITDA to turn negative after `2027` until direct copper costs are largely eliminated. This is a modern infrastructure transition story, not just a telecom earnings story.

### Quarter minus 1

- Results: Q1 `2026` revenue was about `$31.5B`; adjusted EBITDA was about `$11.8B`; capital expenditures were about `$4.9B`; capital investment was about `$5.1B`; and free cash flow was about `$2.5B`.
- Strategic signals: AT&T closed the acquisition of Lumen’s mass-markets fiber business on `2026-02-02`, bringing more than `4M` incremental fiber locations and helping push total fiber locations to more than `37M`.
- Customer metrics: the company reported `584,000` advanced-connectivity internet net adds, split evenly between `292,000` fiber and `292,000` fixed-wireless adds, plus `294,000` postpaid phone net adds with churn of `0.89%`.
- Interpretation: Q1 shows that customer ownership in this lane is increasingly about convergence discipline, not category purity. Management stressed that roughly `42%` of advanced home internet subscribers also chose AT&T wireless and said the rate approached `45%` excluding acquired Lumen customers.
- Bigger-picture read: the quarter also captures the industrial tradeoff clearly. Free cash flow fell year over year because fiber deployment accelerated. The company is deliberately accepting heavier near-term capital intensity to secure longer-lived access relationships.

### Quarter minus 2

- Results: Q4 `2025` revenue was about `$33.5B`; adjusted EBITDA was about `$11.2B`; capital expenditures were about `$6.8B`; capital investment was about `$7.1B`; and free cash flow was about `$4.2B`. Full-year `2025` revenue was about `$125.6B`; adjusted EBITDA was about `$46.4B`; and free cash flow was about `$16.6B`.
- Customer metrics: AT&T added `421,000` postpaid phone net adds in the quarter, more than `1.5M` for full-year `2025`, plus `283,000` fiber net adds and `221,000` Internet Air net adds in Q4. `42%` of AT&T Fiber households also chose AT&T wireless.
- Structural shift: management framed the company around one trusted provider for all connectivity needs and laid out the new reporting structure that would begin in `2026`: `Advanced Connectivity`, `Legacy`, and `Latin America`.
- Interpretation: Q4/FY `2025` is the clearest archive moment where the old telecom identity gives way to a new operating logic. What used to be sold as phone service, broadband, and line access is now sold as one converged connectivity stack with a runoff legacy tail attached to it.

## Signals to feed into higher-level analysis

- Connectivity as infrastructure: AT&T is a strong anchor for the idea that communications access behaves like a utility even when marketed like a consumer subscription product. Fiber locations, wireless churn, capital investment, and network decommissioning all point back to infrastructure economics.
- Customer ownership and subscriptions: the most important commercial metric is not a legacy carrier line count. It is the convergence rate between home internet and wireless. That is where retention, household lock-in, and revenue durability increasingly sit.
- Consumer and cultural shift: management’s repeated language around one trusted provider for all connectivity needs suggests a household preference for simplification. As work, entertainment, and communication all depend on stable access, the winning offer is increasingly low-friction continuity rather than a stand-alone telecom product.
- Legacy model under pressure: wholesale, VPN, copper-network, and older voice-era structures are visibly shrinking. The archive evidence shows a carrier industry being split into a growing digital-access core and a rapidly eroding legacy services shell.
- Durable operating pressure: AT&T has to finance very large network investments while managing debt, acquired-fiber integration, churn competition, and copper decommissioning. The lane is defensible only if scale and convergence overcome commoditization pressure.

## Working interpretation

- AT&T helps explain how the communications-and-access lane is changing from a legacy telecom hierarchy into a convergence-driven household infrastructure business. The old category label survives in the archive taxonomy, but the economics are increasingly organized around fiber, wireless, fixed wireless, and retention through bundle attachment.
- The most important cultural shift here is that connectivity has become background-critical. Consumers may not think about telecom identity very much, but they do care about always-on access, bill consolidation, outage resilience, and whether one provider can handle work, home, and mobile needs at once.
- That is why AT&T’s metrics matter. The convergence percentage, the fiber-location build, the fixed-wireless adds, and the copper-network runoff all describe a society moving away from distinct communications products and toward one integrated access stack.
- In frontier terms, AT&T pairs with Charter to make the communications lane coherent. Charter shows the cable-side rebundling of household access; AT&T shows the telecom-side reinvention of the carrier as a bundled connectivity utility under heavy infrastructure and legacy-transition pressure.
