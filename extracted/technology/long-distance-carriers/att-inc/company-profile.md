## What changed

- Taxonomy and lane fit: AT&T is a clean exact-category `Long Distance Carriers` flagship, but the quarter chain shows why the category itself is historically true and strategically incomplete. The company is no longer being run as a pure legacy carrier. It is being repositioned as a converged connectivity platform across wireless, fiber, and fixed wireless.
- Segment reframing: starting in `2026`, AT&T shifted to `Advanced Connectivity`, `Legacy`, and `Latin America` segments. That is one of the strongest archive signals in this frontier because it openly separates the growth engine from the runoff business.
- Customer ownership: management keeps emphasizing that around `42%` to nearly `45%` of advanced home internet households also take AT&T wireless. That convergence rate is a modern account-ownership metric: the bundle is the moat.
- Capital intensity: the quarter chain shows a company still spending at infrastructure scale. Capital expenditures were about `$6.8B` in Q4 `2025`, `$4.9B` in Q1 `2026`, and `$5.7B` in Q2 `2026`, with fiber expansion and network modernization central to the operating model.
- Legacy pressure: business transitional and wholesale revenue remained under pressure, and legacy revenues were down more than `25%` year over year in both Q1 and Q2 `2026`. The company is explicitly decommissioning copper and expects legacy EBITDA to turn negative after `2027` before direct copper costs are largely removed.

## Open questions

- Can AT&T keep translating fiber expansion and wireless convergence into durable pricing power, or does connectivity become increasingly utility-like and margin-constrained despite scale?
- How much execution risk remains in absorbing acquired fiber footprints while simultaneously accelerating organic build and copper-network retirement?
- If legacy communications revenue keeps falling faster than expected, does the growth in advanced connectivity fully offset the operational and financial drag quickly enough?
