## Annual report takeaways

- TTM is one of the cleanest exact-category additions for this frontier because `Printed Circuit Boards` sits underneath almost every modern electronics story while rarely getting treated as a flagship operating layer.
- The `2025` annual report makes clear that this is not a simple commodity board manufacturer. TTM describes itself as a global manufacturer of mission systems, RF components, RF microwave and microelectronic assemblies, and advanced interconnect products including PCBs and substrates.
- That matters for the frontier because it places TTM at the physical edge between digital demand and manufacturable hardware reality. If AI, data-center networking, aerospace systems, and medical electronics keep scaling, somebody has to deliver the interconnect, board, substrate, and assembly stack.
- The annual filing also establishes useful structural facts: roughly `$2.9B` of `2025` net sales, about `18,200` employees, around `1,300` customers, and `24` specialized facilities across North America and Asia. This is a meaningful industrial platform, not a tiny specialist.
- TTM's reported segment structure also matters: `A&D`, `Commercial`, and `RF&S Components`. That segment mix shows why the company belongs in a wider frontier note about infrastructure and interface systems. It sits at the overlap of defense electronics, commercial compute and networking, and advanced components.

## Quarter-by-quarter takeaways

### Most recent quarter

- Results: Q2 `2026` net sales were about `$1.0B`, up `37%` year on year; adjusted EBITDA was `$166.8M`, or `16.6%` of sales; non-GAAP net income was `$106.9M`, or `$0.99` per diluted share.
- Demand mix: management said data center and networking were `40%` of sales and grew `91%` year on year on continued AI demand. Aerospace and defense was `37%` of sales, with total program backlog above `$1.7B`.
- Interpretation: Q2 is strong evidence that the AI story is not just a software or chip story. It is also a boards, substrates, power, networking, and manufacturing-capacity story, and TTM is monetizing that physical layer directly.
- Strategic shift: management also tied the quarter to European expansion through announced acquisitions of Swiss Technology Group AG and ILFA GmbH plus new financing capacity. That suggests the company sees the current demand wave as durable enough to justify geographic and balance-sheet expansion.

### Quarter minus 1

- Results: Q1 `2026` net sales were `$846.0M`, up `30%` year on year; adjusted EBITDA was `$132.9M`, or `15.7%` of sales; non-GAAP net income was `$80.1M`, or `$0.75` per diluted share; book-to-bill was `1.41`.
- Management tone: TTM explicitly said growth was driven by continued robust demand in data center and networking supporting AI and data-center buildouts, with additional double-digit growth in medical, industrial, instrumentation, and aerospace and defense.
- Interpretation: Q1 shows the lane broadening beyond a single hot category. TTM is benefiting from multiple infrastructure-heavy end markets at once, which makes it a good bridge company between AI infrastructure, defense resilience, and more traditional industrial electronics.

### Quarter minus 2

- Results: Q4 `2025` net sales were `$774.3M`, up `19%` year on year; adjusted EBITDA was `$126.2M`, or `16.3%` of sales; non-GAAP net income was `$74.8M`, or `$0.70` per diluted share; book-to-bill was `1.35`.
- Full-year view: fiscal `2025` net sales rose to `$2.9B`; non-GAAP net income reached `$259.0M`; adjusted EBITDA reached `$456.3M`, or `15.7%` of sales.
- Interpretation: this quarter matters because it already framed the core `2026` pattern. Management was crediting generative AI, data-center-computing demand, aerospace and defense strength, and healthier medical and industrial demand before the `2026` acceleration became even more visible.

## Signals to feed into higher-level analysis

- Hardware and component bottlenecks: TTM is one of the best current anchors for the idea that the digital economy still relies on hard-to-scale physical substrates. Boards and advanced interconnect are not glamorous, but they are gating infrastructure.
- AI infrastructure as an industrial demand shock: the quarter chain shows AI demand arriving downstream as manufacturing demand for networking and interconnect hardware, not only as software spending or hyperscaler capex headlines.
- Resilience through mixed end markets: TTM is stronger than a pure consumer-electronics cyclical because aerospace and defense, medical, and industrial programs diversify the demand base.
- Customer concentration and dependency risk: the filing still warns that a small number of customers matter a lot. That means this is not a pure moat story; it is also a reminder that strategic manufacturing can remain exposed to procurement shifts.
- Geographic and execution pressure: if demand keeps rising, the constraint is not only whether customers want the product. It is whether TTM can add capacity, manage working capital, integrate acquisitions, and hold margins while scaling.

## Working interpretation

- TTM helps explain a part of the infrastructure stack that broad market narratives usually flatten. The constraint on electronics scaling is not only compute architecture or utility power. It is also whether interconnect manufacturing can keep up with the systems demand created by AI, defense modernization, and more instrumented industrial equipment.
- This makes TTM an unusually useful social and industrial signal. A society that wants more cloud capacity, more autonomous systems, more medical instrumentation, and more defense electronics is also choosing a world with more specialized manufacturing dependency.
- The quarter chain also shows that not all durable demand today is consumer-facing. Some of the strongest demand is now hidden one layer down in components, assemblies, and manufacturing platforms that most end users never see directly.
- For the frontier, TTM is the hardware-substrate counterpart to media and communications companies: it shows that interface control and information flow still depend on physical bottlenecks, specialized labor, capital intensity, and geographic manufacturing footprint.
