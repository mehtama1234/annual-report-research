## What changed

- Convergence moved to the center of the story: AT&T is no longer just selling separate wireless and wireline services. The quarter chain shows a more explicit household-convergence model, with `42%` of advanced home internet households also taking AT&T wireless in Q1 2026 and `42.5%` in Q2 2026.
- The growth engine is increasingly labeled `advanced connectivity`, not legacy telecom. Wireless service, fiber broadband, fixed wireless, and business fiber are being grouped into the part of the portfolio that still grows, while copper-heavy and transitional services are visibly shrinking.
- Legacy drag is still severe: the schedules show legacy segment revenue falling `25.3%` in Q1 2026 and `25.9%` in Q2 2026, with margins also compressing. Management went further in Q2 by saying legacy EBITDA should turn negative after 2027 until copper direct costs are substantially eliminated.
- Network expansion and balance-sheet management are happening at the same time. Q1 2026 included the closing of the Lumen Mass Markets fiber acquisition, while the same schedule set still showed long-term debt of about `$131.6B` and a debt ratio of `52.0%`.
- Bandwidth demand does not arrive as pure upside. AT&T still has to convert data growth into profitable converged relationships while retiring copper, funding fiber, absorbing acquisitions, and carrying a large debt stack.

## Open questions

- Can AT&T keep expanding fiber, fixed wireless, and postpaid relationships fast enough to outrun the economic damage from legacy copper and transitional-service runoff?
- Does the convergence model create durable household wallet control, or does it mainly defend the base while capital intensity and balance-sheet constraints keep the upside capped?
