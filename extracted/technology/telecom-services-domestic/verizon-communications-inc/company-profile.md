## What changed

- Verizon is no longer framing itself mainly as a steady incumbent utility. The quarter chain is organized around a strategic transformation built on lower churn, healthier unit economics, simpler plans, and disciplined promotions rather than subsidy-heavy share chasing.
- The growth engine is a combined mobility-and-broadband relationship, not just a wireless base. Management repeatedly groups wireless, fixed wireless, and fiber together and treats total mobility and broadband service revenue as the central performance line.
- Frontier materially changes the wireline side of the story. The acquisition closed on `2026-01-20`, scaled Verizon's fiber reach above `30M` homes and businesses, and gave the company a more credible national convergence strategy against cable and against AT&T's incumbent fiber build.
- Premium positioning and capital discipline are being paired more tightly than before. Verizon is trying to prove it can improve postpaid volumes, reduce churn, and expand margins while spending less on promotions and still returning capital through repurchases.
- The physical-layer build is still present, but the value-capture argument is shifting toward pricing quality and loyalty architecture. Verizon's network reputation, `Simplicity` plans, `Verizon One` converged offers, loyalty programs, and enterprise credibility are being used as economic tools, not just as marketing language.

## Open questions

- Can Verizon sustain better net adds and lower churn without reverting to aggressive subsidy behavior once competitors respond?
- Does the Frontier expansion create a durable wireline-and-wireless convergence advantage, or does it mostly add integration complexity and more fiber capital intensity to an already mature carrier model?
