## Executive read

`AnnualReports` is useful as a filing locator and a source-taxonomy input.

It is not a good organizing system for the bigger-picture work this archive is trying to do.

The main problem is not just that some companies are mis-bucketed. The deeper problem is that the taxonomy is built around legacy market labels instead of modern behavioral systems, relationship ownership, cultural demand patterns, and embedded monetization models.

That means a separate research lane is justified for the areas that the site does not really cover:

- retail as a unified operating system
- platforms and ecosystem businesses
- consumer behavior and cultural-pattern mapping
- recurring attention and cultural-distribution systems
- direct relationship control versus channel dependence
- cross-sector recurring-behavior models
- modern real-estate and infrastructure logic that cuts across old sector lines

Another way to say the same thing:

the blind spot is not a lack of categories, but a lack of the right categories.

The live browse tree can classify issuers into legacy industries.

It does not classify them into the systems that now matter most for comparative research:

- relationship ownership
- demand aggregation
- repeated behavior
- recurring attention
- identity and participation
- loyalty and wallet capture
- second-layer monetization
- embedded infrastructure beneath the visible product

For this archive, the practical implication is strict:

- ordinary uncovered `AnnualReports` industries are not automatically this thread's problem
- this thread should own the important systems that still have no clean home on the site even if other threads finish the normal sector tree

## Areas that are not really covered by the site

These are the main blind spots that should be treated as first-class research lanes because the live browse tree does not organize them coherently:

- retail as a cross-format operating system rather than a scatter across `Services`, `Consumer Goods`, and `Technology`
- marketplace, platform, and ecosystem control rather than plain software, services, or retail labels
- DTC relationship ownership versus channel dependence
- loyalty, wallet, membership, and status mechanics
- consumer identity systems such as beauty, fit, eyewear, fandom, and collectible participation
- recurring attention and cultural-distribution systems spanning subscription, advertising, creator, and franchise-linked habit formation
- value-hunt, off-price, closeout, and treasure-hunt behavior as behavior systems rather than just discount formats
- hybrid physical-digital demand loops such as gaming, creator ecosystems, and app-mediated consumer coordination
- hidden infrastructure beneath visible demand, including packaging, fulfillment, merchant enablement, and the delivery layer behind the shelf

Inside those broader blind spots, the archive now also has cleaner subgroup structure where the source taxonomy is especially weak:

- trust marketplace versus local-demand coordination
- travel-interface orchestration versus loyalty-thickened intermediation versus decentralized host trust
- freight orchestration versus parcel-network density versus consumer-interface-led last-mile coordination
- paid cultural access versus mixed ad-supported attention versus social feed and creator-distribution systems

What the site mainly captures instead is issuer classification:

- legacy sector labels
- legacy industry labels
- filing location

That is useful metadata, but it is not enough to explain the modern consumer and platform economy.

## What the taxonomy misses

### 1. Retail is not treated as a real first-class system

The live `AnnualReports` browse tree still has no standalone top-level `Retail` sector.

Instead, retail-like operators are scattered across:

- `Services`
- `Consumer Goods`
- `Technology`

This makes the source taxonomy weak for any serious comparison across:

- mass retail
- specialty retail
- off-price
- grocery
- beauty retail
- DTC-led brands
- marketplace commerce
- membership retail

This is why the repo already has to do corrective work by holding source taxonomy fixed while reading a separate interface layer across `Amazon`, `Walmart`, `Costco`, `Kroger`, `Ross`, `Ulta`, `Nike`, `Home Depot`, and similar names.

The structured retail correction layer is now preserved in [Retail Systems Index](/indexes/retail-systems-index-2026-08-10.md), which keeps the subtype, frequency, discretionary intensity, and monetization-side splits legible across these otherwise scattered source buckets.

### 2. The taxonomy does not organize around business-model reality

The browse tree is mostly product or legacy-industry based.

It does not cleanly separate companies by modern business-model structure, such as:

- marketplace versus owned inventory
- wholesale versus DTC
- subscription versus transaction
- loyalty or membership economics
- advertising or retail-media layers
- IP licensing and franchise monetization
- ecosystem control and multi-layer monetization

This makes the taxonomy weak for studying how a company actually converts demand into durable economics.

The same issue is now explicitly corrected in [Marketplace Systems Index](/indexes/marketplace-systems-index-2026-08-10.md), which preserves the difference between commerce flywheels, trust marketplaces, local-demand coordination, and travel-interface systems rather than flattening them into old software or retail labels.

The delivery layer is also now explicitly corrected in the archive through [C.H. Robinson Versus UPS Versus DoorDash Boundary Comparison](/extracted/themes/ch-robinson-vs-ups-vs-doordash-boundary-comparison-2026-08-10.md), which separates freight orchestration, parcel-network infrastructure, and local merchant-demand coordination rather than letting all three collapse into legacy service or internet labels.

### 3. Consumer and cultural patterns are mostly invisible

The site categorizes companies, but not the behavioral systems those companies sit inside.

It does not expose research lanes such as:

- comfort and casualization
- routine-use trust
- value-hunt behavior
- beauty and identity signaling
- fandom and collectible behavior
- wellness and practical health signaling
- occasion-based premiumization
- digital ordering or wallet habit formation

Those patterns matter because the archive is trying to understand not only what a company sells, but what recurring behaviors and cultural rituals support the demand.

### 4. Relationship ownership is hidden

The taxonomy does not distinguish between companies that own the customer relationship directly and those that primarily rent access through channels, wholesale partners, distributors, or third-party demand systems.

That distinction is central for:

- gross-margin quality
- pricing power
- loyalty depth
- customer-data control
- media monetization
- resilience under channel stress

This is one of the biggest blind spots because two companies with similar products can have very different economic quality depending on who owns the relationship.

### 5. Real estate and infrastructure logic is split across legacy lines

The live browse tree separates real-estate and finance categories in a way that obscures the actual operating systems.

Examples:

- multiple REIT categories still sit under `Financial`
- only a small remainder sits under `Real Estate`

That makes it harder to study coherent systems like:

- logistics real estate
- data-center infrastructure real estate
- housing and affordability chains
- experience and destination real estate
- the service layer around physical assets

For the archive, those systems matter more than whether the old source taxonomy puts the name inside `Financial` or `Real Estate`.

### 6. Hybrid and taxonomy-breaking names expose the limit directly

The archive already contains several companies where the source taxonomy is only loosely aligned with operating reality.

Examples already visible in the repo include:

- `Airbnb` under `Technology`
- `Expedia` with stale or blank aggregator classification despite a clearer current travel-interface operating reality in the official source chain
- `Take-Two` under `Technology / Application Software`
- `Kenvue` under `Technology / Biotechnology`
- `eBay` under `Consumer Goods / Internet Service Providers`
- `YETI` under `Consumer Goods / --None--`
- `Planet Fitness` under `Consumer Goods / --None--`
- `Carnival` under `Consumer Goods / General Entertainment`
- `Birkenstock` under `Services / Apparel Stores`
- `Frontier` under `Industrial Goods`

These are not edge curiosities. They are evidence that the taxonomy is not designed to explain the current economy cleanly.

## The practical blind-spot list

If another thread is already filling a normal `AnnualReports` industry bucket, this lane should usually leave that work alone unless the company materially improves one of the systems below.

## Remaining highest-value blind spots

If the purpose of this thread is to cover what the `AnnualReports` industry tree still does not explain well, the highest-value remaining additions are now more selective than before.

The strongest remaining gaps are:

- a second behind-the-shelf comparison only if the goal is to test packaging versus environmental-service infrastructure more explicitly across operators such as `Ball`, `Crown Holdings`, or another materials-middleware or route-density case
- subtype extension and comparison work across marketplace, value-hunt, owned-demand, and hidden-infrastructure anchors that are now already in the archive
- subtype extension and comparison work across recurring-attention anchors only when another name adds a clearly different subscription, ad-funded, creator-led, or franchise-distribution structure

This means the thread should now be more disciplined.

It should spend less time proving that blind spots exist and more time selecting the few remaining names that still widen the cross-sector map in a non-duplicative way.

## What is not covered by the `AnnualReports` browse tree in a useful way

The real blind spot is not missing rows on the website.

It is that the browse tree does not provide durable categories for:

- creator-merchant marketplaces and trust-layer commerce
- demand aggregation and local-delivery coordination
- recurring attention, cultural distribution, and audience-control systems
- relationship ownership and first-party customer control
- loyalty, wallet, stored-value, and status systems
- identity, fandom, and participation-led demand
- value-hunt, closeout, and treasure-discovery behavior
- behind-the-shelf consumer infrastructure and embedded fulfillment layers

Those are the areas this thread should treat as its true ownership boundary.

## What this thread should usually not claim

If another thread is already covering normal sector packetization, this lane should usually not claim:

- generic uncovered sectors just because their `AnnualReports` bucket is still incomplete
- ordinary consumer-goods, industrial, healthcare, financial, or utility names with no stronger cross-sector angle
- another brand or retailer that only repeats an already-established pattern
- companies chosen mainly because the filing chain is easy to collect

The threshold should be higher than "not yet covered."

It should be "not actually explained by the source taxonomy."

## Current practical implication

Most of the blind-spot lane now exists as memo-plus-index structure rather than as a hypothetical future build.

That changes the operating rule:

- prefer subtype extension, comparison quality, and connective synthesis over another generic packet
- add a company only when it materially sharpens an existing blind-spot split or opens a genuinely new one
- use the modern real-estate and infrastructure crosswalk as the default extension point when a property, housing, or infrastructure name crosses old sector walls

## What this thread should usually leave to other threads

If a company is mainly useful as ordinary sector coverage, it should usually stay outside this lane.

That includes most straightforward work in:

- basic materials
- standard industrial equipment
- commodity chemicals
- conventional utilities
- plain banking coverage
- ordinary insurers with no stronger wallet, loyalty, or infrastructure angle
- traditional healthcare packetization with no larger trust, routine-care, or data-system implication

This thread is not for generic completeness.

It is for the areas where the `AnnualReports` industry tree is analytically too blunt.

## Working principle for this lane

This thread should keep using `AnnualReports` as:

- a filing-discovery aid
- a saved source taxonomy
- a consistency check for identity and archive availability

It should not use `AnnualReports` taxonomy as the final analytical frame.

The better frame for this lane is:

1. source taxonomy
2. operating model
3. relationship type
4. recurring behavior
5. cultural or societal signal
6. second monetization layer

## Highest-confidence conclusion

The blind spot in `AnnualReports` is structural, not incidental.

It is good at helping locate documents.

It is weak at organizing the current economy around the things that matter most for this archive:

- repeated behavior
- cultural demand systems
- business-model quality
- relationship ownership
- cross-sector infrastructure and interface patterns

That is why this thread should specialize in the cross-sector correction layer rather than compete with the other threads on ordinary sector coverage.
