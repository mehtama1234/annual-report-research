# AnnualReports Taxonomy Blind Spots

Date baseline: 2026-08-10

## Scope

This memo defines the research areas that are structurally under-covered by the live `AnnualReports.com` industry taxonomy and therefore should be owned by a separate cross-sector thread rather than by standard sector packetization work.

## Executive read

`AnnualReports` is useful as a filing locator and a source-taxonomy input.

It is not a good organizing system for the bigger-picture work this archive is trying to do.

The main problem is not just that some companies are mis-bucketed. The deeper problem is that the taxonomy is built around legacy market labels instead of modern behavioral systems, relationship ownership, cultural demand patterns, and embedded monetization models.

That means a separate research lane is justified for the areas that the site does not really cover:

- retail as a unified operating system
- platforms and ecosystem businesses
- consumer behavior and cultural-pattern mapping
- direct relationship control versus channel dependence
- cross-sector recurring-behavior models
- modern real-estate and infrastructure logic that cuts across old sector lines

## What the taxonomy misses

### 1. Retail is not treated as a real first-class system

The live `AnnualReports` browse tree still has no standalone top-level `Retail` sector.

Instead, retail-like operators are scattered across:

- `Services`
- `Consumer Goods`
- `Technology`

This makes the source taxonomy weak for any serious comparison across:

- mass retail
- specialty retail
- off-price
- grocery
- beauty retail
- DTC-led brands
- marketplace commerce
- membership retail

This is why the repo already has to do corrective work by holding source taxonomy fixed while reading a separate interface layer across `Amazon`, `Walmart`, `Costco`, `Kroger`, `Ross`, `Ulta`, `Nike`, `Home Depot`, and similar names.

### 2. The taxonomy does not organize around business-model reality

The browse tree is mostly product or legacy-industry based.

It does not cleanly separate companies by modern business-model structure, such as:

- marketplace versus owned inventory
- wholesale versus DTC
- subscription versus transaction
- loyalty or membership economics
- advertising or retail-media layers
- IP licensing and franchise monetization
- ecosystem control and multi-layer monetization

This makes the taxonomy weak for studying how a company actually converts demand into durable economics.

### 3. Consumer and cultural patterns are mostly invisible

The site categorizes companies, but not the behavioral systems those companies sit inside.

It does not expose research lanes such as:

- comfort and casualization
- routine-use trust
- value-hunt behavior
- beauty and identity signaling
- fandom and collectible behavior
- wellness and practical health signaling
- occasion-based premiumization
- digital ordering or wallet habit formation

Those patterns matter because the archive is trying to understand not only what a company sells, but what recurring behaviors and cultural rituals support the demand.

### 4. Relationship ownership is hidden

The taxonomy does not distinguish between companies that own the customer relationship directly and those that primarily rent access through channels, wholesale partners, distributors, or third-party demand systems.

That distinction is central for:

- gross-margin quality
- pricing power
- loyalty depth
- customer-data control
- media monetization
- resilience under channel stress

This is one of the biggest blind spots because two companies with similar products can have very different economic quality depending on who owns the relationship.

### 5. Real estate and infrastructure logic is split across legacy lines

The live browse tree separates real-estate and finance categories in a way that obscures the actual operating systems.

Examples:

- multiple REIT categories still sit under `Financial`
- only a small remainder sits under `Real Estate`

That makes it harder to study coherent systems like:

- logistics real estate
- data-center infrastructure real estate
- housing and affordability chains
- experience and destination real estate
- the service layer around physical assets

For the archive, those systems matter more than whether the old source taxonomy puts the name inside `Financial` or `Real Estate`.

### 6. Hybrid and taxonomy-breaking names expose the limit directly

The archive already contains several companies where the source taxonomy is only loosely aligned with operating reality.

Examples already visible in the repo include:

- `Airbnb` under `Technology`
- `Kenvue` under `Technology / Biotechnology`
- `eBay` under `Consumer Goods / Internet Service Providers`
- `Carnival` under `Consumer Goods / General Entertainment`
- `Birkenstock` under `Services / Apparel Stores`
- `Frontier` under `Industrial Goods`

These are not edge curiosities. They are evidence that the taxonomy is not designed to explain the current economy cleanly.

## What this thread should own

Because other threads are covering standard sector packetization, this lane should focus on the gaps the source taxonomy cannot cover well by itself.

### 1. Taxonomy correction layer

Build cross-sector files that preserve source taxonomy but add a more analytically useful overlay.

Core outputs:

- retail-system map
- platform and ecosystem map
- DTC versus channel-control crosswalk
- modern real-estate and infrastructure crosswalk

### 2. Consumer and cultural behavior layer

Map the recurring patterns that sit underneath company results.

Core outputs:

- comfort and casualization
- fandom, play, and collectible identity
- beauty, self-presentation, and fit-confidence demand
- value-hunt and bargain-discovery behavior
- routine-use trust and replenishment behavior
- premiumization, occasion, and ritual spending

### 3. Business-model layer

Organize companies by how they monetize the relationship rather than only by formal industry.

Core outputs:

- recurring interface types
- second monetization layer map
- direct relationship ownership map
- platform versus linear-operator comparison set

### 4. Cross-sector connective memos

Write the files that other sector threads are unlikely to produce because those threads are mostly packet and synthesis work inside existing sector boundaries.

Examples:

- how loyalty and wallet systems recur across restaurants, travel, gaming, retail, and payments
- how comfort and wellness signals recur across footwear, apparel, household behavior, and beauty
- how advertising, marketplace, and seller-service economics recur across retail and platform businesses

## Working principle for this lane

This thread should keep using `AnnualReports` as:

- a filing-discovery aid
- a saved source taxonomy
- a consistency check for identity and archive availability

It should not use `AnnualReports` taxonomy as the final analytical frame.

The better frame for this lane is:

1. source taxonomy
2. operating model
3. relationship type
4. recurring behavior
5. cultural or societal signal
6. second monetization layer

## Highest-confidence conclusion

The blind spot in `AnnualReports` is structural, not incidental.

It is good at helping locate documents.

It is weak at organizing the current economy around the things that matter most for this archive:

- repeated behavior
- cultural demand systems
- business-model quality
- relationship ownership
- cross-sector infrastructure and interface patterns

That is why this thread should specialize in the cross-sector correction layer rather than compete with the other threads on ordinary sector coverage.
