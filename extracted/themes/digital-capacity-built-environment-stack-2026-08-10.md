## Executive read

The modern built environment is no longer just a property story.

For digital-capacity systems, the more useful map is:

1. who owns and monetizes the campus
2. who supplies the power-and-thermal environment
3. who builds the grid, site, and mission-critical network connections
4. who fits out and maintains the building interior
5. who extends the fiber and communications routes into and between facilities

The source taxonomy scatters those answers across `Real Estate` and `Industrial Goods`.

The useful comparison is the stack.

## What the stack clarifies

### 1. Data-center demand is not one lane

`Equinix` and `Digital Realty` show that campus ownership itself already splits into different systems:

- `Equinix` is denser, more ecosystem-led, and more interconnection-driven
- `Digital Realty` is more hyperscale, backlog-led, and powered-land intensive

That is only the ownership layer.

Below that layer:

- `Vertiv` monetizes the power-and-cooling environment
- `Quanta` monetizes grid and mission-critical field buildout
- `EMCOR` monetizes mechanical, electrical, controls, and facilities execution inside the building
- `Dycom` monetizes route and inside-plant connectivity

The point is that digital-capacity growth is not one capex bucket.

It is a layered physical system.

### 2. Ownership economics and execution economics are different

`Equinix` and `Digital Realty` are rewarded when:

- bookings stay strong
- interconnections deepen
- backlog converts
- powered capacity gets delivered

`Quanta`, `EMCOR`, and `Dycom` are rewarded when:

- crews are available
- projects convert cleanly
- scope complexity is absorbed without margin damage
- customers keep awarding work into a constrained capacity base

`Vertiv` sits between those groups.

It is not the landlord and not the field labor platform.

It is the hardware-and-systems layer whose economics improve when infrastructure density and deployment urgency rise.

### 3. The modern bottleneck map is more useful than the old sector map

The old sector map says:

- datacenter REIT
- electrical equipment
- contractor

The bottleneck map says:

- power and land scarcity
- campus-capacity delivery
- thermal and electrical complexity
- skilled-labor scarcity
- route and cabling density
- permitting and schedule risk

That bottleneck map is much closer to how the build cycle actually behaves.

### 4. The same AI wave monetizes through different reporting signals

The most important point across the packets is that the demand wave appears in different ways:

- `Equinix`: interconnections, MRR, annualized gross bookings
- `Digital Realty`: backlog, hyperscale leases, annualized rent bookings
- `Vertiv`: order growth, book-to-bill, backlog, free cash flow
- `Quanta`: RPO, total backlog, self-perform capacity
- `EMCOR`: RPO, project awards, facilities and interior complexity
- `Dycom`: backlog, book-to-bill, route density, inside-plant extension

So even when the social story is one thing, the investable signal is not one thing.

### 5. The digital economy is mechanically denser than the interface suggests

The user sees:

- AI tools
- cloud applications
- streaming
- low-latency enterprise systems

The archive shows the physical requirement stack underneath:

- a powered campus
- a thermal and electrical environment
- substations and grid connections
- interior controls, cooling, and mechanical systems
- fiber routes and inside-facility cabling

That is exactly the type of blind spot the `AnnualReports` browse tree does not explain.

## Best comparisons

- `Equinix` versus `Digital Realty`: interconnection-density ownership versus hyperscale-capacity ownership
- `Vertiv` versus `EMCOR`: critical systems and equipment versus interior installation and building-uptime execution
- `Quanta` versus `Dycom`: electric-grid and mission-critical construction versus communications-route and inside-plant deployment
- `Digital Realty` versus `Quanta`: capacity-owner backlog versus field-execution backlog
