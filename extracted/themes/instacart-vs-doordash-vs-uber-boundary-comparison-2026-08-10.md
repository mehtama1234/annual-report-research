# Instacart Versus DoorDash Versus Uber Boundary Comparison

Date baseline: 2026-08-10

## Scope

This memo is the next direct comparison built from the marketplace and local-demand coordination blind-spot lane.

It focuses on the cleanest three-way split inside app-mediated local demand:

- `Instacart`
- `DoorDash`
- `Uber`

The useful question is not whether they all deliver things.

The useful question is what kind of local demand each company is actually organizing:

- household basket demand
- merchant-led convenience demand
- movement and multi-surface wallet demand

## Executive read

The three companies sit in the same broad local-demand family, but they are not the same system.

- `Instacart` is the retailer-embedded and grocery-specific coordination case
- `DoorDash` is the merchant-density and convenience-ordering case
- `Uber` is the broader movement, delivery, and wallet-thickening case

That means the best current ordering is:

1. `Instacart`: grocery-demand and retailer-enablement coordinator
2. `DoorDash`: local-commerce and merchant-logistics coordinator
3. `Uber`: movement-and-wallet coordinator

This is not a quality ranking.

It is an ordering by subtype clarity inside the local-demand blind spot.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| `Instacart` | grocery basket assembly, substitution help, household replenishment, and price-visible digital grocery convenience | grocery-demand routing, retailer integrations, retail-media surfaces, first-party shopping data, and white-label retailer tools | advertising, retailer software, in-store tools, enterprise integrations, and AI-enabled retailer services | grocery-demand and retailer-enablement coordinator |

## What the filings now show

### 1. `Instacart` is the most retailer-embedded case

Instacart is the clearest example where the interface is only part of the story.

The packet shows the company also operates as retailer infrastructure through:

- `Storefront`
- `Storefront Pro`
- in-store technologies
- data products
- AI solutions
- retail media

The FY `2025` to Q2 `2026` chain makes that structural depth explicit:

- FY `2025` GTV of `$37.224B`
- FY `2025` revenue of `$3.742B`
- FY `2025` advertising and other revenue of `$1.065B`
- more than `2,200` retail banners and nearly `100,000` locations in `2025`
- more than `380` grocers using `Storefront` technology at year-end `2025`
- Q2 `2026` advertising and other revenue of `$297M`

That is why Instacart is first in this comparison.

It is the cleanest example of a business that would be badly misunderstood if treated as generic software, generic ecommerce, or generic delivery.

### 2. `DoorDash` is the strongest merchant-density convenience system

DoorDash is the clearest case where the company sits between consumers and local merchants at high frequency.

Its filings show the relationship thickening through:

- local merchant density
- convenience habit
- memberships
- courier coordination
- merchant tooling

The operating scale makes the subtype obvious:

- FY `2025` revenue of `$13.717B`
- Q2 `2026` Marketplace GOV of `$33.1B`
- Q2 `2026` orders of `970M`
- over `56M` monthly active users at year-end `2025`
- over `35M` DashPass, Wolt+, and Deliveroo Plus members at year-end `2025`

DoorDash is therefore not best read as a food-delivery app or an internet-service provider.

It is a local-commerce coordination and merchant-logistics system with a strong convenience membership layer.

### 3. `Uber` is the broadest wallet and movement layer

Uber differs from both because movement remains the anchor demand surface.

The platform then widens that relationship across delivery, membership, and local commerce.

The FY `2025` to Q2 `2026` chain shows that breadth clearly:

- FY `2025` gross bookings of `$193B`
- Q2 `2026` gross bookings of `$58.0B`
- Q2 `2026` MAPCs of `208M`
- Q2 `2026` trips of `3.9B`
- Q1 `2026` Uber One members of `50M`
- those members driving roughly half of gross bookings across Mobility and Delivery

That makes Uber the broadest consumer wallet relationship in this trio.

But it is third in this comparison because the subtype is wider and therefore less narrow than the retailer-embedded Instacart case or the merchant-density DoorDash case.

## The most useful boundary split

The cleanest way to separate the three is:

### `Instacart`: retailer operating depth first

Instacart matters most when the question is who owns grocery interface plus retailer-enablement depth.

The distinguishing asset is not only demand aggregation.

It is the combination of:

- household basket demand
- retailer software
- retail media
- in-store enablement
- first-party grocery data

### `DoorDash`: convenience frequency and merchant routing first

DoorDash matters most when the question is who owns recurring local convenience behavior across merchants.

The distinguishing asset is:

- merchant density
- order frequency
- local discovery
- courier coordination
- membership-thickened convenience habit

### `Uber`: movement and wallet depth first

Uber matters most when the question is who owns the broadest everyday local-demand relationship.

The distinguishing asset is:

- repeated movement
- rides plus delivery attachment
- membership
- supply density
- one-account wallet behavior across local-demand surfaces

## What each company proves

### `Instacart`

`Instacart` proves that digital grocery is no longer only a consumer checkout interface.

It is also a retailer technology, data, and advertising layer.

### `DoorDash`

`DoorDash` proves that local-commerce ordering and merchant dependence on platform traffic-routing can become durable everyday infrastructure.

### `Uber`

`Uber` proves that repeated local movement can be the anchor for a wider wallet-like coordination layer spanning transportation, delivery, and future supply expansion.

## Highest-confidence conclusions

- `Instacart` is the clearest grocery-demand and retailer-enablement blind-spot case in the archive, especially because AnnualReports does not present a clean issuer page for it as of `2026-08-10`
- `DoorDash` is the clearest merchant-density and convenience-habit coordination case, not a plain delivery app and not a useful fit for legacy internet-service labeling
- `Uber` is the strongest movement-and-wallet coordination case, not a useful fit for plain application-software labeling
- the practical comparison is not delivery versus non-delivery, but retailer enablement versus merchant-logistics coordination versus movement-and-wallet coordination
- the right current order is `Instacart` first on subtype distinctiveness, `DoorDash` second on local-commerce clarity, and `Uber` third on broad wallet-scale coordination
