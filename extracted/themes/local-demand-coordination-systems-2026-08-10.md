# Local-Demand Coordination Systems

Date baseline: 2026-08-10

## Scope

This memo covers a narrower blind spot inside the broader marketplace lane.

`DoorDash`, `Uber`, and `Instacart` all sit inside app-mediated local demand, but they should not be treated as one generic delivery or software category.

They are different systems for coordinating everyday physical-world demand.

## Executive read

The important question is not whether these companies are delivery apps.

The important questions are:

- what demand surface they own
- how often that surface repeats
- whether the core counterparty is a merchant, a rider, a retailer, or a household basket
- how deeply the company is embedded in physical execution
- where the second monetization layer actually sits

The current archive supports a cleaner three-part split:

- `DoorDash` is the local-commerce and merchant-logistics coordinator
- `Uber` is the movement-and-wallet coordinator
- `Instacart` is the grocery-demand and retailer-enablement coordinator

That is a more useful category system than `Application Software` or `Internet Service Providers`.

## Why this lane matters

`AnnualReports` flattens these names in ways that hide the real economic comparison:

- `DoorDash` sits in `Technology / Internet Service Providers`
- `Uber` sits in `Technology / Application Software`
- `Instacart` has no clean discoverable issuer page on the site at all as of `2026-08-10`

Those labels are filing-locator artifacts.

They do not tell you:

- whose demand is being routed
- what repeat behavior is being trained
- how far each company extends into merchant or retailer infrastructure
- whether membership deepens convenience, wallet capture, or traffic density

## What each system really means

### 1. DoorDash: local-commerce and merchant-logistics coordinator

`DoorDash` is the strongest case where the platform sits between local demand and merchant fulfillment at very high frequency.

Its core asset is not broad search.

It is:

- convenience habit
- local merchant density
- membership-driven repeat behavior
- delivery coordination
- merchant tooling

The FY `2025` to Q2 `2026` chain makes that plain:

- FY `2025` revenue of `$13.717B`
- Q2 `2026` Marketplace GOV of `$33.1B`
- Q2 `2026` orders of `970M`
- over `56M` monthly active users at year-end `2025`
- over `35M` DashPass, Wolt+, and Deliveroo Plus members at year-end `2025`

The best way to read DoorDash is as local-commerce infrastructure with an app front end.

Restaurants remain central, but grocery and retail widening matter because they make the convenience relationship more ambient and more frequent.

### 2. Uber: movement-and-wallet coordinator

`Uber` is the broader but less merchant-specific local-demand case.

Its coordination layer spans:

- rides
- delivery
- riders
- drivers and couriers
- memberships
- future autonomous-vehicle supply

The key difference from `DoorDash` is that Uber starts from movement rather than meal ordering.

That produces a different type of repeat behavior:

- transport frequency
- app habituation
- cross-surface wallet use
- movement plus delivery attachment

The core evidence:

- FY `2025` gross bookings of `$193B`
- Q2 `2026` gross bookings of `$58.0B`
- Q2 `2026` MAPCs of `208M`
- Q2 `2026` trips of `3.9B`
- Q1 `2026` Uber One members of `50M`
- those members driving roughly half of gross bookings across Mobility and Delivery

That makes Uber the strongest wallet-like local-demand interface in this subgroup.

The relationship is not just about one category.

It is about using one app to mediate a wider share of everyday movement and fulfillment.

### 3. Instacart: grocery-demand and retailer-enablement coordinator

`Instacart` is the most retailer-embedded and grocery-specific case.

It matters because the company is not only routing consumer demand.

It is also becoming part of retailer operating infrastructure through:

- `Storefront`
- `Storefront Pro`
- in-store technologies
- data products
- AI solutions
- retail media

The FY `2025` to Q2 `2026` chain is unusually clear:

- FY `2025` GTV of `$37.224B`
- FY `2025` revenue of `$3.742B`
- FY `2025` advertising and other revenue of `$1.065B`
- more than `2,200` retail banners and nearly `100,000` locations in `2025`
- more than `380` grocers using `Storefront` technology at year-end `2025`
- Q2 `2026` advertising and other revenue of `$297M`

Instacart therefore differs from both `DoorDash` and `Uber`.

It is less about a generic local-demand wallet and more about becoming a digital operating layer for grocery and retail partners.

## The most important distinctions

### Merchant density versus retailer enablement

- `DoorDash` depends more on merchant density, local discovery, and logistics coordination.
- `Instacart` depends more on retailer integration, white-label software, and retail-media economics.

### Movement frequency versus basket frequency

- `Uber` is anchored by repeated movement and then widened through delivery.
- `Instacart` is anchored by recurring household basket assembly.
- `DoorDash` sits between the two through recurring local convenience and meal-led ordering.

### Consumer wallet depth versus merchant operating depth

- `Uber` has the strongest wallet-style cross-surface relationship in this group.
- `Instacart` has the strongest retailer-operating and merchant-enablement depth.
- `DoorDash` has the strongest direct local-commerce and merchant-logistics middle position.

### Physical embedding

All three are physically embedded, but in different ways:

- `DoorDash` embeds through courier density and merchant fulfillment coordination
- `Uber` embeds through transportation supply plus delivery execution
- `Instacart` embeds through pick, pack, substitution logic, store systems, and retailer software

## Consumer, societal, and industrial read

This subgroup is one of the clearest filing-backed proofs that app-mediated local demand is now an embedded part of everyday life.

But the demand systems are not identical.

- `DoorDash` shows normalized convenience purchasing and rising dependence of local merchants on traffic-routing platforms.
- `Uber` shows that movement itself can become a repeated app-owned interface and then widen into local-demand coordination.
- `Instacart` shows that digital grocery is no longer only a consumer checkout surface; it is also a retailer technology, data, and advertising layer.

The industrial implication is equally important:

- local demand is being intermediated by platforms with real physical-world operating depth
- ads and data are no longer separate from logistics and fulfillment
- retailer and merchant dependence on platform visibility and tooling is becoming a structural feature, not a side effect

## Blind-spot conclusion

The source taxonomy does not have a useful category for these systems.

It can place them in software-like buckets, or omit them, but that does not explain the operating reality.

The better frame is:

- `DoorDash` as local-commerce and merchant-logistics coordination
- `Uber` as movement, delivery, and wallet coordination
- `Instacart` as grocery-demand routing plus retailer enablement

That is the real blind spot this thread should own.

## Highest-confidence conclusions

- local-demand platforms should be compared by what part of everyday demand they coordinate, not by generic tech labels
- `DoorDash`, `Uber`, and `Instacart` are all high-quality blind-spot anchors, but each owns a different choke point
- memberships matter differently across the set: habit density for `DoorDash`, wallet depth for `Uber`, and less centrally than retailer-software and ads for `Instacart`
- `Instacart` is the most retailer-embedded and media-enabled case
- `Uber` is the broadest cross-surface consumer wallet case
- `DoorDash` is the clearest pure local-commerce and merchant-logistics case
