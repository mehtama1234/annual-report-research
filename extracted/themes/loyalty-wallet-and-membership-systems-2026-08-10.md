## Executive read

`AnnualReports` can tell you that `Costco` is a discount-variety store, `Marriott` is lodging, `Caesars` is a resort and casino, and `American Express` is credit services.

It cannot tell you that all four are monetizing a repeat relationship through some form of membership, status, stored value, wallet continuity, or partner-funded loyalty economics.

That is the real blind spot.

The archive now supports a stronger claim:

- loyalty is not one business model
- wallet behavior is not confined to payments companies
- membership is not just a retail club feature
- the deeper pattern is relationship monetization layered on top of repeated behavior

The repeated behavior differs by sector:

- coffee visits
- quick-service food occasions
- warehouse shopping
- same-day retail convenience
- airline travel
- branded stays
- resort visits and wagering
- premium card spend
- low-friction fitness attendance

But the economic logic converges:

- identify the customer
- increase repeat usage
- make the behavior easier or more status-reinforcing
- widen the economics around the same customer over time

## Why this is a blind spot in the source taxonomy

The `AnnualReports` browse tree does not group companies by:

- stored-value systems
- paid membership systems
- points and status currencies
- wallet continuity across physical and digital channels
- co-brand and partner-funded loyalty economics

So the browse tree obscures one of the most useful cross-sector comparisons in the repo:

- `Starbucks` uses stored value, Stars, and deferred revenue
- `McDonald's` uses loyalty-member sales and active-user scale
- `Costco` uses membership fees and trusted value access
- `Target` uses membership, same-day convenience, retail media, and marketplace widening
- `Delta` uses miles, co-brand economics, and partner remuneration
- `Marriott` uses loyalty concentration and co-branded-card funding
- `Caesars` uses rewards identity and one universal digital wallet
- `American Express` uses a premium membership model and closed-loop data network
- `Planet Fitness` uses a low-price recurring access model where dues, annual fees, pricing review, retention work, and a franchise-scaled club base all shape the relationship

Those companies are scattered across different sectors and industries, but the relationship layer is similar enough that they belong in one analytical frame.

## What the archive now says

### 1. Loyalty comes in several structurally different forms

The archive now supports at least five distinct relationship models:

- stored-value routine: `Starbucks`
- digital loyalty-at-scale in everyday food: `McDonald's`
- paid utility membership: `Costco`
- convenience-and-membership retail layer: `Target`
- status currency and co-brand economics: `Delta`
- loyalty-funded distribution and co-brand travel economics: `Marriott`
- universal wallet and rewards continuity: `Caesars`
- premium card-membership and network loop: `American Express`
- low-friction access membership and habit conditioning: `Planet Fitness`

This matters because "loyalty" is too blunt by itself.

The right question is not whether a company has a rewards program.

The better question is what kind of relationship system it has and whether the system is:

- prepaid
- fee-funded
- partner-funded
- status-led
- convenience-led
- app-led
- wallet-linked
- closed-loop and data-rich

### 2. The strongest systems widen the economics beyond the base transaction

Every anchor company in this set does more than monetize the original purchase occasion.

`Starbucks` widens the coffee occasion through stored value, app usage, rewards liabilities, and deferred revenue.

`McDonald's` widens the food occasion through loyalty-member sales, active-user scale, and digital offers inside a franchise-heavy system.

`Costco` widens the retail trip through membership-fee revenue layered on top of merchandise volume and digital convenience.

`Target` widens the retail basket through Target Circle 360, same-day fulfillment, Roundel, and marketplace growth even when the core merchandise line is uneven.

`Delta` widens the trip through SkyMiles, sold miles, American Express remuneration, and premium demand reinforcement.

`Marriott` widens the stay through Marriott Bonvoy concentration, co-branded-card economics, and loyalty-funded distribution.

`Caesars` widens the visit through a rewards identity that follows the customer across resorts, sportsbook, racebook, iGaming, and one digital wallet.

`American Express` widens card spend through annual fees, merchant relationships, premium benefits, travel and dining partnerships, and a closed-loop network that creates more product and data leverage than a plain issuer model.

`Planet Fitness` widens the exercise occasion through recurring dues, annual fees, a broad club network, franchise scale, and a welcoming low-intimidation brand that keeps the barrier to repeat participation unusually low.

This is one of the clearest archive-wide business-quality markers.

The better models are not just attracting demand. They are thickening the relationship.

### 3. The relationship system can be cultural as well as financial

The cultural read matters here because the relationship layer is not only mechanical.

`Costco` shows a membership ritual built on trust, value, and belonging.

`Starbucks` shows a daily-place and app-routine behavior where preload and rewards become part of the habit.

`McDonald's` shows that mass-market value behavior can still be digitally organized at enormous loyalty scale.

`Delta` and `Marriott` show that status, upgrades, branded familiarity, and co-brand participation can become part of identity as well as revenue architecture.

`Caesars` shows a more explicit tier-and-wallet entertainment identity where visit behavior and digital wagering feed one another.

`American Express` shows that card membership itself can function as a premium consumer identity system rather than only a payment tool.

`Planet Fitness` shows a different kind of membership identity where the point is not status, travel perks, or stored value. It is affordable permission to keep showing up without embarrassment, complexity, or premium positioning.

That means the archive can treat loyalty and membership not just as CRM features, but as recurring cultural interfaces.

### 4. The best comparison is by relationship type, not by sector

The archive is now stronger if it compares:

- `Costco` with `Delta`, because both monetize belonging and repeat use through a formal membership or status layer
- `Starbucks` with `American Express`, because both use preload or membership structure to pull the customer into a higher-frequency closed loop
- `McDonald's` with `Target`, because both show a mass-market operator widening the relationship through digital engagement and convenience rather than only premium identity
- `Marriott` with `Caesars`, because both show travel and destination demand reinforced by rewards continuity and cross-surface monetization
- `Planet Fitness` with `Costco`, because both monetize recurring access through a paid membership layer, but one is basket-and-stock-up utility while the other is low-friction bodily maintenance and self-improvement

That is exactly the kind of comparison the source taxonomy does not help produce.

### 5. The archive now has enough evidence to separate thin and thick relationship systems

Some operators mainly have traffic, product, or price.

This set shows the stronger condition: a relationship layer that is disclosed clearly enough to matter in the business model.

High-confidence examples already in the repo include:

- `Starbucks`: unredeemed card balances and Stars sit in deferred revenue
- `McDonald's`: trailing-twelve-month loyalty-member sales reached nearly `$37B` in `2025` and over `$40B` by `Q2 2026`, with nearly `220M` 90-day active users
- `Costco`: `2025` membership-fee revenue reached `$5.323B`
- `Target`: membership revenue more than doubled year over year in `Q4 2025`, while same-day delivery and marketplace also grew strongly
- `Delta`: `2025` American Express remuneration reached `$8.2B`
- `Marriott`: in `2025`, about `75%` of U.S. room nights and `68%` of global room nights were booked by loyalty members
- `Caesars`: a universal digital wallet now connects sportsbook, racebook, iGaming, and Caesars Rewards
- `American Express`: `2025` revenue reached `$72.2B`, with management explicitly framing the model around premium card membership, merchant relationships, and a global closed-loop network
- `Planet Fitness`: full-year `2025` revenue increased `12.1%` to about `$1.3B`, the company ended `2025` with about `20.8M` members and `2,896` clubs, and `Q1 2026` management paused a planned national Black Card price increase while working to improve retention and member growth

These are not soft branding signals. They are operating-structure signals.

## Working comparison frame

| Relationship type | Company | Core repeated behavior | Attached economic layer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stored-value daily routine | Starbucks | repeat drink purchase and app-led ordering | preload balances, Stars, deferred revenue, digital engagement |
| Loyalty-at-scale food routine | McDonald's | repeat everyday food occasions | loyalty-member sales, active users, digital offers, franchise throughput |
| Paid utility membership | Costco | repeat warehouse trips and stock-up behavior | membership fees, trust-led retention, digital convenience |
| Convenience-and-membership retail layer | Target | repeat household and discretionary retail trips | paid delivery utility, retail media, marketplace, same-day fulfillment |
| Travel status and partner-funded loyalty currency | Delta | repeat travel and premium trip behavior | sold miles, partner remuneration, co-brand economics, premium reinforcement |
| Branded-stay loyalty distribution system | Marriott | repeat hotel stays and travel trust | loyalty concentration, co-brand fees, funded distribution, owner-demand support |
| Destination rewards and wallet loop | Caesars | repeat resort visits and digital wagering | tiering, cross-market spend capture, universal wallet continuity |
| Premium card-membership network | American Express | repeat card spend, travel, dining, and premium benefits usage | annual fees, merchant economics, network data, partner ecosystem, premium spend mix |
| Low-friction access membership and habit conditioning | Planet Fitness | repeat fitness attendance and self-maintenance | monthly dues, annual fees, franchise scale, broad access, and retention-sensitive pricing |

## What this means for the broader research project

- The archive should treat loyalty, wallet, and membership systems as a first-class cross-sector lane, not as scattered company attributes.
- This lane materially improves the project because it ties together consumer behavior, cultural identity, relationship ownership, and second-layer monetization in one frame.
- It also sharpens a bigger point about the current economy: many of the better consumer-facing models are becoming relationship businesses with products or trips attached.
- This is one of the cleanest places where the source taxonomy is not wrong, but incomplete in a way that matters analytically.

## Highest-confidence conclusions

- Loyalty is now part of operating structure, not only marketing language, in several of the archive's strongest packets.
- Membership can monetize trust and frequency without looking like software or payments.
- Wallet behavior shows up well outside Financial, especially in restaurants, travel, and gaming.
- Status, belonging, convenience, and stored value are recurring economic mechanisms across sectors, not isolated category quirks.
- A relationship system can be thick without looking premium if the repeated behavior is routine enough and the fee-access model is simple enough, which is exactly what Planet Fitness adds.
- The right comparison set is no longer "all restaurants" or "all hotels." It is the set of companies that own a repeat relationship strongly enough to widen the customer economics over time.
