# Retail Versus Marketplace Boundary

Date baseline: 2026-08-10

## Scope

This memo separates one of the most persistent `AnnualReports` blind spots: the boundary between retail systems and marketplace or coordination systems.

The source taxonomy can place `Amazon`, `eBay`, `Instacart`, `DoorDash`, `Walmart`, `Target`, `Ulta`, and `Best Buy` into legacy sectors and industries.

It does not explain when a company is still mainly a retailer with attached marketplace layers, when it is a marketplace with retail-like demand, or when it is a coordination layer sitting between merchants and the customer.

## Executive read

The useful distinction is not "stores versus online."

The useful distinction is where the operating center of gravity sits:

- inventory and merchanting
- owned customer relationship
- third-party seller coordination
- merchant enablement
- logistics routing
- ad and service monetization layered on top of demand

In practical terms, the archive now supports five different boundary cases:

- inventory-led retailers with attached marketplace layers
- utility retailers thickening into interface systems
- retailer-service hybrids with lighter marketplace widening
- marketplace retail without owned inventory
- merchant-enablement and local-demand coordination layers that are no longer best read as retailers at all

That is the blind-spot correction the source taxonomy does not provide.

## Working boundary map

| Boundary type | Core names | What they mainly control | Why they are not well described by source taxonomy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inventory-led retailer with marketplace extension | `Walmart`, `Target`, `Ulta`, `Best Buy` | first-party assortment, stores, fulfillment, loyalty, and a growing second layer | the marketplace or media layer matters, but inventory-led retail still anchors the economics |
| Utility retailer with major ecosystem widening | `Amazon` | the consumer account, demand frequency, fulfillment, seller services, and ad inventory | it still looks retail in demand behavior, but the monetization stack is too wide for a plain retail label |
| Marketplace retail without owned inventory | `eBay`, `Etsy` | buyer traffic, seller supply, trust, discovery, and listing economics | these names can feel retail-like to consumers but economically behave more like thinner coordination systems |
| Retailer-enablement coordination layer | `Instacart` | retailer ecommerce, grocery demand routing, retail media, and store software | it intermediates retail demand without being the merchanting center of gravity |
| Local-demand coordination layer | `DoorDash`, `Uber` | app habit, merchant density, logistics routing, membership, and wallet behavior | these systems sit around retail and restaurants, but the real engine is local-demand coordination rather than retail inventory |

## The main decision test

When deciding whether a company belongs primarily in the retail lane or the marketplace lane, the best first question is:

what does the company control that the customer actually comes back to?

### Mostly retail

Keep the company mainly in the retail lane if the repeated behavior is still anchored in:

- owned assortment
- owned pricing and merchanting
- store or branch footprint
- first-party household basket formation
- category authority inside a merchant-controlled environment

### Mostly marketplace or coordination

Keep the company mainly in the marketplace lane if the repeated behavior is mainly anchored in:

- third-party seller density
- merchant network participation
- demand routing
- trust or listing economics
- logistics coordination
- retailer or merchant enablement

### Mixed cases

Use both lanes when the demand loop is retail-like but the second monetization layer is becoming too important to ignore.

That is the right treatment for `Amazon`, `Walmart`, `Target`, `Ulta`, and `Best Buy`.

## What the current names reveal

### 1. `Amazon` is not just a retailer and not just a marketplace

`Amazon` is the clearest hybrid boundary case in the archive.

The demand loop still looks retail-like:

- frequent household shopping
- repeat replenishment
- account-level utility

But the economics are widened by:

- third-party seller services
- ads
- subscriptions
- fulfillment

So the best read is not "pick one label."

It is "retail demand loop with marketplace and ecosystem monetization thick enough to require a second lane."

### 2. `Walmart` and `Target` are still retailers first

Both companies are widening into media, marketplace, same-day, and membership layers.

But the center of gravity is still:

- first-party household basket
- mass retail footprint
- owned merchanting
- omnichannel utility

That means they belong mainly in the retail lane, with marketplace-like widening as a quality marker rather than as the primary identity.

### 3. `Ulta` and `Best Buy` are retailer-service hybrids, not platform leaders

`Ulta` has loyalty, services, media, and marketplace assortment.

`Best Buy` has services, marketplace support, and ad layers.

But both still depend mainly on merchant-controlled category environments rather than on an external seller network as the primary engine.

So the right read is:

- retailer with widening service and marketplace layers

not:

- full coordination platform

### 4. `eBay` and `Etsy` are retail-adjacent but economically marketplace-first

These companies feel retail-like because consumers browse, discover, and purchase goods.

But the economic center of gravity is different:

- seller supply
- listing depth
- trust
- discovery
- take-rate and ad economics

That keeps them closer to the marketplace lane than to inventory-led retail even when the consumer experience overlaps.

### 5. `Instacart` is not best read as a retailer

It sits inside grocery demand.

But the archive evidence shows the stronger description is:

- retailer-enablement layer
- grocery-demand coordinator
- retail-media and data layer

It matters because it routes demand around retailers rather than replacing them as the main merchanting environment.

### 6. `DoorDash` and `Uber` are convenience infrastructure, not retail

Both touch restaurant, grocery, and retail demand.

But the customer relationship is returning mainly for:

- convenience
- routing
- app habit
- local fulfillment
- wallet-like account behavior

That makes them coordination systems first, with retail adjacency rather than retail identity.

## The most useful boundary splits

The archive now supports these practical distinctions:

### Retail first, marketplace second

- `Walmart`
- `Target`
- `Ulta`
- `Best Buy`

### Retail and marketplace both matter at scale

- `Amazon`

### Marketplace first, retail-like demand second

- `eBay`
- `Etsy`

### Retailer-enablement or local-demand coordination first

- `Instacart`
- `DoorDash`
- `Uber`

## Why this matters for thread ownership

This is one of the clearest places where the blind-spot thread should keep first claim.

A normal sector thread can packetize:

- retailers
- software companies
- internet companies
- travel companies

But it will usually miss the structural comparison across:

- owned inventory versus third-party supply
- merchanting versus coordination
- retail-media versus seller-services monetization
- store footprint versus logistics routing
- category authority versus trust and listing density

That is exactly the kind of comparison the source taxonomy does not organize well.

## Highest-confidence conclusions

- a marketplace layer does not automatically make a company marketplace-first
- inventory control, merchanting control, and basket ownership still keep `Walmart`, `Target`, `Ulta`, and `Best Buy` mainly inside retail
- `Amazon` is the clearest hybrid case where retail demand and marketplace monetization are both too important to reduce to one label
- `eBay` and `Etsy` are better understood as marketplace systems even though the user-facing experience looks retail-like
- `Instacart`, `DoorDash`, and `Uber` sit outside retail-first logic because coordination and routing matter more than owned inventory
