# Starbucks Versus Costco Versus American Express Boundary Comparison

Date baseline: 2026-08-10

## Scope

This memo is the next direct comparison built from the loyalty, wallet, and membership blind-spot lane.

It focuses on the cleanest three-way split inside formal repeat-relationship systems:

- `Starbucks`
- `Costco`
- `American Express`

The useful question is not whether all three have loyal customers.

The useful question is what kind of formal relationship each company actually monetizes:

- prepaid stored-value routine
- fee-backed utility membership
- premium closed-loop wallet membership

## Executive read

The three companies sit in the same broad relationship-economics family, but they do not monetize the relationship in the same way.

- `Starbucks` is the stored-value and app-routine case
- `Costco` is the fee-funded belonging-and-value-access case
- `American Express` is the premium card-membership and closed-loop wallet case

That means the best current ordering is:

1. `Starbucks`: stored-value daily routine
2. `Costco`: paid utility membership
3. `American Express`: premium card-membership network

This is not a quality ranking.

It is an ordering by relationship form, moving from prepaid everyday habit to fee-backed access to full premium wallet membership.

## The core comparison

| Company | What the customer mainly returns for | What the company mainly controls | What widens the model beyond the base transaction | Best current read |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `Starbucks` | repeat beverage purchase, app-led ordering, and a familiar daily routine | branded everyday occasion, stored value, rewards balances, app behavior, and coffeehouse visit frequency | preload balances, Stars, deferred revenue, digital ordering, and higher-frequency habit reinforcement | stored-value daily routine |
| `Costco` | warehouse trips, trusted low-price access, and stock-up behavior | fee membership, trusted access to value, warehouse scale, and repeat household shopping behavior | membership-fee revenue, renewal behavior, digital convenience, and large-basket repeat utility | paid utility membership |
| `American Express` | repeat card spend, premium benefits usage, travel and dining attachment, and status-linked membership identity | premium card relationship, merchant network, closed-loop data, and payment behavior across a broad spend wallet | annual fees, merchant economics, network data, partner ecosystem, and premium spend mix | premium card-membership network |

## What the filings now show

### 1. `Starbucks` is the clearest prepaid routine case

Starbucks is the narrowest but most behaviorally repetitive system in the trio.

The filing set shows the company explicitly tying:

- card activation
- app behavior
- Starbucks Rewards enrollment
- unredeemed stored-value balances
- unredeemed Stars

to deferred revenue and repeat visit behavior.

The FY `2025` to Q3 `2026` chain shows both the relationship thickness and the operating strain:

- fiscal `2025` net revenues of `$37.2B`
- fiscal `2025` operating income of `$2.9B`
- Q1 `2026` net revenues of `$9.9B`
- Q2 `2026` net revenues of `$9.5B`
- Q3 `2026` net revenues described as about `$9.3B`

That is why Starbucks is first in this comparison.

It is the clearest case where the formal relationship is not a membership fee or a travel-status system, but a preload-and-routine loop embedded inside everyday physical visits.

### 2. `Costco` is the clearest fee-for-access utility case

Costco differs because the relationship is less about preload and less about premium status.

It is built around upfront paid access to trusted value.

The FY `2025` to Q3 `2026` chain makes that structure explicit:

- fiscal `2025` net sales of `$269.9B`
- fiscal `2025` total revenue of `$275.235B`
- fiscal `2025` membership fees of `$5.323B`
- fiscal `2025` net income of `$8.099B`
- Q3 `2026` net sales of `$69.15B`
- Q3 `2026` digitally-enabled comparable sales growth of `21.5%`

Costco is second because it is the clearest proof that a retailer can own a very thick relationship without looking like a classic wallet, points, or payments platform.

The core trust mechanism is fee-backed access to value, not ongoing preload or premium closed-loop spend capture.

### 3. `American Express` is the broadest premium wallet case

American Express is the widest and most economically layered relationship in the set.

Its filing language is unusually explicit that the operating model is the `Membership Model`, built from:

- premium card products
- merchant and partner relationships
- a global closed-loop network
- first-party data

The FY `2025` to 2Q `2026` chain shows the scale clearly:

- FY `2025` revenue of `$72.2B`
- FY `2025` net income of `$10.8B`
- FY `2025` billed business of `$1.67T`
- `12.5M` new proprietary cards in `2025`
- 2Q `2026` revenue of `$19.6B`
- 2Q `2026` EPS of `$4.53`

AmEx is third because this ordering moves from narrower routine systems toward the broadest premium wallet architecture.

It is the clearest case where the relationship itself becomes the product, not just the coffee occasion or the warehouse trip attached to it.

## The most useful boundary split

The cleanest way to separate the three is:

### `Starbucks`: prepaid habit and app-loop first

Starbucks matters most when the question is who turns everyday small-ticket frequency into a formal stored-value and deferred-revenue relationship.

The distinguishing asset is:

- preload
- app-led ordering
- Stars
- daily or near-daily routine behavior

### `Costco`: fee-backed belonging and access first

Costco matters most when the question is who monetizes trust and repeat traffic through a paid access layer rather than a payment network or points-heavy wallet.

The distinguishing asset is:

- membership fees
- renewal behavior
- trusted value access
- warehouse-trip repetition

### `American Express`: premium spend wallet and network first

American Express matters most when the question is who owns the richest formal wallet relationship in the set.

The distinguishing asset is:

- premium fee-paying card membership
- closed-loop network data
- merchant acceptance economics
- travel and dining ecosystem attachment

## What each company proves

### `Starbucks`

`Starbucks` proves that an everyday physical routine can carry a real stored-value and rewards-liability system underneath it.

### `Costco`

`Costco` proves that paid membership can monetize trust, belonging, and repeat household utility without needing a classic payments or travel-status architecture.

### `American Express`

`American Express` proves that premium membership, payments, network data, and partner ecosystems can be one formal relationship model rather than separate businesses.

## Highest-confidence conclusions

- `Starbucks` is the clearest stored-value and deferred-revenue routine case, not just a restaurant loyalty program
- `Costco` is the clearest fee-backed access and value-trust membership case, not just a strong retailer with repeat customers
- `American Express` is the clearest premium wallet and closed-loop membership case, not just a generic card issuer
- the practical comparison is not restaurant versus retail versus financial, but preload routine versus paid access versus premium wallet membership
- the right current order is `Starbucks` first on stored-value routine clarity, `Costco` second on fee-backed access clarity, and `American Express` third on broad premium wallet architecture
