# Thick vs. Thin Relationship Systems

Date baseline: 2026-08-10

## Scope

This memo extends the blind-spot work by separating consumer-facing models with a thick disclosed relationship layer from models where demand is real but the relationship remains thinner, more implicit, or more dependent on price, product, traffic, or format execution.

## Executive read

One of the easiest mistakes in this archive would be to assume that every sticky or high-performing consumer business is converging toward the same kind of loyalty, wallet, or membership model.

The evidence does not support that.

Some companies have thick relationship systems:

- they identify the customer directly
- they disclose a formal member, wallet, or rewards layer
- they widen the economics around the same customer through fees, deferred revenue, partner funding, co-brand economics, or cross-channel continuity

Other companies still have durable demand, but the relationship is thinner:

- the customer comes for price, product, convenience, assortment, or discovery
- repeat behavior is real, but the monetization layer is less formalized
- the economics still sit closer to traffic, inventory turn, merchandise margin, ancillary attachment, or supply density

That difference is one of the clearest business-quality distinctions that `AnnualReports` does not organize well.

## The core distinction

### 1. Thick systems disclose the relationship as part of the operating model

The thick side is not just "good brand" or "high repeat use."

The thick side is where the filing set makes the relationship infrastructure visible.

Examples:

- `Starbucks` discloses stored value, Stars, and deferred revenue.
- `Costco` discloses membership-fee revenue as a major economic layer.
- `Delta` discloses sold miles and `2025` American Express remuneration of `$8.2B`.
- `Marriott` discloses extraordinary loyalty concentration, with about `75%` of U.S. room nights and `68%` of global room nights booked by loyalty members in `2025`.
- `Caesars` discloses one rewards identity and one universal wallet spanning resorts and digital wagering.

These are thick systems because the relationship is measurable, monetized, and strategically central.

### 2. Thin systems can still be strong businesses

Thin does not mean weak.

It means the relationship layer is lighter, less financialized, or less explicitly monetized.

Examples:

- `Ross` wins through branded bargains, traffic, merchandise execution, and store experience rather than a formal membership loop.
- `Dollar General` wins through consumables density, neighborhood convenience, and trip productivity more than through a large owned-interface layer.
- `Burlington` wins through bargain discovery, opportunistic buying, and store traffic rather than wallet or media economics.
- `Frontier` keeps some loyalty, subscription, and co-brand features, but the core economics still live in low fares, ancillary revenue per passenger, stage-length management, and fleet productivity.
- `eBay` has sticky behavior, but the stickiness is category-, trust-, and enthusiast-led rather than built around a thick paid-membership bundle.

The point is not that these models lack repeat behavior.

The point is that their repeat behavior is monetized more indirectly.

## What the archive now says

### 1. The thick-versus-thin divide is more useful than a simple digital-versus-physical divide

The wrong comparison would be:

- digital companies are thick
- physical companies are thin

The repo already disproves that.

`Costco` is physical and thick because membership fees are central.

`Caesars` is physical-plus-digital and thick because rewards identity and wallet continuity are explicit.

`Ross` and `Burlington` are physical and thinner because the relationship mostly runs through value and discovery rather than through formalized member economics.

`eBay` is digital but still thinner than `Amazon Prime`, `Delta SkyMiles`, or `Caesars Rewards` style systems because its stickiness is driven more by category behavior, seller density, and trust than by one dominant bundled relationship product.

### 2. Thin systems usually rely on one of five other mechanisms

The current archive points to several recurring thinner alternatives:

- value and price trust
- convenience and proximity
- merchandise discovery
- enthusiast or category culture
- ancillary attachment to a low upfront price

Examples:

- `Ross`: value and branded-bargain discovery
- `Dollar General`: quick low-ticket replenishment near home
- `Burlington`: off-price discovery and store density
- `eBay`: collectibles, resale, seller supply, and trust
- `Frontier`: low base fare plus aggressive attached monetization

These are still real systems. They are just not as thickly formalized as member or wallet models.

### 3. Thick systems usually widen margins or resilience through a second explicit layer

The thick examples repeatedly show a second layer that the filings discuss directly:

- prepaid balances and deferred revenue
- membership fees
- co-brand remuneration
- loyalty-funded distribution
- cross-surface wallet continuity

That gives thick systems a clearer mechanism for:

- higher visibility
- better customer recognition
- more pricing leverage
- more partner monetization
- stronger resilience when the base product category is pressured

That does not guarantee better results every quarter, but it usually makes the model easier to analyze as a relationship business rather than only as a product or traffic business.

### 4. Thin systems can still have excellent economics, but the drivers are different

`Ross` and `Burlington` show that a thinner formal relationship can still produce excellent results when the format advantage is real.

`Dollar General` shows that convenience, consumables mix, and store maintenance can matter more than wallet depth in a neighborhood essentials chain.

`eBay` shows that a marketplace can remain sticky through supply depth and enthusiast culture without becoming a membership-heavy ecosystem.

`Frontier` shows the sharper edge of the distinction. The consumer relationship exists, but the filing set keeps forcing attention back to fleet structure, ancillary intensity, and unit revenue design rather than to a thick customer-ownership engine.

So the thick-versus-thin distinction is not a ranking of good versus bad businesses.

It is a distinction about where the economics actually live.

### 5. Cultural stickiness is not the same thing as relationship thickness

This is another important correction.

`Ross` and `Burlington` both have real cultural stickiness through bargain hunting.

`eBay` has real cultural stickiness through recommerce, collectibles, and enthusiast demand.

`Frontier` has a real behavioral niche in budget mobility.

But those are not the same thing as a thick wallet, membership, or status system.

The archive therefore needs both concepts:

- cultural or behavioral stickiness
- relationship-system thickness

Some companies have both.

Some have only one.

## What this means for the broader research project

- The archive should not flatten all recurring consumer behavior into one loyalty story.
- A company can be sticky without having a thick wallet or membership layer.
- A company can also have a very thick relationship system even if the core product is ordinary.
- This distinction improves cross-company analysis because it clarifies whether the model is primarily monetizing customer ownership, format advantage, cultural demand, or operational throughput.

## Highest-confidence conclusions

- Thick relationship systems are now visible across restaurants, retail, travel, gaming, and payments.
- Thin systems remain economically important and often culturally sticky, but their economics are usually driven more by traffic, price, assortment, or operational design than by formalized member monetization.
- The most useful business-quality question is not "does the company have loyalty?" but "how thick is the relationship layer, and where do the economics really live?"
- This is a blind spot in the `AnnualReports` taxonomy because sector and industry labels do not reveal relationship thickness at all.
