## What changed

- Demand visibility: Alliant moved from describing major growth agreements in `2025` to showing about `3.4 GW` of contracted demand by Q1 `2026`, which management says drives roughly a `60%` increase in projected demand by `2031` from `2025` levels.
- Scale of the story: this is a smaller utility than Duke, AEP, or NextEra, but the same power-demand pattern is visible. Alliant’s peak-hour demand base is about `5,465 MW`, so incremental data-center load matters more visibly to the overall system.
- Capital posture: Alliant’s Q1 `2026` presentation framed a `$13.4B` four-year capital plan for `2026` through `2029`, with about `12%` investment CAGR and additional upside from Iowa resource-plan refreshes.
- Guidance posture: management closed `2025` with ongoing EPS of `$3.22`, affirmed `2026` ongoing EPS guidance of `$3.36` to `$3.46`, and by Q2 said results were trending in the upper half of that range.
- Regulatory framing: unlike a broad power-platform story, Alliant’s case is heavily tied to Iowa and Wisconsin regulatory constructs, individual-customer rates, advance ratemaking, and keeping retail electric rates below national averages.
- Customer-affordability framing: management repeatedly argues that growth is being handled in a disciplined way, with large-load customers paying their own way and Iowa base rates remaining stable through the decade.

## Open questions

- How much of Alliant’s contracted load pipeline converts into durable demand on the original timeline, especially for the later-stage `2029` to `2031` ramp projects?
- Can the company keep the affordability argument intact if the Iowa resource-plan refresh pulls forward more capital than the current `$13.4B` plan assumes?
- Does Alliant remain a relatively lower-risk regulated-growth story, or does concentrated large-load exposure make execution outcomes more volatile than the market currently assumes?
