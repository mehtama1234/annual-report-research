## What changed

- Demand: the cleanest change is load-growth acceleration. The company moved from `56 GW` of signed incremental load by `2030` in the Q4 2025 release to `63 GW` in Q1 2026 and `69 GW` in Q2 2026.
- Capital intensity: AEP moved from a `$72B` five-year capital plan at year-end 2025 to `$78B` by Q1 2026, while still pointing to more than `$10B` of additional opportunity beyond the base plan.
- Guidance: management began `2026` with operating EPS guidance of `$6.15` to `$6.45`, reaffirmed it in Q1, then raised it in Q2 to `$6.25` to `$6.55`.
- Business mix: this is still a regulated utility system first, but the earnings story is increasingly tied to transmission buildout, large-load interconnection, generation adequacy, and affordability engineering rather than only traditional retail-electric demand.
- Regulatory / affordability tone: management repeatedly ties growth to affordability. By Q2 it was emphasizing up to `$16B` of expected customer cost offsets plus about `$1.4B` of estimated customer benefits from DOE loans and grants.
- Physical constraints: the AEP materials make power availability, gas-turbine procurement, transmission approvals, and regulatory timing central strategic variables. This is not a software-style growth story; it is a build-permit-finance-execute utility story.
- Management tone: AEP's tone is expansionary but disciplined. The company is openly leaning into “generational” load growth while arguing that scale, signed contracts, securitization, DOE financing, and regulatory execution are needed to keep rates workable.

## Open questions

- How much of the `69 GW` load queue ultimately converts into durable rate-base growth versus slipping because of generation, permitting, or interconnection constraints?
- Can AEP keep the affordability case intact if capital needs keep rising beyond the current `$78B` plan?
- Does the gas-turbine procurement push become a bottleneck or a moat relative to other utilities serving similar hyperscaler and industrial demand?
