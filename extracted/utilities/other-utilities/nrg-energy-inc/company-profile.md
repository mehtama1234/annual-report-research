## What changed

- Business-model clarity: NRG is the utilities set's cleaner wholesale-plus-retail and large-load commercialization case. It is not a regulated wires utility. It combines a large retail customer book with generation ownership, competitive supply, smart-home exposure, and customer-backed new-build generation.
- Strategic demand framing: management is explicit that large-load growth should be customer-backed. The Bring Your Own Power model is a direct statement that new power investment should be paid for by the incremental large-load customer rather than socialized across the broader system.
- Capital posture: the platform is being reshaped through the LS Power portfolio acquisition, demand-response expansion via CPower, Texas Energy Fund gas projects, and new generation tied to hyperscaler demand.
- Earnings architecture: quarter-to-quarter results are driven by retail load, supply costs, weather, realized capacity prices, generation performance, and acquisition effects. That makes NRG a useful contrast to both regulated utilities and cleaner nuclear-contracting platforms.
- Operating identity: NRG repeatedly describes itself as a customer-first platform serving roughly `8 million` electricity, natural gas, and smart-home customers and supporting that franchise with approximately `25 GW` of generation.

## Open questions

- Does NRG's Bring Your Own Power strategy become a repeatable template for hyperscaler and large-load growth, or does it remain a limited Texas-specific opportunity?
- How much of the LS Power acquisition upside survives once higher interest expense, depreciation, and integration complexity are fully absorbed?
- If weather normalizes and retail margins soften, does the enlarged generation footprint provide enough offset to keep the growth algorithm intact?
