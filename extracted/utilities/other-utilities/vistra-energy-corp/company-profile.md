## What changed

- Business-model clarity: Vistra is the utilities set's merchant and competitive-generation counterexample. It is not primarily a regulated wires story. The company combines a large retail load book with a dispatchable and diversified generation fleet, power-market exposure, and an active hedging program.
- Earnings architecture: quarter-to-quarter results move materially with realized power and capacity prices, hedge marks, acquisition contributions, and fleet performance. That makes Vistra a useful contrast to utilities whose earnings are driven mainly by authorized returns, rate-base growth, and approved capital plans.
- Strategic demand framing: management repeatedly links the company to rising load and reliability needs, but it does so through dispatchable generation, nuclear PPAs, gas-generation acquisitions, and digital-infrastructure partnerships rather than through a classic regulated load-growth and substation narrative.
- Capital posture: the company is clearly in expansion mode. The quarterlies tie the current story to the closed Lotus acquisition, the pending Cogentrix acquisition, Permian Basin gas units, solar projects, and Helix Digital Infrastructure.
- Hedging posture: management repeatedly highlights very high hedge coverage, which is central to how Vistra converts merchant and competitive-generation exposure into a more underwritten forward earnings profile.

## Open questions

- How much of Vistra's current valuation depends on market confidence that merchant-power upside can remain partially de-risked through hedging, nuclear PPAs, and contracted load relationships?
- Does the pending Cogentrix acquisition broaden Vistra's dispatchable growth platform enough to shift it from a merchant counterexample into a more mainstream power-infrastructure compounder?
- If power prices or hedge marks normalize, does Vistra still read as the strongest non-regulated utility expression of data-center and reliability demand?
