## Strongest repeated patterns

1. Channel control is not abstract. The strongest packets show distributors and service intermediaries winning through default workflow position, route density, inventory access, and procurement leverage rather than through ownership of the underlying goods.
2. Institutional demand is one of the cleanest stabilizers in the archive. Foodservice, healthcare, workplace safety, and business uptime needs recur even when the end consumer is cautious.
3. The most valuable middle-layer operators increasingly attach services to product flow. Specialty healthcare support, project logistics, owner relationships, inspection and monitoring, facilities uptime support, field-service coordination, uniforms and safety routes, and digital procurement tools all widen the moat.
4. Working-capital discipline remains central. Inventory timing, backlog conversion, fleet utilization, and cash conversion are not side metrics in this frontier; they are the operating core.
5. Cultural and societal shifts still matter here. Value sensitivity, healthcare complexity, project outsourcing, premium travel demand, and digital infrastructure build-out all show up indirectly through the middle layer before or alongside end-demand companies.

## Best next targets

1. A medical-equipment or medical-supply distributor to widen institutional healthcare replenishment beyond drug channels.
2. A technical-services or protection-services operator to separate compliance-heavy service models from broader route-based workplace support and facilities uptime support.
3. A more cyclical manufacturing or metalworking procurement name such as MSC to sharpen the differences among Fastenal, Ferguson, Grainger, and WESCO.
4. A second hospitality or route-based physical-service operator if the goal is to make the service-layer physical-interface lane more than a single-name proof.
