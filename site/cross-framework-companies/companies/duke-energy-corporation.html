Annual Report Takeaways

  • Duke is a useful third utility because it is a cleaner traditional regulated-utility comparison than NextEra while still showing the same electricity-scarcity and large-load themes now appearing across the archive.
  • Duke’s investor materials describe the company as a Fortune `150` energy holding company headquartered in Charlotte, North Carolina. The electric utilities serve `8.7 million` customers across North Carolina, South Carolina, Florida, Indiana, Ohio, and Kentucky; the system includes about `55,700` megawatts of energy capacity. The natural gas utilities serve about `1.6 million` customers and the company employs approximately `26,400` people.
  • The `2025` annual framing is a regulated-growth utility model centered on load growth, grid modernization, generation expansion, rate recovery, and customer-affordability politics rather than on simple defensive demand.
  • Management said `2025` EPS was `$6.31`, up about `7%` from `2024` and above the midpoint of guidance, then introduced `2026` adjusted EPS guidance of `$6.55` to `$6.80`.
  • The annual materials already tied growth to large-load infrastructure. Duke said it had approximately `4.5 GW` of data-center load secured under electric service agreements, including Microsoft and Amazon, and argued that these costs are not passed through to current customers in a simple way because new growth broadens the cost base.
  • Duke also framed itself as running the largest regulated capital plan in its sector, with management stressing rate recovery, constructive regulation, and keeping rates below the national average while still funding expansion.

Latest Quarter Chain

  • Results: Q2 `2026` reported EPS was `$1.38` and adjusted EPS was `$1.43`, compared with reported and adjusted EPS of `$1.25` in Q2 `2025`.
  • Guidance: management reaffirmed `2026` adjusted EPS guidance of `$6.55` to `$6.80` and the long-term adjusted EPS growth rate of `5%` to `7%` through `2030`, off the `2025` midpoint of `$6.30`.
  • Management message: management said a strong first half, constructive regulatory outcomes, and continued strategic execution positioned the company to deliver within the guidance range.
  • Structural themes: the quarter was framed around reliability investment, regulatory support, and generation build. Duke explicitly said generation build supports growth in its jurisdictions and fuels local economies.
  • Results: Q1 `2026` reported EPS was `$1.97` and adjusted EPS was `$1.93`, compared with reported and adjusted EPS of `$1.76` in Q1 `2025`.
  • Guidance: management reaffirmed `2026` adjusted EPS guidance of `$6.55` to `$6.80` and the `5%` to `7%` long-term growth rate through `2030`, with confidence to earn in the top half of the range beginning in `2028`.

Signal Map

  • Sector: Duke strengthens the utilities sample by adding a traditional regulated multi-state utility that still shows the same AI-adjacent and industrial-load themes visible at AEP and NextEra.
  • Industrial: this is a direct industrial-buildout read because Duke’s growth depends on substations, transmission, dispatchable generation, gas-turbine procurement, labor availability, and large-project regulatory timing.
  • Consumer: the consumer angle is explicit. Duke repeatedly argues that large-load customers pay their fair share and that growth can help protect existing customers by spreading fixed costs over a broader base.
  • Cultural: Duke reinforces the idea that electricity is becoming visible national infrastructure. Data centers, advanced manufacturing, and regional economic-development competition now sit inside a utility earnings story.
  • Regulatory: Duke is another strong example of regulation as earnings architecture. Efficient recovery mechanisms, case outcomes, utility combinations, tax-credit monetization, and state approvals are central to the growth model.
  • Technology: Duke is a clean second-order AI infrastructure read. The company is not selling software; it is selling the regulated wires, substations, generation, and service agreements that let AI and data-center demand physically happen.
