McDonald's Corporation Full Analysis Memo

Bottom Line

McDonald's is a strong business for the parent company, but the reason is easy to misunderstand. The simple story is "McDonald's sells burgers." The better investor story is "McDonald's controls a large restaurant system and gets paid by that system in several ways."

Many McDonald's restaurants are run by franchisees. A franchisee is a local operator who runs the restaurant under the McDonald's name. The franchisee hires workers, buys food, serves customers, and handles many day-to-day store problems. In return for using the brand, location, operating system, and support, the franchisee pays McDonald's rent, royalty fees, and other fees.

That setup can produce a lot of cash for McDonald's even when McDonald's does not directly run every restaurant. It also creates the main risk. The parent company can look healthy while the people running restaurants are under pressure from wages, food costs, discounts, remodels, and technology spending.

The conclusion is not "buy because the brand is famous." The conclusion is: McDonald's is a high-quality cash-producing parent company, but the next review must prove that customer visits recover and that franchisees still make enough money after all the costs of running and updating restaurants.

How To Read The Company

The first step is to separate the company into pieces.

  • Customers visit restaurants and buy meals.
  • Restaurants collect the customer dollars.
  • Franchisees operate many of those restaurants.
  • McDonald's collects rent, royalty fees, and other fees from many franchisees.
  • McDonald's also benefits when the brand, app, menu, speed, and store locations make customers return.

This means McDonald's is partly a food company, partly a brand company, partly a restaurant operating system, and partly a rent-and-fee collector. That is why the parent company can be very profitable. It does not carry every store cost in the same way a fully company-owned restaurant chain would.

This also means the analysis has to ask two questions at the same time. First, is McDonald's parent company producing strong cash? Second, are the restaurants and franchisees healthy enough to keep producing that cash over time?

If we only answer the first question, we may miss hidden stress. If we only answer the second question, we may miss why the parent company is so powerful.

What The FY2025 Numbers Mean

McDonald's reported $26.9B of revenue in FY2025. Revenue is the money the company recorded from its business. Revenue alone does not tell us whether the business is attractive, because a company can have high revenue and low profit.

McDonald's reported $8.563B of net income. Net income is profit after expenses under accounting rules. This tells us that a large amount of revenue turned into profit.

McDonald's reported $11.95 of diluted earnings per share. This is the profit spread across the company's shares. It is useful for comparing the company over time, but it is not enough by itself because accounting profit is not the same as cash in the bank.

The strongest FY2025 number is operating cash flow of $10.551B. Operating cash flow means cash produced by the normal business. This number was higher than net income. That is a good sign because the reported profit was backed by cash.

McDonald's also spent $3.365B on capital spending. Capital spending is money used for things such as restaurants, equipment, remodels, and technology. After subtracting that capital spending from operating cash flow, McDonald's had about $7.186B before dividends and share buybacks.

That is the positive side of the analysis. The parent company produced a lot of cash after important spending needs.

The caution is that this parent-company cash number does not prove that every restaurant operator is comfortable. Franchisees may still face pressure from labor, food, rent, discounting, remodels, and technology. The parent cash result is strong, but the health of the system still needs checking.

What The Q2 2026 Numbers Mean

The Q2 2026 demand numbers are mixed.

Global comparable sales rose 1.3%. U.S. comparable sales rose 0.8%. Comparable sales means sales at restaurants that have been open long enough to compare with the prior period. This measure is useful because it is not only counting sales from newly opened restaurants.

The problem is that U.S. customer visits were negative. Customer visits are the number of times customers came to restaurants. So in the U.S., sales rose even though fewer customers visited.

That can happen in several ways.

  • Prices can be higher.
  • Customers can buy more expensive items.
  • Promotions can change what people buy.
  • A smaller number of customers can spend more per order.

This is why the number needs interpretation. Sales growth from more customer visits is usually healthier than sales growth from fewer visits and higher spending per visit. More visits mean the customer relationship is getting stronger. Fewer visits mean the company may be leaning on price, menu mix, or promotions.

This does not break the McDonald's story. It makes the next few quarters more important. The next filings need to show whether customer visits recover or whether sales keep depending on price and promotions.

What The Rewards App Has To Prove

McDonald's has a large rewards program. Over the trailing twelve months, loyalty members generated about $40B of systemwide sales. McDonald's also had nearly 220M active loyalty users.

Systemwide sales means sales across the whole McDonald's restaurant system, including restaurants run by franchisees. This is bigger than McDonald's own reported revenue. It shows the size of the customer activity connected to the brand.

The rewards app may be valuable, but size alone does not prove it. A large app can mean two very different things.

The good version is that the app changes customer behavior. Customers join the program, visit more often than before, buy orders that still make money for restaurants, and keep returning after coupons fade.

The weaker version is that the app mostly tracks people who already liked McDonald's. In that case, the loyalty number is still useful data, but it is not proof that the app created new demand.

The first-principles question is simple: did the app cause more profitable visits, or did it mostly count visits that would have happened anyway?

Why The Value Meal Matters

The $5 Meal Deal matters because it speaks to customer trust. When customers feel fast food has become too expensive, they may visit less often. A visible low-price meal tells the customer, "You can still get an affordable meal here."

That can help in two ways. It can bring back customers who had stopped visiting. It can also remind customers that McDonald's is convenient and predictable.

But it can also hurt. If customers learn to come only for the cheapest meal, or if they stack discounts without buying extra items, restaurant profit can fall even when traffic improves.

The important question is not "did the promotion sell meals?" The important question is "did the promotion lead to later normal orders that still made money?"

What Each Approach Found

The first-principles approach asks what the business is made of before using labels. For McDonald's, it found a system made of customer visits, dollars per order, restaurant speed, franchisees, rent, royalty fees, parent-company cash, and franchisee profit. This matters because calling McDonald's a burger chain hides how the parent company actually gets paid.

The Damodaran-style approach asks how the story turns into future cash. It found that brand strength is not enough. The important drivers are customer visits, average order size, store count, cash produced by the parent company, required spending, and risk. This matters because even a great company can be a poor stock purchase if the price already assumes too much success.

The Lyn Alden-style macro approach asks how the outside money environment affects the company. It found that McDonald's depends on everyday household spending. If rent, food, debt payments, and other costs pressure households, customers become more careful about restaurant prices. This matters because it explains why value offers are central, not decorative.

The owner-cash approach asks how much cash is left after the business spends what it needs to keep going. It found that FY2025 cash production was strong after capital spending. This supports the quality case. The remaining question is whether franchisees also have enough cash to keep restaurants updated and profitable.

The industry-power approach asks who has bargaining power. It found that McDonald's has power from brand, convenient locations, habit, speed, and scale. Customers still have power because they can eat at home, buy grocery food, choose another restaurant, trade down, or wait for discounts. This matters because falling customer visits are meaningful even when reported sales rise.

Final Interpretation

McDonald's looks strong because the parent company produced a lot of cash and has a large restaurant system paying it. The concern is that recent U.S. sales growth did not come with more customer visits. That means the next proof must come from traffic, rewards behavior, value-meal follow-through, and franchisee health.

If customer visits recover and franchisees remain profitable, the McDonald's system still looks very strong. If customer visits keep falling and franchisees absorb more pressure, the parent company may look better than the full restaurant system really feels.

What Would Make The Conclusion Stronger

  • U.S. customer visits turn positive for several quarters.
  • Rewards members keep visiting more often after their first year in the program.
  • Value-meal customers come back later for normal orders.
  • Franchisees keep opening stores, remodeling stores, and earning acceptable profit.
  • Store technology reduces waiting time, order mistakes, broken equipment time, or worker time per order.

What Would Make The Conclusion Weaker

  • U.S. customer visits keep falling while sales rise only from higher prices or promotions.
  • Rewards growth mostly comes from customers who were already frequent visitors.
  • Franchisees make less money after rent, fees, wages, food costs, discounts, remodels, and technology spending.
  • Cheap meal deals bring traffic but reduce profit per order.
  • McDonald's uses buybacks while the restaurant system needs more support.

But the 2025 return of $7.131B to shareholders—$2.016B of treasury-stock purchases and $5.115B of dividends— roughly consumed the cash after capex before the company continued to fund restaurant acquisitions and other investing.

This is not the same contradiction as Chipotle. McDonald's can return more cash because the franchise system funds much of the labor, inventory, and restaurant-level operating burden. That is the source of the economic advantage and the source of the diligence risk.

Franchisee burden-transfer test

The 2025 filing says franchised margins represented approximately 90% of restaurant margin dollars. This confirms the parent-level model: McDonald's captures rent and royalty economics while franchisees carry most restaurant labor, food, local operations, and much of the reinvestment burden.

The correct system-level bridge is:

`systemwide sales -> franchisee restaurant profit -> rent/royalty/advertising -> parent cash -> franchisee remodel, labor, debt, and technology capacity`

The parent can therefore look stronger as franchisee economics weaken, at least for a period. The next filing cannot be read only through McDonald's operating margin. It must capture franchisee closures, openings, remodel cadence, restaurant-level sales, rent and royalty burden, value-promotion economics, labor cost, and the health of franchisee financing.

### Value offers and loyalty are not automatically accretive

McDonald's Q2 2026 loyalty-member systemwide sales exceeded $40B on the local packet's trailing-twelve-month measure, with nearly 220M active users. This is valuable demand data, but the economic question is whether loyalty traffic produces profitable system sales after discounts, delivery, payment fees, and franchisee labor complexity.

The $5 Meal Deal is a similar test. It can restore traffic and protect habit, but a low-price offer may reduce franchisee margin if customers do not add profitable items or return later at normal pricing. The company should be judged on post-promotion frequency and franchisee cash returns, not only on reported systemwide sales.

Earnings-quality and financial-engineering tests

### 1. Parent cash is not franchisee free cash flow

McDonald's operating cash flow exceeded net income in 2025, a positive parent- company quality signal. It does not prove the system is under no pressure. The parent's rent and royalty receipts are economically dependent on franchisees being able to staff, maintain, remodel, and finance stores.

### 2. Capital-light presentation still carries property and lease exposure

The franchised model reduces direct restaurant operating cost, but McDonald's still owns or controls substantial real estate and records franchised-restaurant occupancy expenses. The 2025 capital plan allocated $3.365B mainly to restaurant openings and reinvestment. The owner-cash test must therefore separate new restaurant growth, maintenance/remodel capex, property economics, and franchisee-funded investment.

### 3. Debt and capital returns deserve a liquidity lens

The parent returned $7.131B to shareholders while ending 2025 with $774M of cash and equivalents, compared with $1.085B a year earlier. Long-term financing issuances and repayments were both large in the cash-flow statement. That is compatible with a stable franchised model, but it means liquidity and funding markets matter to a business that promises dividends, buybacks, remodels, and real-estate investment simultaneously.

Alden and Damodaran synthesis

The Alden lens favors McDonald's relative to a company-operated restaurant chain when household spending weakens because rent and royalty economics can remain resilient with less direct labor exposure. The risk is second-order: franchisees are the balance-sheet and labor shock absorbers. If their liquidity or returns deteriorate, the parent eventually faces closures, lower development, support spending, or weaker rent collection.

The Damodaran lens says the value of the franchise model is the spread between systemwide demand and the capital McDonald's itself must invest. That spread is real only if franchisees continue to earn enough to renew the relationship. The valuation should therefore include a franchisee-health stress case rather than capitalizing parent cash flow in isolation.

Plain-English Investment Memo

The main result is that McDonald's is a strong parent-company business, but the strength is not simply that people recognize the brand. The stronger explanation is that McDonald's sits on top of a very large restaurant system. Customers buy meals. Franchisees operate many of the restaurants. Those franchisees pay McDonald's rent, royalty fees, and other fees. That means McDonald's can earn a lot of cash from restaurants it does not directly run every day.

The FY2025 numbers support that view. McDonald's reported $26.9B of revenue and $8.563B of net income, but the more important number is $10.551B of operating cash flow. Operating cash flow is cash produced by normal business activity. When that number is higher than net income, it tells us the accounting profit is showing up as cash, not just as paper earnings. After $3.365B of capital spending, McDonald's still had about $7.186B before dividends and buybacks.

The risk is in the customer-visit data. In Q2 2026, U.S. comparable sales rose 0.8%, but U.S. customer visits were negative. That is a mixed signal. Sales rose, but fewer people came. That can happen when prices are higher, customers buy a different mix of items, or promotions change what people buy. Growth from more visits is stronger than growth from fewer visits paying more.

This is why the rewards app and value meals matter. The rewards app is valuable only if it makes people come more often, buy orders that still make money, and keep returning after discounts fade. The $5 Meal Deal matters because it tells customers that McDonald's can still be affordable. But cheap meals can either repair trust or train customers to wait for discounts. Those are very different outcomes.

The investment conclusion is therefore conditional. McDonald's looks like a high-quality cash producer. But the next review should not just celebrate the brand or the app. It should check whether customer visits recover, whether loyalty causes extra visits, and whether franchisees still make enough money after rent, fees, wages, food costs, discounts, remodels, and technology spending.

First-Principles Explanation

Start with what McDonald's really sells. A customer thinks they are buying a meal, but an investor has to see a larger machine: recognizable brand, convenient real estate, franchise contracts, rent, royalties, menu pricing, mobile ordering, and repeat customer behavior. The parent company does not need to own every restaurant to make money. Franchisees run many McDonald's restaurants, and they pay McDonald's rent, royalty fees, and other fees for using the brand and locations.

That is why the difference between the parent company and the restaurant operator matters. The parent can report strong cash flow while the people operating restaurants face wage inflation, food inflation, discount pressure, remodel requirements, and technology spending. The analysis therefore does not stop at McDonald's corporate profit. It asks whether the whole system is healthy enough to keep producing that profit.

Comparable sales, or comps, mean sales at restaurants that have been open long enough to compare against the prior period. A positive comp is good only after asking what caused it. If more customers visited, the business may be strengthening. If fewer customers visited but paid more per order, the headline sales number can hide demand weakness.

The rewards app matters only if it changes what customers do. A big user count is not enough. The stronger proof would be this: after joining, customers visit more often than they did before, their orders still make money for the restaurant, and they keep coming back after coupons fade. Without that proof, the app may mostly be a way to track customers who already liked McDonald's.

What The Numbers Mean

Revenue tells us how much money came into the company. Net income tells us what profit was left after expenses under accounting rules. Operating cash flow tells us how much cash the business actually produced from normal operations. McDonald's produced $10.551B of operating cash flow in FY2025 versus $8.563B of net income. That is a strong sign because the reported profit showed up as real cash.

The Q2 2026 U.S. sales number needs careful reading. U.S. comparable sales were up 0.8%, but guest counts were negative. Guest counts mean customer visits. This is weaker than growth caused by more people visiting.

The loyalty numbers are very large: about $40B of trailing-twelve-month sales to loyalty members and nearly 220M active loyalty users. But a large app program is not automatically proof of a stronger business. The important question is whether the app changes behavior. Do customers come more often because of the app, buy orders that make good profit, and keep returning after coupons or launch offers fade? If not, the app may mostly be counting customers who already liked McDonald's.

Capital spending was $3.365B in FY2025. Capital spending means money spent on things such as restaurants, equipment, remodels, and technology. After that spending, McDonald's still had about $7.186B of cash before dividends and share buybacks. That is good for the parent company, but the system can keep working only if franchisees can also afford their own store spending and still make money.

Plain-Language Definitions

Comparable sales

Sales growth at existing restaurants. It helps separate real store performance from growth caused only by opening new restaurants.

Guest counts

The number of customer visits. If guest counts fall while sales rise, customers may be paying more but visiting less.

Average check

The average dollars spent per order. It can rise because of price increases, menu mix, or larger baskets.

Franchise model

A structure where local operators run many restaurants while McDonald's collects rent, royalties, and fees from them.

Free cash flow

Cash left after capital spending needed to maintain and grow the business. It is closer to what owners can ultimately use than revenue or EPS alone.

Systemwide sales

Sales made by all restaurants in the system, including restaurants run by franchisees. This is larger than McDonald's own reported revenue.

Did the app cause more visits?

The key loyalty question: did customers visit more because of the app, or were frequent customers simply the first people to join?

What would change the answer: The answer would improve if value offers led to repeat normal-price visits. It would weaken if customers came only for deals or if franchisees had to pay too much of the discount cost.

What it found: FY2025 operating cash flow was $10.551B and capital spending was $3.365B, leaving about $7.186B before dividends and buybacks.

Company Conclusion

McDonald's is best understood as a restaurant system owner. The parent company benefits from the brand, busy locations, rent, royalty fees, and customer data from the app. The main risk is that the parent company can keep looking strong while customer visits weaken or franchisees carry more of the cost burden.

What The Market Could Be Missing

  • The market may give too much credit to loyalty users before proving those users visit more often because of the app.
  • The parent company may look healthier than the franchisees who run many of the restaurants.
  • Sales can rise while customer visits fall, so higher prices or different order mix must not be confused with stronger demand.
  • A short-term marketing event can create attention without proving customers will keep coming back.

What Would Prove This Wrong

  • Rewards members do not keep visiting more often after their first year in the program.
  • $5 Meal Deal buyers come back only for the cheapest meal and do not buy extra items that help restaurant profit.
  • Franchisees make less money, struggle with debt, delay remodels, close stores, or stop opening new stores.
  • Store technology does not reduce waiting time, order mistakes, broken equipment time, or worker time per order.
  • Customer visits keep falling while sales depend on higher prices, different menu items, or promotions.

Deep Questions

Did loyalty actually make people come more often?

The app is powerful only if the same people visited more after joining, and similar non-members did not show the same rise.

Weakens if: Most new members were already frequent customers; app sales rose while total U.S. visits stayed weak; members visited more only when large discounts were offered.

Did the $5 Meal Deal repair trust, or did it only buy cheap visits?

It worked if it reminded customers that McDonald's is worth visiting. It failed if it trained customers to wait for discounts.

Weakens if: Customers bought only the $5 meal; app coupons were stacked on top; franchisees saw more orders but worse profit per order.

Who pays when McDonald's promises value?

The cost can land on the franchisee, McDonald's, suppliers, workers, or customers through higher prices elsewhere. Parent cash can look strong while franchisees carry more of the burden.

Weakens if: Franchisee profit falls while parent cash stays high; operators push back on required spending; stores need more discounts to keep traffic from falling.

Are U.S. sales rising for the right reason?

Sales can rise because prices are higher or people buy different items. More visits would be stronger proof than higher dollars per visit.

Weakens if: Traffic stays negative; sales depend on higher checks; value deals grow but normal-price visits do not.

Does Ready on Arrival actually improve the restaurant?

If the restaurant knows the customer is close, it can start the order at the right time. That should mean less waiting, fresher food, fewer wrong orders, and better use of workers.

Weakens if: Wait-time claims do not show up in broad store data; the system adds cost and training burden without better service; franchisees pay before seeing store benefits.

Did the Grinch socks create real demand or just a one-day spike?

The event mattered if it brought new or lapsed customers back and some of them returned after the socks were gone.

Weakens if: Sales spiked for one day and faded; the campaign mostly attracted existing heavy users; the socks created buzz but little profit.

Next Filing Watchlist

  • In the next three U.S. quarters, check whether sales are rising because more customers visit or because each customer pays more.
  • For loyalty members, compare how often they visited before joining with how often they visit after joining.
  • Check whether franchisees are still making enough money after rent, royalty fees, remodels, wages, and food costs.
  • Check whether digital ordering improves waiting time, order accuracy, and worker time per order.
  • Check whether share buybacks are happening at sensible prices after McDonald's funds restaurant needs.

Comparison Bridge

Compared with Chipotle, McDonald's pushes more store work and store cost onto franchisees. McDonald's then earns rent and fees from those franchisees. That can make the parent company look very strong, but the key question is whether franchisees are still healthy after paying those costs.
