McDonald's Corporation

A plain read on why McDonald's still makes so much cash: busy locations, rent and fees, the app, the $5 meal, store technology, and the Grinch socks campaign.

Core Answer

Plain answer: McDonald's still makes a lot of cash because it gets paid in several ways from many busy restaurants.

Why: Customers buy the food. Franchisees run many stores. McDonald's also collects rent and fees. The app and promotions try to bring customers back.

Main doubt: The parent company can look strong while customers visit less and franchisees earn less.

What we need to check: Visits, prices, store profit, and parent-company cash.

Investment read

McDonald's made strong cash in FY2025. Operating cash flow was about 123% of net income; free cash flow before dividends and buybacks was roughly $7.186B. The risk is paying too much for slow growth or missing stress at franchisee restaurants.

Where Pressure Goes

When customers resist prices, the cost can move to customers, franchisees, suppliers, workers, or McDonald's itself. The parent company looks strongest when customers or franchisees carry the pressure without visits breaking.

The hard question is whether current cash flow proves the business is healthy, or only shows that the pressure has not yet reached the parent company's statements. Because McDonald's collects rent and fees, parent profit can look excellent while restaurant operators carry more strain.

Main tension

McDonald's wants to be cheap enough for pressured customers and profitable enough for franchisees to keep investing. The app helps because McDonald's can give one person a deal without lowering every price for everyone. Store technology helps only if it starts orders sooner, cuts waiting, and reduces mistakes.

Deep Questions

1. Did loyalty actually make people come more often?

Plain answer: We do not know yet. McDonald's says members visit more, but that does not prove the app caused it.

Why: Frequent customers may have been more likely to join. We must compare their visits before and after joining.

Proof needed: visits before and after joining, a similar non-member group, repeat visits after the first-year offer rush, and order size after rewards are used.

What would weaken it: app sales rose while total visits stayed weak, or members visited more only when large discounts were offered.

2. Did the $5 Meal Deal repair trust, or only buy cheap visits?

Plain answer: It may have repaired trust, but it may also have bought low-profit visits.

Why: The deal tells customers that McDonald's can still be affordable. The follow-up visit tells us whether the message worked.

Proof needed: customers who bought the $5 meal later came back for normal-price items, added extra items, and helped guest counts without a large profit drop.

What would weaken it: customers bought only the $5 meal, stacked coupons, and left franchisees with more orders but worse profit per order.

3. Who pays when McDonald's promises value?

Plain answer: We do not know yet who carries the discount cost.

Why: Parent-company cash can stay strong while franchisees absorb lower profit, higher wages, remodel costs, or technology costs.

Proof needed: franchisee profit after discounts and wage increases, remodel debt, store closures, and whether franchisees keep opening and renewing restaurants.

What would weaken it: franchisee profit falls while parent cash stays high, or operators push back on required spending.

4. Are U.S. sales rising for the right reason?

Plain answer: Not clearly. Q2 sales rose 0.8%, but fewer guests visited.

Why: Higher prices or larger orders can raise sales even when demand is weaker.

Proof needed: guest counts turn positive, sales rise without leaning mainly on price, low-income customers return, and peers do worse with similar customers.

What would weaken it: traffic stays negative and normal-price visits do not improve.

5. Does Ready on Arrival actually improve the restaurant?

Plain answer: It should reduce waiting, but the store data must show that it does.

Why: Knowing when a customer is close lets workers start the order at the right time.

Proof needed: shorter wait times in normal busy stores, better order accuracy, less equipment downtime, lower labor per order, and faster pickup or drive-thru lines.

What would weaken it: the system adds cost and training burden without better store results.

6. Did the Grinch socks create real demand or just a one-day spike?

Plain answer: The campaign created a large event, but we do not know whether it created lasting demand.

Why: A one-day sales record can come from existing customers visiting once earlier than planned.

Proof needed: new or lapsed customers came in, came back after the campaign, and the meal plus socks added profit after product, labor, and marketing costs.

What would weaken it: sales spiked for one day, mostly from existing heavy users, and then faded.

7. Are buybacks helping owners?

Plain answer: Buybacks help only when McDonald's buys its shares below their value.

Why: A mature company can destroy value by buying expensive shares while stores still need investment.

Proof needed: shares were bought at a strong owner-earnings yield, per-share cash earnings rose for real reasons, and buybacks did not crowd out needed store investment.

What would weaken it: repurchases happened at a high price while U.S. visits were weak, or debt rose mainly to fund shareholder returns.

Deeper Insights

  1. McDonald's wins because it gives people a known answer when they are hungry and busy.
  2. Loyalty matters only if it makes people come more often.
  3. The $5 Meal Deal tests whether customers still believe McDonald's is worth the price.
  4. High parent-company profit comes partly from rent and fees, not just selling food.
  5. Store technology matters because seconds, mistakes, and broken equipment cost real money at McDonald's size.
  6. The Grinch socks event matters because it shows McDonald's can make the meal a collectible access point, not just a food purchase.
  7. The biggest warning sign is positive U.S. sales with negative U.S. guest counts.
  8. The stock question is not whether McDonald's is good. It is how much future visit growth and pricing power is already in the stock price.

What Would Change The Read

  • Loyalty users grow but total traffic does not improve.
  • Loyalty sales mainly replace non-app sales.
  • Franchisee returns weaken enough to slow remodels, technology adoption, or new restaurant development.
  • $5 Meal Deal customers do not return for normal-price orders.
  • Buybacks continue at prices that do not make sense against owner earnings.

Reflection Queue
