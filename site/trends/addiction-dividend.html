The Addiction Dividend

The most durable moat in modern capitalism is not a patent or a network—it is a daily habit that users have stopped noticing they are performing. The companies that engineered that invisibility are now disclosing it openly to investors as their core competitive advantage.

Companies that convert discrete transactions into recurring behavioral loops have discovered that once the habit is invisible—pet food arriving before the owner remembers to order, a playlist that starts before the commute begins, a drive-thru order the app remembers before the car arrives—the economics change category. Price increases stop triggering comparison. Churn rates fall to levels that defeat competitive entry. Margins expand as the cost of customer acquisition amortizes over a compounding lifetime. The infrastructure that makes the habit invisible—Autoship, Dutch Rewards, Uber One, Netflix's recommendation engine, PMI's IQOS device ownership—is the actual product being sold. The consumer experience is the delivery mechanism. Behavioral lock-in is the balance sheet asset.

The mechanism

The mechanism is not loyalty—it is friction removal at the moment of repeat purchase, combined with a system that makes the user's preferences the platform's asset rather than the user's. Chewy's Autoship does not reward loyalty with a discount. It removes the cognitive event of choosing to buy pet food. The customer chooses Chewy once and delegates the rest to the platform. With 84% of Chewy's net sales coming through Autoship in Q3 2025, on $12.60B of fiscal 2025 net sales, the business has effectively converted a retail relationship into a subscription with no subscription fee and no contract—the switching cost is entirely behavioral. The same logic runs through every case. Philip Morris's IQOS device creates hardware ownership: the user who switches to the smoke-free format buys a device and becomes structurally dependent on PMI's consumables refill chain. Among 43 million adults using smoke-free PMI products by end of 2025, the migration from cigarettes to IQOS was framed as harm reduction; the economic consequence is a higher-priced, device-anchored repeat relationship with lower churn than combustibles. Combustibles net revenues still grew 9.5% in Q2 2026 despite volume declines—pure pricing power enabled by addiction stickiness.

The loyalty infrastructure that supports these habits is not superficially similar across industries—it is structurally identical. Dutch Rewards links beverage ritual to app check-ins, Stars accumulation, and order-ahead convenience that speeds the drive-thru experience. The user who links Dutch Rewards to a daily commute is not making a daily decision to visit Dutch Bros; they are executing a habit that includes the app as part of the routine. Thirteen consecutive quarters of positive same-shop sales growth through Q2 2026, and eight of those with positive transaction growth—not just ticket size—confirms the model is capturing repeat visit frequency, not just price. Uber One converts the two largest on-demand consumer platforms—mobility and food delivery—into one membership relationship, and by Q1 2026 those 50 million members were driving roughly half of $53.7B quarterly gross bookings. The membership does not create the habit; it captures an existing habit inside a fee structure and then deepens it by making Uber One the default across both surfaces. Electronic Arts builds the same loop through live-service content: battle passes, seasonal events, and progression systems that require regular check-ins to stay current in the social graph of a game. Missing a season means competitive disadvantage among peers. The 71% live services share of FY2026 net revenue confirms this is not a side feature—it is the business model.

The measurement layer is what separates modern behavioral infrastructure from previous-generation loyalty programs. Supermarket points tracked basket size. Airline miles tracked trips. Current systems track session length, content consumption by genre and mood, check-in cadence, progression velocity through reward tiers, peer-network depth and comparison, and content drop response rates. Spotify's AI-powered personalization builds a user model that makes the service literally irreplaceable by the user's own standards: a Spotify recommendation engine trained on 777 million MAUs and a user's own three-year listening history cannot be replicated by a competitor starting from scratch. The Wrapped product—an annual cultural moment built entirely on showing users data about their own behavior—demonstrates that the personalization system is the moat, not the music catalog. Netflix's 97 billion hours watched in H1 2026 across 325 million paid memberships is not just a viewership metric. It is a reorder-rate metric. Every hour watched trains the recommendation engine, which increases the probability of the next session, which absorbs price increases without triggering comparison shopping. Operating margin expanded from 29.5% full-year 2025 to 33.4% in Q2 2026 as that compounding deepened.

The externality accounting never appears in the filings. PMI's cost structure does not include a line item for nicotine dependence in the 43 million adults who switched formats. EA's FY2026 10-K does not include a line item for time captured from players who log in to avoid falling behind in seasonal progression. Dutch Bros does not account for the caffeine dependency that makes 13 consecutive quarters of positive same-shop transaction growth structurally more durable than it would be for a non-stimulant food operator. Chewy does not account for the friction it has deliberately removed that previously allowed a pet owner to comparison shop at the moment of purchase. The companies have successfully defined the absence of externality accounting as a feature of category-level consumer choice, not a cost they have externalized. The benefit—pricing power, expanding margins, reduced churn—flows entirely to shareholders.

Why now

The inflection is not recent in a vague sense—it has a specific date structure. PMI's smoke-free business crossed 41.5% of total net revenues in FY2025, up from a position where combustibles still dominated economics. Smoke-free shipment volumes grew 12.8% in 2025 and the consumer base grew by 4.5 million adults in one year. The threshold where habit-migrated consumers materially determine company economics was crossed in 2025, not approached. Netflix did not always have this model: the company spent its first decade competing on content breadth and DVD access. The operating margin moved from single digits in the early 2010s to 29.5% in 2025 and 33.4% in Q2 2026 as algorithmic personalization deepened and password-sharing enforcement pushed members into verified paid accounts. The 97 billion hours watched in H1 2026 is a behavioral proof-of-normal, not a novelty metric. Dutch Bros is a specific case: 13 consecutive quarters of same-shop sales growth is a streak that began before the Dutch Rewards app reached critical mass and has continued through it, but 8 consecutive quarters of positive transaction growth—not just average ticket—proves the app-driven ritual deepened the visit frequency rather than just the spend per visit. Starbucks required external intervention to get back to positive comparable transaction growth: the Q1 2026 positive comp was the first in eight quarters, achieved through stored-value card recovery and Rewards reactivation after the brand's service execution collapsed. The mechanism only works when the habit infrastructure is intact. When Starbucks broke its own habit loop, transactions fell for two years. What changed is not that companies discovered behavioral economics after 2020. What changed is that mobile-first account management, stored-value systems, app-linked order-ahead, and subscription membership layers have become the default interface between consumer and brand across every category simultaneously. The cost of building and maintaining that infrastructure has dropped below the point where only tech companies can afford it. Dutch Bros and Chewy and PMI now run the same behavioral capture layer that Amazon and Netflix pioneered.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Consumers who have crossed the threshold from active choice to automated habit. The 43 million adults who switched from combustible cigarettes to IQOS or ZYN are still nicotine-dependent—they are now dependent in a format that costs more and requires device ownership. Low-income pet owners who use Chewy Autoship have surrendered price comparison at the moment of need; they delegated that function to the platform in exchange for convenience. Dutch Bros drive-thru regulars are paying for a daily caffeine ritual at average unit volumes of $2.1M per shop—a spend level that implies multiple visits per week from the repeat-customer base, not occasional treats. Spotify Premium subscribers paying EUR 4.331B in Q2 2026 premium revenue are paying partly for music access and partly for switching costs: a personalization engine they cannot take with them and a listening history the competitor does not have. Uber One members pay a membership fee on top of transaction fees so that the platform they already use reflexively is slightly cheaper—a structure that deepens wallet share rather than reducing it. Public health systems absorb the externalities of nicotine dependence at scale; EA's seasonal content systems capture time from a predominantly young-male player base; the Spotify data that deepens personalization belongs to Spotify, not the user.
Who profits
Philip Morris International: smoke-free business at 41.5% of FY2025 net revenues and 43% of gross profit, with combustibles still growing net revenues 9.5% in Q2 2026 despite volume declines—habit stickiness enabling pure pricing power even in the declining format. Netflix: 29.5% operating margin for full-year 2025 expanding to 33.4% in Q2 2026, with ad revenue growing 2.5x to over $1.5B in 2025 as the platform double-dips on the same captive audience. Chewy: $562M record free cash flow in FY2025 on $12.60B of net sales with 84% Autoship penetration providing the cash flow predictability of a subscription business without requiring subscribers to sign contracts. Electronic Arts: $7.531B of FY2026 net revenue with 71% from live services, generating $2.553B of operating cash flow, meaning the ongoing participation system—not the initial sale—is the financial engine. Dutch Bros: record system AUVs of $2.1M in Q2 2026 on its 13th consecutive quarter of positive same-shop sales, with adjusted EBITDA guidance raised to $385M-$390M for FY2026. The tension

The model faces three structural limits, none of which appear in current filings as risks of the same magnitude as the opportunities they enable. First, the externality gap is growing. PMI explicitly states that combustible pricing power depends on habit stickiness caused by nicotine addiction—that language is in investor filings. The harm-reduction framing for smoke-free products claims they are safer than combustibles while the long-term health data on IQOS and ZYN at scale in 43 million users does not yet exist. If the safety claim fails at scale, the regulatory tolerance that enabled category migration collapses and the premium pricing with it. Second, saturation is real. Chewy has 21.3M active customers; pet ownership rates in the United States set an upper bound on addressable customer additions. Dutch Bros has 13 consecutive quarters of positive same-shop sales growth because it is still in geographic expansion mode—its AUVs of $2.1M reflect a still-maturing shop base, not a saturated one. When the expansion story matures, the habit must deepen to sustain growth without unit adds. Starbucks proved this is not guaranteed: it spent two years with negative comparable transaction growth, not because customers left the habit but because execution failed to support it. Third, the measurement and personalization layer that creates the stickiness is also the liability. Spotify's moat is a user model it built from behavioral data it owns. Regulatory pressure on behavioral data, personalization transparency requirements, or major data events could require companies to give users portable versions of their own behavioral models—which would reduce the switching cost the personalization layer was designed to create. Uber One's half-of-gross-bookings concentration is a regulatory signal: when a membership layer drives that share of demand, antitrust scrutiny shifts from pricing behavior to wallet capture.

The surprising part

The least obvious thing about the addiction dividend is not that it exists—it is that the companies have chosen to report it with unusual candor, and that investors price it as a virtue rather than a risk. PMI's investor filings say the smoke-free business commands premium pricing because users have switched to a higher-cost format and face switching costs; the same filings acknowledge that combustibles grow net revenues through pricing power that depends on nicotine dependence. Netflix discloses 97 billion hours of member viewing not as a vanity metric but as evidence of habit formation that supports margin expansion. Chewy reports 84% Autoship penetration as its primary competitive moat. Electronic Arts names live services—seasonal content designed to require regular engagement to avoid falling behind socially—as 71% of total revenue. Dutch Bros emphasizes that Dutch Rewards and app-linked ordering are the mechanism behind 13 consecutive quarters of same-shop sales growth. Uber tells investors that 50 million Uber One members drive roughly half of gross bookings, signaling successful wallet capture rather than just member count. In every case, the company is disclosing the behavioral capture mechanism to shareholders as the core durability argument—and being rewarded with premium multiples for doing so. The regulatory and societal response has been modest not because regulators miss it, but because each company has successfully reframed addiction as a category transition: PMI as harm reduction, Netflix as entertainment routine, Chewy as pet care delegation, EA as sports identity, Dutch Bros as beverage ritual, Spotify as music discovery, Uber as everyday mobility. The behavioral infrastructure that makes the compulsion invisible to the consumer is visible and celebrated in the shareholder letter.

Why it matters

When the most financially durable business model available to a mature company is behavioral habituation—and when the infrastructure of that habituation is built inside apps that mediate daily life, reminders that arrive before a need is consciously felt, and loyalty systems that convert the user's own history into switching costs—the question of individual choice becomes structurally complicated. Chewy's Autoship, Dutch Rewards, Uber One, and Spotify's recommendation engine are not coercive in any legal sense. They are opt-in systems that deliver genuine convenience. But they are also systems explicitly designed, measured, and optimized to make the repeat behavior invisible, the decision moment inaccessible, and the competitor's interface feel unfamiliar. The financial disclosures prove the intent: 84% Autoship penetration, 97 billion hours of habit-confirmed viewing, 13 consecutive quarters of transaction growth, half of gross bookings from members. These are not accidental outcomes of good products. They are disclosed metrics of deliberate behavioral engineering. The societal question is not whether these habits harm users—most of them do not in any direct sense. The question is whether a democratic economy wants its most durable profit structures to rest on behavioral capture at scale, and whether the public costs of the cases where the habit is genuinely harmful—nicotine dependence in 43 million adults, time capture from young gamers in EA's seasonal progression systems—should appear somewhere in the accounting of the businesses that profit from them.

Across tobacco, streaming, pet care, gaming, beverages, and mobility, the highest-margin businesses of the 2020s are those that converted consumer choice into automated habit—and then disclosed the behavioral capture infrastructure to investors as their primary competitive moat.

Netflix, Spotify, SiriusXM, and Roku have transformed entertainment consumption from active choice into ambient default, where autoplay, algorithmic queues, and subscription inertia have engineered a user base that stays not because switching costs are high but because forgetting to cancel has become the path of least resistance.
