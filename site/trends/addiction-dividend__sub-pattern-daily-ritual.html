The Ritual Premium: Coffee, Oral Care, and the Pricing Power of Morning Routine

When repetition becomes identity, companies discover a pricing engine that rivals chemical dependency—without the regulatory exposure.

Dutch Bros, Starbucks, Colgate-Palmolive, and Planet Fitness occupy a different register of the addiction dividend: not chemical dependency but deep behavioral routine that functions as identity and self-care. The evidence is clear—companies that layer app-linked rewards, stored-value systems, or membership friction around daily rituals can raise prices without losing customers, because the ritual has already become part of how people see themselves.

The mechanism

The ritual premium exists because daily consumption bundled with convenience and identity creates stickiness that price alone cannot explain. Dutch Bros serves as the clearest case study: the company achieved record AUVs of $2.1M in 2025 while maintaining 13 consecutive quarters of positive same-shop sales growth and eight consecutive quarters of transaction growth. This is not foot traffic from occasional customers. This is habit. The mechanism is layered: drive-thru speed removes friction; stimulation satisfies biochemical demand; the Dutch Rewards app-based loyalty program creates a prepaid relationship; order-ahead functionality further reduces friction. The result is that Dutch Bros is not selling coffee as a commodity. It is selling a ritual object embedded in an app, accessed from a car, mediated by rewards, and reinforced by speed and familiarity. Customers absorb price increases because the ritual itself has become part of their morning identity.

Starbucks proves this pattern at scale. The company's fiscal 2025 numbers show consolidated net revenues of $37.2B with operating margin pressure—the margin fell to 7.9% from 15.0%, and diluted EPS declined to $1.63 from $3.31. Yet the company did not collapse. Instead, management launched 'Back to Starbucks' to rebuild service, and by Q2 2026, global comparable store sales had increased 6.2%, driven by a 3.8% increase in comparable transactions and a 2.3% increase in average ticket. U.S. comparable transaction growth turned positive for the first time in eight quarters in Q1 2026. The mechanism here is stored value and social location. Starbucks discloses a real loyalty-and-stored-value operating system: prepaid cards activated in stores, online, and via the app; registered cardholders automatically enrolled in Starbucks Rewards; and both unredeemed card balances and unredeemed Stars sitting in deferred revenue. When customers prepay and earn Stars, they are no longer anonymous transactions. They are loyalty members with an economic interest in returning.

Colgate-Palmolive and Planet Fitness extend the pattern into health and wellness. Colgate's 2025 results show full-year net sales of $20.382B with organic sales growth of 1.4%, base business EPS rising 3% to $3.69, and record operating cash flow of $4.198B—all in oral care, a category where the ritual is literally twice-daily. Oral Care alone represents 44% of total revenue. There is no innovation premium here, only discipline. Customers brush their teeth at a fixed time, reaching for the same branded product out of routine and trust. The science-based innovation and advertising support maintain the habit's legitimacy. Planet Fitness operates at the behavioral frontier: full-year 2025 revenue grew 12.1% to $1.3B from 20.8M members paying $15 to $24.99 monthly. Remarkably, the company ended 2025 as the first full year after a 50% price increase for new Classic Card members, yet it still added 1.1M net new members and maintained 6.7% system-wide same-club sales growth. The ritual here is social body confidence in a non-intimidating space. Members are not paying for equipment or personal training. They are paying for permission to maintain their body and self-respect inside a 'Judgement Free Zone' at a price they can sustain.

The pricing power follows because each company has monetized a moment in the customer's day that feels non-negotiable. Coffee is morning energy. Toothpaste is twice-daily hygiene. Fitness is weekly self-maintenance. These are not luxury goods or occasional purchases. They are habitual, low-ticket, high-frequency transactions embedded in identity. The stored-value systems at Dutch Bros and Starbucks, the brand-lock in toothpaste, and the membership frame at Planet Fitness all create economic stickiness that generic price elasticity does not capture. When a customer has already prepaid, earned Stars, or committed to a monthly membership, the marginal cost of one more visit approaches zero. Price increases do not eliminate the habit; they reduce margin on each transaction, but total lifetime value often rises because habit deepens.

Why now

The ritual premium has become visible now because three forces converged in the 2024-2026 period. First, post-pandemic consumer behavior stabilized around what researchers call 'nesting inflation'—willingness to pay more for everyday goods that happen at home or in predictable rituals rather than discretionary experiences. Second, app-mediated loyalty and prepayment infrastructure became the default distribution model for quick-service and subscription businesses; Starbucks, Dutch Bros, Planet Fitness, and Colgate all embed digital enrollment as standard. Third, price discipline became existential. When growth slowed after the 2021-2022 demand surge, companies discovered that customers would accept price increases on habitual goods even if they rejected them on discretionary ones. Dutch Bros achieved this in record AUVs while maintaining transaction growth. Starbucks recovered comparable transaction growth for the first time in eight quarters after 'Back to Starbucks' cost pressure. The moment is now because these companies have data proving that routine and identity, not just stimulation, generate pricing power.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
The customer who has integrated the ritual into their self-image. The person who cannot imagine a morning without coffee, a day without brushing their teeth, or a week without a gym visit. For Dutch Bros, it is the drive-thru regular who checks the app before arriving. For Starbucks, it is the Rewards member with a prepaid balance. For Colgate-Palmolive, it is the household that has always used the brand, renewed out of habit, and trusts the science claims. For Planet Fitness, it is the member who has told themselves and others that they are now 'a gym person' and cannot quit without signaling failure to their identity. These customers pay not because they shop rationally but because the alternative—breaking the ritual and the identity attached to it—feels worse than price increases.
Who profits
The companies that own the ritual infrastructure and can layer pricing directly on top of repetition. Dutch Bros captures AUVs of $2.1M per unit by controlling the drive-thru experience, the app, and the rewards program. Starbucks captures margin through stored value and loyalty liabilities, converting prepaid customer capital into free working capital while incentivizing repeat visits. Colgate-Palmolive captures brand lock and category leadership: oral care is 44% of revenue, growth is measurable through science-based innovation, and market share is defended through advertising rather than price competition. Planet Fitness captures 90% of revenue through franchisees who pay national advertising contributions and fee percentages while the company retains the membership data, pricing architecture, and member relationship. Each model creates a moat where the company owns the behavior, and the customer's ritual pays rent to that ownership.

The tension

The tension is psychological capture and consumer lock-in without consent. When a ritual becomes identity, price increases begin to feel like moral betrayal rather than market signals. Dutch Bros customers feel the app's reward mechanics as fairness, not addiction. Starbucks customers feel loyalty membership as relationship, not financial extraction. Colgate users feel brand trust as evidence-based choice, not habit. Planet Fitness members feel the gym as self-improvement, not recurring debt. In each case, the company has nudged behavior into ritual and then monetized it at the edge of what customers will tolerate before questioning the relationship itself. The risk is that when customers realize they are not choosing but trapped, the backlash can be swift. Starbucks experienced this in Q1 2026 when it paused a planned Black Card price increase because market testing signaled customer resistance. Planet Fitness did the same in Q1 2026, pausing a national price increase and explicitly testing 'pricing, member experience, and retention.' The tension is not yet visible as a structural problem, but these companies are already showing behavioral caution around the ceiling of what ritual customers will bear.

The surprising part

The non-obvious twist is that transaction growth persists even as prices rise. Conventional economics would predict that price increases reduce traffic. Instead, Dutch Bros maintained 8 consecutive quarters of same-shop transaction growth while achieving record AUVs of $2.1M. Starbucks turned U.S. comparable transaction growth positive for the first time in eight quarters while raising average ticket by 2.3% in Q2 2026. Planet Fitness added 1.1M net new members in 2025, the first full year after a 50% price increase for new customers. None of this should happen if these were commodity goods. The explanation is that ritual has decoupled from price elasticity. The more identity-adjacent the ritual, the more customers prioritize consistency over cost. This is the deepest proof of the addiction dividend: companies are not competing on price. They are competing on identity capture.

Why it matters

The individual stakes are psychological autonomy and financial transparency. If rituals are identity, then companies that monetize routine are, in effect, monetizing selfhood. The broader societal stakes are two-fold. First, income inequality: these pricing models are sustainable only for customers with disposable income. Working families and the poor cannot absorb 50% price increases or maintain $24.99 monthly fitness subscriptions; they either exit the ritual or reallocate from other needs. Second, public health and behavior engineering: if companies discover that app-mediated loyalty and prepayment can sustain pricing on coffee, fitness, and toothpaste, then digital platforms become infrastructure for consumption management. The line between convenience and control becomes invisible. A person brushing their teeth twice daily with Colgate, paying $2.49 for a branded tube, does not feel controlled. But they are. The ritual, the brand lock, and the price are three interlocking components of a system engineered to feel inevitable and personal.

When companies bundle daily rituals—coffee, oral care, fitness—with app-linked rewards or membership frames, they convert habits into identity, and identity absorbs price increases that would destroy volume in commodity categories.
