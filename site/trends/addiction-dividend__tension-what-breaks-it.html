When the Habit Breaks: The Loyalty Membership That Cannot Save Shrinking Revenue

A 40-million-member loyalty base fails to arrest sales decline when the brand loses relevance. Membership becomes a mirror of failure, not a moat.

Loyalty membership size is a lagging indicator of business health, not a leading one. When product relevance, regulatory permission, or price tolerance shift, the loyalty infrastructure reveals its true fragility: it can measure defection in real-time but cannot stop it. The addiction dividend thesis assumes stickiness persists. These filings prove it does not.

The mechanism

The addiction dividend thesis assumes that behavioral habits, once wrapped in identity and subscription billing, become durable and profitable. Loyalty infrastructure, repeat purchase cycles, and brand loyalty create a moat. But the company filings reveal a critical structural weakness: the habit is only as durable as the product, the regulatory permission, and the consumer's cost tolerance. When any one shifts, membership becomes a liability that measures defection in real-time.

Kohl's demonstrates the pattern directly. With 40 million active loyalty members, credit-card economics, omnichannel access, and Sephora adjacency, the company still reported net sales decline of 4.0% to $14.8B in fiscal 2025, comparable sales decline of 3.1%, and diluted EPS of $2.38. The loyalty base did not fail to prevent decline; the brand failed to remain relevant. The membership data made that failure undeniable and immediate.

Bath & Body Works tells the same story with higher stakes. Fiscal 2025 showed 40 million active loyalty members across 1,927 company-operated stores, direct e-commerce, international operations, and marketplace access. Yet net sales were $7.291B (down 0.2%), operating income fell 11% to $1.126B, and net income was $649M. Management responded with an explicit Consumer First Formula transformation. The loyalty infrastructure did not break; the brand's ability to command pricing and frequency did. The membership base became evidence of the problem, not a solution.

Philip Morris faces a different but equally real fragility. Smoke-free products now account for 41.5% of revenues and are used by 43 million legal-age consumers globally. This looks like successful habit migration. But the entire business depends on regulatory tolerance for IQOS and ZYN. If youth adoption triggers legislative action, if harm studies prompt restrictions, or if plain-packaging rules extend to smoke-free formats, the transition strategy collapses. PMI cannot defend cigarettes—that category faces structural headwinds. It must succeed with smoke-free, but regulatory permission is not guaranteed. The company's filings acknowledge this risk explicitly.

DraftKings illustrates how engagement metrics and revenue can diverge sharply. Q2 2026 showed sports consumer volume up 15% and user base up 9%, yet revenue fell 5% to $1.443B. Promotions and customer-friendly sports outcomes consumed the value. The habit—checking accounts, placing bets, chasing favorites—is strong. The conversion to profit is fragile. Hold volatility and promotional reinvestment override engagement growth.

Why now

The addiction dividend thesis was built on regulatory permissiveness (tobacco had its place), cultural acceptance (loyalty programs seemed permanent), and consumption expansion (subscriptions seemed infinite). But the inflection is now visible in filings: regulatory bodies are restricting nicotine products, consumer attention is fragmenting across competing subscriptions, and cultural identity premiums attached to legacy brands have eroded. Companies built infrastructure during expansion. These filings show contraction phase pressure.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
The consumer who holds the habit pays twice: once in direct cost (monthly dues, purchase price), and again in sunk-cost psychology that keeps them showing up even as the experience deteriorates. When the habit finally breaks—brand relevance fades, regulation tightens, a competitor offers the same signal at half the cost—the consumer stops paying and exits. The loyalty member is gone.
Who profits
In the expansion phase, nobody profits; companies invest in infrastructure and consumer education at massive cost. In the plateau phase, only companies that manage the transition profit: PMI through smoke-free premiums, DraftKings through multi-product cross-sell and adjusted EBITDA expansion ($620M in 2025), Planet Fitness through scale efficiencies despite membership pressure. Companies that relied on loyalty as a proxy for durability without solving product and brand problems see profitability collapse: Kohl's and Bath & Body Works demonstrate this clearly.

The tension

The addiction dividend breaks when product relevance erodes, regulation tightens, or a cheaper substitute emerges offering the same behavioral signal. At that inflection, loyalty infrastructure becomes a liability that proves the consumer used to care, making their exit undeniable. The company cannot hide the defection behind macro headwinds because the membership data is real-time. The shift from growth to decline is immediate.

The surprising part

Larger loyalty bases accelerate the downside rather than cushion it. Kohl's 40-million-member base made the 4.0% sales decline more visible and damaging to credibility. Bath & Body Works' 40-million-member base did not prevent 11% operating-income decline. Planet Fitness' membership growth of 1.1M in 2025 looked strong (1.0% growth on 20.8M base) until the company explicitly paused Black Card price increases and reframed the year as a membership-repair story, not a growth story. The infrastructure built to create pricing power created instead an obligation to fix it.

Why it matters

This matters because you are in these systems: part of PMI's 43-million-user smoke-free base, or Kohl's 40-million loyalty program, or Planet Fitness' 20.8-million membership. The price you pay and the experience you receive depends on whether the underlying product or brand stays relevant. If regulation tightens, if the brand fades, or if you find a cheaper substitute, you will leave. These filings show that when you do, the company has no fallback; the loyalty infrastructure that was supposed to keep you was built on the assumption you would never question it.

A loyalty base of 40 million becomes evidence of failure when the brand loses relevance—the larger the base, the more visible the defection.
