When the Habit Breaks: Regulatory Pressure, Substitution, and the Limits of Stickiness

A 40-million-member loyalty base cannot stop decline when the brand loses cultural relevance and the product erodes.

The addiction dividend contains a hidden fragility: habits can be disrupted by regulation, generational substitution, or competing habits that offer the same identity signal at lower cost. Companies that built moats around behavioral infrastructure discover those moats crumble when the underlying product or regulatory environment shifts. Loyalty membership size alone does not guarantee revenue.

The mechanism

The addiction dividend thesis assumes that once a habit is formed and wrapped in identity, it becomes durable and profitable. Loyalty infrastructure, subscription billing, and behavioral conditioning create a moat. But the company filings reveal a critical blind spot: the habit is only as durable as three unstable foundations—the underlying product, the regulatory permission, and the consumer's willingness to absorb the cost. When any one of these shifts, membership numbers become a vanity metric, not a revenue anchor.

Kohl's exemplifies this pattern. The company ended fiscal 2025 with large-scale customer reach, rewards programs, credit-card economics, and omnichannel tools. Yet net sales decreased 4.0% to $14.8B, comparable sales decreased 3.1%, and diluted EPS was $2.38. Management repeatedly emphasized value, inventory cleanup, and Sephora adjacency—not because loyalty failed, but because it never solved the deeper erosion. The loyalty base proved worthless when the product and brand no longer signaled the right identity. Kohl's loyalty members did not abandon the program; they abandoned Kohl's because the brand stopped mattering. Bath & Body Works tells the same story differently. With 40 million active loyalty members, direct e-commerce reach, 1,927 company-operated stores, and an explicit Consumer First Formula transformation, the company was still unable to stop a 0.2% sales decline in fiscal 2025. Operating income fell 11% to $1.126B. Again, the loyalty infrastructure did not break. The consumer's willingness to show up did.

Philip Morris International faces a different but equally real fragility. The smoke-free business now accounts for 41.5% of revenues, with 43 million legal-age consumers using IQOS, ZYN, and nicotine pouches. Management frames this as a transition success. But the model depends entirely on regulatory tolerance. If IQOS or ZYN prove harmful at scale, if youth adoption rates trigger legislative action, or if plain-packaging rules extend to smoke-free formats, the entire habit-migration strategy fails. PMI cannot default to defending cigarettes—that category faces structural headwinds. It must succeed with smoke-free, but success is not assured. The company's filings acknowledge this risk; investors discount it. That is the fragility.

Why now

The addiction dividend thesis was born in an era of regulatory permissiveness and cultural acceptance. Tobacco had its place, streaming subscriptions seemed infinite, gaming was becoming normalized, and loyalty programs were seen as permanent moats. But the inflection has arrived: regulatory bodies are moving toward restrictions on nicotine products, consumer attention is fragmenting across more subscriptions than any household can sustain, and the cultural identity premium attached to many habit-based brands has eroded. The companies built their infrastructure during the expansion phase. Now the contraction phase exposes which habits were truly sticky and which were just expensive.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
The consumer who held the habit pays twice: first in the cost of the subscription or product, and second in the sunk-cost psychology that keeps them showing up even when the experience deteriorates. But when the habit finally breaks—when the brand stops mattering, when regulation tightens, or when a competitor offers the same identity signal at half the cost—the consumer stops paying and walks away. The loyalty member is gone.
Who profits
During the expansion phase, nobody. The companies built infrastructure and educated consumers at massive cost. During the plateau phase, the companies that manage the transition profit: PMI through smoke-free premiums, Planet Fitness through scale efficiencies, and DraftKings through multi-product cross-sell. But the companies that fail to manage the transition—those that relied on loyalty membership as a proxy for durability without solving the product and brand problem—see profitability collapse. Kohl's and Bath & Body Works are in that category.

The tension

The addiction dividend breaks when the product loses cultural relevance, when regulation tightens, or when a cheaper substitute emerges that delivers the same behavioral signal. At that point, the loyalty infrastructure becomes a liability: it proves the consumer used to care, which makes their exit more visible and more damaging to earnings guidance. The company cannot hide the defection behind macro headwinds because the loyalty data is real-time. The inflection from membership growth to membership erosion is immediate and undeniable.

The surprising part

The most counterintuitive finding is that larger loyalty bases accelerate the downside rather than cushion it. Kohl's 40-million-member base made the sales decline more visible and more damaging to management credibility. Bath & Body Works' 40-million-member base did not prevent operating-income decline. Planet Fitness' membership growth of 1.1 million in 2025 looked strong on a 20.8-million-member base, but the company explicitly paused Black Card price increases and reframed the year as a membership-repair story, not a growth story. The loyalty infrastructure that was supposed to create durable pricing power instead created obligation to show up and fix it.

Why it matters

This matters to a normal person because it exposes the fragility of their own habits. You are part of PMI's 43-million-user base, or Kohl's 40-million-member loyalty program, or Planet Fitness' 20.8-million-member system. The company's ability to keep you engaged and the price you pay depends on whether the underlying product or brand stays relevant. If regulation tightens, if the identity signal fades, or if you discover a cheaper substitute, you will leave. The addiction dividend thesis assumes you stay; the tension reveals why you might not.

A loyalty membership of 40 million is worthless if the product erodes and the brand stops mattering—and the larger the membership, the more visible and damaging the defection.
