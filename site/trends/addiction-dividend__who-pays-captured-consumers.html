The Cost of the Loop: Who Actually Pays for the Addiction Dividend

Low-income consumers bear the disproportionate cost of the habits that generate returns for addiction-dividend companies—spending a higher share of income on inelastic, compulsive behaviors while public health systems absorb the downstream damage.

The addiction dividend is not a victimless profit structure. Companies that successfully habituate consumers—Philip Morris (nicotine products), DraftKings (sports betting), Electronic Arts (live services)—extract wealth from the consumers least able to refuse, externalize all costs of addiction onto public health systems and families, and concentrate the returns into shareholder value. A dependent consumer spends 15%+ of household income on cigarettes, daily sports betting, or gaming progression before cutting spending on food or housing. This is not a choice in the economic sense—it is compulsion monetized.

The mechanism

The addiction dividend works by converting habit into recurring revenue streams captured entirely by companies and shareholders. Philip Morris generated $40 billion in revenue in 2025 by selling nicotine products to 43 million dependent consumers. For dependent users, nicotine spending is non-discretionary—a pack costing $8-12 daily is purchased before food or rent. A household earning $25,000 annually spends 15%+ of income on cigarettes alone. Price elasticity is near zero: when incomes are squeezed, dependent consumers cut other essentials first. PMI's EPS grew 67% year-over-year precisely because the captured consumer base cannot switch away, allowing price increases that deepen poverty for those already dependent.

DraftKings demonstrates the same extraction mechanism through sports betting. With $53.6 billion in Sportsbook handle and 7.1% net revenue margin in 2025, the company extracted approximately $3.75 billion in theoretical gross winnings from millions of daily users. The company's litigation disclosures explicitly acknowledge addiction claims and responsible-gaming litigation as operational risks. Yet the earnings presentations frame this as growth and engagement. What they do not disclose is what fraction of revenue comes from users exhibiting compulsive-play behavior. Industry research suggests the most engaged users—who return daily and drive the highest revenue—are also the highest-risk users. DraftKings profits from the overlap between engagement and addiction.

Electronic Arts' live-services model (71% of FY2026 revenue at $8.026 billion) concentrates the same dynamic into seasonal games and progression systems. The company monetizes repeat seasons and identity investment inside Ultimate Team and Apex Legends where consumers spend disproportionately high shares of income on cosmetics and progression. For gaming-addicted teenagers and young adults, this becomes a competition for income with other needs. The WHO recognizes gaming disorder. EA's earnings reflect none of the treatment costs borne by healthcare systems or the lost productivity borne by families and employers.

Why now

The addiction dividend reached critical industrial scale in 2025-2026. Philip Morris smoke-free products represent 41.5% of company revenue—this transition occurred over five years with aggressive global rollout. DraftKings expanded to 27+ states in 2025-2026 while launching prediction markets as new monetization frontiers. Electronic Arts reached record $8.026 billion net bookings with live services as the dominant earnings driver. These are no longer emerging business models; they are central to multinational earnings. Simultaneously, regulatory pressure is building: DraftKings disclosed $620M in adjusted EBITDA in 2025 but faces litigation over addiction claims; PMI faces global regulatory scrutiny on harm-reduction claims for IQOS and ZYN. The inflection is that at scale, the addiction dividend model can no longer hide behind individual-choice framing. It is now an industrial system designed to maximize extraction from populations most vulnerable to habit.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Low-income consumers pay the largest price as a percentage of household income. A $10 daily cigarette spend is 15%+ of a $25,000 annual salary. Daily sports bettors (placing $20-100 wagers) are withdrawing from households also carrying healthcare, childcare, and housing cost burdens. The user base is concentrated in 18-35 age cohorts and older adults on fixed incomes—populations with minimal accumulated wealth. Public health systems pay the second price. Smoking-related disease costs U.S. healthcare $225+ billion annually (lung cancer, COPD, heart disease). Gambling disorder treatment costs an estimated $5 billion annually, mostly through public insurance. Families pay a third price through lost wages, mental-health crises, and relationship breakdown. None of these costs appear on Philip Morris, DraftKings, or Electronic Arts balance sheets.
Who profits
Shareholders capture the value with perfect efficiency. PMI distributed $7.54 in adjusted diluted EPS in 2025—67% above 2024. DraftKings generated $620 million in adjusted EBITDA in 2025 and is building toward $1 billion in 2026 while executing share buybacks. Electronic Arts generated $2.553 billion in operating cash flow in FY2026 directed toward new acquisitions and game development. Snap, despite remaining unprofitable on GAAP basis, generated $656 million in operating cash flow in 2025 by monetizing 971 million users through advertising—a significant portion from addiction-dividend companies promoting their products. Institutional investors own the majority of shares and capture value through dividends and capital appreciation. Workers in these companies capture salaries. The consumer bearing the highest cost of the habit captures nothing.

The tension

Addiction-dividend profits depend on externalizing harm that regulation and litigation may force internalized. Litigation around responsible gaming, addiction disclosure, and marketing practices is already mounting for DraftKings and Philip Morris. If healthcare systems begin billing addictive-product companies for treatment costs (as attempted in Europe), margin structures collapse. If youth-protection regulations tighten on gaming or if advertising bans extend to sports betting, revenue growth stops. If users develop alternatives (vaping for nicotine, skill-based games without gambling), switching friction dissolves. The business model is durable only while external costs remain external.

The surprising part

The most non-obvious part is that addiction-dividend companies simultaneously acknowledge addiction as legal reality while optimizing business models to maximize engagement with exactly those users most vulnerable to addiction. Philip Morris spent $40 billion annually building distribution for nicotine products and explicitly frames the business as a nicotine migration engine. DraftKings launched Predictions—new real-money speculation—immediately after profitability, rather than using that profit to reduce customer-acquisition spend. Electronic Arts aggressively pushes cosmetic monetization and battle-pass sales in games played by teenagers. These companies are not denying addiction exists. They have decided the legal and reputational cost of addiction is cheaper than the revenue cost of product redesign. The litigation reserves exist. The responsible-gaming disclosures exist. But monetization intensity only increases.

Why it matters

The social cost of the addiction dividend is inequality embedded in corporate structure. When companies design core business models around maximizing repeat spending from consumers least equipped to refuse, the entire earnings power is built on extracting wealth from lower-income households. This is not ordinary business profit. It is industrial monetization of human vulnerability. A person addicted to nicotine or compulsively gambling is not exercising choice—they are exhibiting compulsion. When billions in shareholder value are generated by optimizing for compulsion, the result is not a market rewarding efficiency or innovation. It is a market rewarding the ability to capture people in cycles of repeat spending they cannot escape. This creates a durable mechanism for wealth transfer away from those who can least afford it, with the explicit design and approval of major corporations and their investors.

The addiction dividend is a transfer of wealth from the consumers least able to afford it to the shareholders most able to capture it, with the full cost of addiction externalized onto public health systems and families.
