The Cost of the Loop: Who Actually Pays for the Addiction Dividend

Low-income consumers bear the disproportionate cost of the habits that generate returns for addiction-dividend companies.

The addiction dividend is not a victimless profit structure. Companies that have successfully habituated consumers to cigarettes, gaming, sports betting, and other repeat-spend products externalize their costs onto the consumers themselves—who spend a disproportionate share of income on inelastic habits—and onto public health systems that absorb the downstream damage. The profits flow upward while the burden flows downward.

The mechanism

The addiction dividend works by converting habit into recurring revenue. Philip Morris generated $40 billion in net revenues in 2025 by continuing to sell cigarettes while pioneering a migration toward smoke-free products like IQOS and nicotine pouches. Across 43 million consumers, the company has built a self-renewing cash engine where the consumer—not the product innovation—bears the true cost. A pack of cigarettes or a daily nicotine pouch is not a discretionary purchase for dependent users. The price elasticity is near zero. This means that when Philip Morris raises prices or when consumers exhaust their disposable income, they cut spending on food, healthcare, or housing before they cut nicotine spending. For low-income households, the addiction dividend translates directly into poverty-deepening repeat spend.

DraftKings demonstrates the same pattern through sports betting. With $53.6 billion in Sportsbook handle in 2025 and a 7.1% net revenue margin, the company captured $3.75 billion in theoretical gross winnings before paying out bonuses, free plays, and other promotional incentives. The company's earnings presentation frames this as growth and engagement—and from the user side, it is participation and entertainment. But from the financial side, it is the extraction of disposable income from millions of daily users who experience the product as a form of second-screen entertainment tied to sports they care about. The most engaged users, who return most frequently and contribute the most revenue, are also the most at-risk users. DraftKings acknowledges this in its litigation risk disclosures around responsible gaming and addiction claims. What it does not disclose is what fraction of revenue comes from users exhibiting signs of compulsive play.

Electronic Arts' live-services model concentrates the same dynamic into seasonal sports games and ongoing competitive loops. With 71% of FY2026 revenue coming from live services—primarily EA SPORTS FC's Ultimate Team and Apex Legends cosmetics—the company monetizes repeat seasons, progression systems, and identity investment inside games where consumers build teams and compete over months. The consumer experience is engagement, achievement, and community. The company experience is that this user base exhibits high retention, high lifetime value, and high willingness to pay. But the underlying truth is that much of this value capture depends on consumers spending proportionally more of their income on in-game progression than on other consumption. For gaming-addicted users, especially teenagers and young adults, this becomes a competition for income with other needs. Gaming disorder is recognized by the WHO. EA's earnings do not reflect the treatment costs that fall on healthcare systems or the lost productivity that falls on families and employers.

Why now

The addiction dividend has reached critical scale in 2025-2026 because regulated nicotine products (smoke-free alternatives), sports betting legalization across U.S. states, and mobile gaming monetization have all matured simultaneously. Philip Morris smoke-free products now represent 42% of company revenue after just five years of aggressive global rollout. DraftKings expanded from 26 to 27+ states in 2025-2026, with prediction markets launching as a new monetization frontier. Electronic Arts reached a record $8 billion in net bookings with live services as the dominant engine. These are no longer emerging business models—they are now central to the earnings power of major multinational companies. At scale, the addiction dividend can no longer be defended as the sum of individual choices. It is now an industrial system designed to maximize the extraction of repeat spending from populations most vulnerable to habit.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Low-income consumers pay the largest price as a share of household income. A pack of cigarettes costs $8–$12 and is purchased daily by dependent users; for a household earning $25,000 annually, this is a non-negotiable 15%+ budget item. Mobile gaming and sports betting users who gamble $20–$100 daily are withdrawing discretionary funds from households that also face healthcare, childcare, and housing cost burdens. The users are often young adults (18–35) who have not yet built wealth, or older adults on fixed incomes (Social Security, pensions). Public health systems pay the second price. Smoking-related disease (lung cancer, COPD, heart disease) costs U.S. healthcare $225+ billion annually. Gambling disorder treatment and emergency intervention cost an estimated $5 billion annually, mostly borne by public insurance. Families pay a third price through lost wages, mental health crises, and relationship breakdown tied to addiction. None of these costs appear on Philip Morris, DraftKings, or Electronic Arts' income statements.
Who profits
The profits flow upward with perfect efficiency. Philip Morris distributed $7.54 in adjusted diluted EPS to shareholders in 2025—a 67% increase from 2024. DraftKings generated $620 million in adjusted EBITDA in 2025 and is building toward an internal target of ~$1 billion in 2026, while repurchasing shares. Electronic Arts generated $2.553 billion in operating cash flow in FY2026, with management explicitly directing this toward new product frontiers like acquisition and new game development. Snap, despite still being unprofitable on a GAAP basis, generated $656 million in operating cash flow in 2025 by monetizing the attention of 971 million users through advertising that sells products (often including other addiction-dividend companies). The shareholder base captures the value through dividends, buybacks, and capital appreciation. Institutional investors and wealth managers own the majority of shares. Workers in these companies capture salaries. But the consumer bearing the highest cost of the habit captures none of the benefit.

The tension

The core tension is that addiction-dividend profits depend on externalized harm that regulation and public pressure may eventually force internalized. Litigation around responsible gaming, addiction disclosure, and marketing practices is already mounting for both DraftKings and Philip Morris. If healthcare systems begin to bill addictive-product companies for treatment costs (as some European countries have attempted with tobacco), the entire margin structure of the addiction dividend collapses. If youth protection regulations tighten around mobile gaming or if advertising bans extend to sports betting, revenue growth stops. If users develop methadone-equivalent alternatives (vaping for nicotine, skill-based games for engagement without gambling), the switching friction dissolves. The business model is durable only as long as the external costs remain external. The moment regulation, litigation, or technology force the company to absorb the cost, the addiction dividend reverses into the addiction loss.

The surprising part

The surprising part is that addiction-dividend companies are simultaneously willing to acknowledge addiction as a legal and operational reality while optimizing their business models to maximize engagement with exactly those users most vulnerable to addiction. Philip Morris spent $40 billion annually building distribution for nicotine products and explicitly frames the business as a nicotine migration engine. DraftKings launched Predictions—a new real-money speculation product—immediately after achieving profitability, rather than using that profitability to reduce customer acquisition spend. Electronic Arts continues to aggressively push cosmetic monetization and battle-pass sales inside games played by teenagers. These are not companies denying addiction exists. They are companies that have decided the legal and reputational cost of addiction is cheaper than the revenue cost of product redesign. The litigation reserves exist. The responsible-gaming disclosures exist. But the monetization intensity only increases.

Why it matters

The social cost of the addiction dividend is inequality on a structural scale. When companies design their core business model around maximizing repeat spending from consumers least equipped to refuse, the entire earnings power of those companies is built on extracting wealth from lower-income households. This is different from ordinary business profit. It is the industrial monetization of human vulnerability. A person who is addicted to nicotine or compulsively gambling is not exercising choice in the economic sense—they are exhibiting compulsion. When billions in shareholder value are generated by optimizing for compulsion, the result is not a market that rewards efficiency or innovation. It is a market that rewards the ability to capture people in cycles of repeat spending they cannot escape. This creates a durable mechanism for wealth transfer away from those who can least afford it, and it does so with the explicit design and approval of major corporations and their investors.

The addiction dividend is a transfer of wealth from the consumers least able to afford it to the shareholders most able to capture it, with the full cost of addiction externalized onto public health systems and families.
