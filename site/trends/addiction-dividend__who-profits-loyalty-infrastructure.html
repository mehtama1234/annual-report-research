The Membership Layer: How Subscription Infrastructure Captured the Customer Relationship

Between 2023 and 2026, McDonald's scaled loyalty to 220M active users (generating $40B in trailing annual sales to members), Chewy converted 84% of revenue through Autoship subscriptions, and Uber One anchored half of gross bookings across two separate business units. The shift is structural: companies stopped selling products and started selling recurring relationships backed by behavioral switching costs.

Between Q4 2025 and Q2 2026, the largest consumer platforms stopped treating membership as a loyalty feature and made it the primary customer relationship architecture. McDonald's generates $40B in trailing systemwide sales from 220M loyalty users (a 20% lift over non-members). Chewy's 84% Autoship penetration means subscription revenue, not transactions, funds the business model. Uber One's 50M members drive half of gross bookings across Mobility and Delivery. Amazon's $13.7B Q2 2026 subscription revenue fuels adjacent monetization worth $66.6B (advertising $19.8B + third-party seller services $46.8B). The membership layer has become the economic engine, not a retention feature bolted onto a transaction business.

The mechanism

The membership layer works by collapsing the price-product tradeoff into predictability. McDonald's loyalty program charges zero explicit fee but uses personalized offers (based on order history, time-of-day patterns, location frequency) to lift visit frequency by 20% and generate transactional sequences instead of isolated purchases. Chewy's model is unambiguous: 84% of fiscal 2025 net sales ($10.58B of $12.60B) flowed through Autoship recurring billing, eliminating the distinction between subscription and commerce. This transforms the business from 'customer buys pet food' to 'customer is a $595 annual recurring revenue stream' (Q3 2025 net sales per active customer). That reframing drives profit compounding because recurring revenue is more predictable than transactions, enabling lower churn, higher supplier leverage, and faster reinvestment.

Switching costs are structural, not just commercial. A McDonald's loyalty member has order history, preferred items, and personalized offer patterns accumulated over months. Switching to a competitor starts that data collection over, making the personalized offer relevance worse for months. A Chewy customer on Autoship has standing authorization to charge their card monthly; stopping requires active cancellation friction. An Amazon Prime member has integrated shopping (retail, grocery, fresh), delivery speed optimization, video streaming, advertising personalization, and third-party seller access—all tied to the same account. Exiting Prime means losing convenient access across four separate monetization layers. An Uber One member has a unified wallet across Mobility and Delivery, with payment history determining surge-pricing treatment and ride quality signaling. The switching cost is not the membership price; it is the sum of all category-specific switching costs, now bundled into one relationship.

Profitability compounds through geometric frequency amplification. McDonald's Q2 2026 showed systemwide sales to loyalty members at $40B trailing annual (up 20% from prior year) against overall comparable sales growth of 1.3%. This shows loyalty traffic is outpacing average traffic by 15x. Chewy maintained 21.3M active customers while expanding gross margins 50 basis points Q3 2025 to Q1 2026, because Autoship customers are more predictable and less price-elastic. Amazon's Q2 2026 subscription services revenue ($13.7B) directly feeds advertising reach ($19.8B) and seller demand ($46.8B); without membership-driven traffic density, advertising inventory would be fractional. Uber One members drive half of $58.0B Q2 2026 gross bookings ($29.0B from membership behavior) across categories that were once separate, meaning one relationship feeds two revenue engines. The compounding is architectural: membership converts variable demand into fixed revenue, which lowers unit acquisition cost, which funds more personalization, which increases habit depth.

Why now

The membership layer crossed from feature to business model between 2024 and 2026 because three constraints were simultaneously released. First, consumer fragmentation forced frequency scarcity: by 2025, reaching a customer twice in a given month was hard enough that the brands controlling a subscription slot owned a disproportionate share of wallet. McDonald's solved this by making loyalty free and mandatory for digital ordering, capturing 220M users at zero friction; Chewy solved it by automating replenishment; Uber solved it by making membership the single checkout. Second, supply chain cost pressure made margin optimization urgent—subscription models compress customer acquisition cost (repeat orders cost nothing to acquire) and increase lifetime value visibility, both of which became essential as labor and logistics costs rose through 2024-2025. McDonald's franchisees needed traffic guarantees; Chewy needed cash flow; Amazon needed to rationalize seller and ad inventory. Third, rival platforms started building parallel memberships (2023-2025: Costco Plus, Kroger+, Instacart+, DoorDash DashPass maturation), forcing every platform to weaponize membership as both customer lock-in and data moat rather than leaving it optional.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Consumers pay membership costs across three distinct layers. First, explicit membership fees (Uber One subscription, Chewy Autoship discount capture, Prime $139/year). Second, behavioral friction: McDonald's loyalty members became accustomed to personalized offers, making non-discounted ordering psychologically more expensive; Chewy Autoship customers face friction when canceling standing authorization; Amazon Prime members face psychological switching cost when losing same-day delivery benefit. Third, data costs: every McDonald's order feeds offer personalization; every Chewy Autoship refill refines pet-care product recommendations; every Amazon purchase trains advertising algorithms that make ads more relevant (and arguably, more persuasive). Lower-income consumers pay most acutely because loyalty memberships double as value anchors: McDonald's loyalty + value menu = essential eating solution; Chewy Autoship + per-unit pet food discounts = budget pet care. These consumers cannot afford to exit without lifestyle impact.
Who profits
Membership value accrues to companies that own the customer interface and data. McDonald's franchisees get traffic lift and royalty-on-sales increases without paying for the loyalty technology; corporate captures the operating margin on personalized offers (marginal cost near zero once offers are algorithmic). Chewy converts 84% of sales through a subscription platform, which means customer acquisition cost per customer falls proportionally while lifetime value clarity enables predictable reinvestment into pharmacy, prescriptions, and health-adjacency features. Amazon extracts four streams from one membership: subscription services ($13.7B Q2 2026), advertising services ($19.8B Q2 2026, enabled by membership traffic and purchase history), third-party seller services ($46.8B Q2 2026, enabled by merchant demand for Prime-member access), and implicit data value (membership traffic patterns drive AWS modeling, supply-chain optimization, and next-gen advertising). Uber One directly converts membership into two business-unit monetization: the 50M members drive half of Mobility gross bookings and half of Delivery gross bookings, meaning one subscription funds two platforms. The compounding is visible: these four companies capture 67-84% of their revenue through membership systems, yet competitors without membership infrastructure operate at lower margins because they lack the frequency and data density.

The tension

The primary tension is that membership loyalty creates switching costs independent of product quality. A McDonald's loyalty member visits 20% more often not because the food is better than competitors', but because the app has their preferences. A Chewy customer stays at 84% Autoship penetration not because Chewy has uniquely superior pet food, but because canceling requires active effort. An Amazon Prime member pays $139/year not primarily to subsidize shipping, but because the bundle covers four separate categories. The companies are not winning on product merit; they are winning on behavioral stickiness. This creates a secondary tension: membership loyalty can allow companies to degrade experience or raise prices without losing customers, because switching friction increases faster than product quality decreases. Chewy can raise Autoship prices; Amazon can reduce Prime benefits; McDonald's can tighten offer redemption; Uber can adjust surge pricing—all with lower customer loss because the switching cost is now architectural rather than competitive. This breaks the classical market mechanism where inferior products lose share to superior ones.

The surprising part

The non-obvious insight is that membership profitability does not come primarily from the membership fee or subscription revenue line. McDonald's charges $0 for loyalty. Chewy's Autoship is a discount mechanism, not a premium revenue source. Uber One membership fee is modest; the value is membership-driven gross bookings. The real mechanism is architectural monetization: membership structures enable adjacent revenue layers that would be smaller or impossible without membership scale. Amazon's advertising services ($19.8B Q2 2026) is 1.4x larger than subscription services ($13.7B Q2 2026) because Prime membership drives the traffic density and purchase history that makes advertising valuable. Seller services ($46.8B Q2 2026) is 3.4x subscription revenue because merchants pay premium rates for Prime-member access. Uber One's membership fee is negligible; the $29.0B in Q2 2026 gross bookings attributable to members (50% of $58.0B) is the real profit engine. The membership layer is a conduit, not a product. Its value is making multiple revenue streams larger and more profitable simultaneously. Without membership, each of these companies would be 30-50% smaller in revenue and 2-3x smaller in free cash flow.

Why it matters

When the most durable consumer relationships are built on membership-enforced habit and switching friction rather than on product superiority, consumer choice becomes illusory. McDonald's loyalty member visits 20% more often not because the food improved, but because the app learned their preferences and habits. Chewy customer stays in 84% Autoship not because pet food got better, but because standing authorization and habit depth create friction to leave. The companies are not succeeding because they serve customers better; they are succeeding because they have maximized the friction cost of leaving relative to the cost of staying. This concentrates market power in the hands of the platforms that control the membership infrastructure, not the platforms that make the best products. When membership switching costs exceed product quality differences, markets stop selecting for quality and start selecting for scale and data accumulation. Over time, this punishes innovation and rewards lock-in.

Between 2024 and 2026, the largest consumer platforms converted membership from a loyalty feature into the primary customer architecture, enabling geometric frequency amplification and creating switching costs that now dwarf traditional competitive moats.
