The Membership Layer: Where Loyalty Infrastructure Becomes a Pricing Machine

Companies with the most sophisticated loyalty infrastructure—Amazon Prime, Uber One, McDonald's at 220M 90-day actives, and Chewy's 84% Autoship mix—are not rewarding customers; they are converting transactional demand into subscription economics and behavioral data that compounds moats.

Membership-driven loyalty infrastructure has crossed from convenience feature into core business moat. Companies like McDonald's (220M 90-day active loyalty users generating $40B trailing-twelve-month systemwide sales), Chewy (84% of Q3 2025 net sales from Autoship, 21.3M active customers), and Amazon Prime (embedded in $716.9B full-year revenue ecosystem) demonstrate that the switching costs built into loyalty systems are now more valuable than the underlying products themselves.

The mechanism

The membership layer works by collapsing the distinction between subscription fee and product value. McDonald's does not charge a monthly fee for its 220M-strong loyalty program; instead, the reward structure—personalized offers, faster digital ordering—creates frequency that generates intrinsic data about time-of-day preferences, product mix sensitivity, and location-based patterns. That data then funds the next generation of personalized offers. Chewy's model is even more explicit: 84% of Q3 2025 net sales flow through Autoship, which means the subscription frame has completely replaced the transactional frame. A customer is not buying pet food; they are in a recurring relationship that generates revenue predictability, customer lifetime value clarity, and leverage with suppliers.

Switching costs in these systems are relational, not just commercial. Amazon Prime members pay for shipping speed and convenience, but they remain because the relationship encompasses third-party seller access, advertising exposure, subscription video, grocery delivery, and ad-supported shopping—a bundle so entangled that exiting requires forgoing entire categories of convenience. McDonald's loyalty users accumulate personalization data: the app learns not just that you bought a Big Mac, but when, with what else, and which offers moved you to frequency. Exiting means restarting that data collection with a competitor. Uber One members get half-price rides and delivery, but they also get a single wallet that works across mobility and food. The data and the behavior become inseparable from the identity.

The profitability compounding arrives because membership turns customer acquisition into retention economics. Chewy's loyalty-driven model generated record free cash flow of $562M in fiscal 2025 while maintaining 21.3M active customers and gross margin expansion. McDonald's 90-day active user base grew to 220M while systemwide sales to loyalty members hit $40B trailing-twelve-month. Amazon's subscription services revenue hit $13.7B in Q2 2026 and feeds into advertising services ($19.8B Q2 2026) and third-party seller services ($46.8B Q2 2026)—all layers that benefit from the membership relationship. Frequency compounds because habit-reinforcement works: the more personalized the offers, the higher the engagement; the higher the engagement, the more data; the more data, the more personalization. Membership economics turn that into guaranteed recurring revenue, not hope.

Why now

Three conditions converged to make membership-driven loyalty structural rather than tactical. First, digital storefronts enabled direct data capture without intermediaries: McDonald's can track individual 90-day actives across 70 loyalty markets and surface that in quarterly earnings; Chewy can know exactly which customers are on Autoship and what their lifetime value looks like. Second, consumer attention fragmented so much that frequency became scarce—the brands that owned a recurring slot in the customer's app or delivery behavior had a structural edge. Third, supply-chain cost pressure and margin compression made the membership fee or subscription economics essential. McDonald's systemwide sales to loyalty members grew 20% while overall comp growth was 3.1%; Chewy's Autoship customers retain at rates that standalone e-commerce cannot match. Membership transformed from loyalty program into business model.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Consumers in membership systems pay in three ways: first, they pay the explicit membership or subscription fee (Uber One, Chewy Autoship discount capture). Second, they pay through behavioral lock-in—once you are personalized into a system, switching has real friction. Third, they pay through data: frequency and purchase patterns become predictive assets that the company uses to further monetize the relationship. Lower-income customers feel this most acutely because these memberships are often bundled with value positioning (McDonald's loyalty + value menu + digital offers; Chewy Autoship + lower per-unit pet food costs), making them feel essential rather than optional.
Who profits
The platforms that own these memberships capture outsized value. McDonald's franchisees and corporate benefit from loyalty-driven traffic increases. Amazon extracts value across four surfaces simultaneously: subscription services revenue, advertising services revenue, third-party seller monetization, and the demand data that improves all of them. Chewy converts 84% of sales through a subscription interface, reducing customer acquisition costs and increasing lifetime value. Uber One drives half of Mobility and Delivery gross bookings, which means the membership fee anchors two separate business units. The compounding comes from having control over both the consumer relationship and the data generated by that relationship—something traditional loyalty programs (run by third parties) never achieved.

The tension

The tension is that membership loyalty creates an artificial moat based on switching costs rather than on superior product or service. If McDonald's loyalty users leave, it is because they got frustrated or their circumstances changed, not because another fast-casual chain has equal or better food. If Chewy loses a customer, it is usually because the customer relationship is strong enough that they stay through price increases or service friction. That creates the classic monopoly-style tension: the company stops needing to compete on experience quality once the membership stickiness is high enough. The second tension is data concentration: as memberships mature, the companies holding these relationships accumulate behavioral data about customers that competitors cannot easily replicate, creating asymmetric power. Consumer data rights and privacy become flashpoints precisely because the membership economics depend on data being non-transparent.

The surprising part

The non-obvious part is that membership profitability does not come primarily from the membership fee itself. McDonald's does not charge to join; Chewy's Autoship is a discount program, not an upfront fee. The real profit mechanism is that membership structures convert random transactions into sequences, which enables adjacent monetization. Amazon's subscription services ($13.7B Q2 2026) is dwarfed by advertising services ($19.8B Q2 2026), which would be a fraction of that without Prime membership driving traffic and data. Uber One members drive half of gross bookings, but the membership fee is not the core profit engine; the ability to keep those high-frequency users engaged across two categories is. Membership profitability is architectural, not linear.

Why it matters

When the most durable consumer relationships are built on membership-enforced habit and switching costs rather than on superior product quality, consumer choice becomes an illusion. If McDonald's loyalty gets you to eat there 20% more often, and Chewy's Autoship makes pet food a background subscription you barely notice, then the companies are not succeeding because they are better—they are succeeding because you are locked in. The broader social cost is that the companies with the best loyalty infrastructure are not necessarily the ones serving customers best; they are the ones best at converting convenience into habit and habit into lock-in. That shapes market power and pricing power in ways that hurt consumers over time.

Membership-driven loyalty infrastructure converts transactional demand into recurring relationships and behavioral data that compounds switching costs and creates profitable habit formation at scale.
