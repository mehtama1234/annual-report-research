Trends · The Affordability Ceiling

The Department Store Was Always a Bet on the Middle Class—and It Stopped Working

The mid-market retail format was built for postwar mass aspiration; when income stagnation met tariffs and inflation, the affordability ceiling became a floor that couldn't support the model anymore.

The American department store was engineered around a specific economic bet: that a broad middle class would consistently trade up for branded, curated merchandise at predictable price points. That bet sustained Macy's, Nordstrom, and aspirational specialty retail for fifty years. The inflection point came between 2008 (income stagnation) and 2020 (tariffs, supply-chain disruption, inflation), collapsing the middle by squeezing both aspirational uplift and margin recovery.

The mechanism

The postwar department store was built on a straightforward model: anchor the mall with prestige and breadth, let specialty stores trade on the traffic, and capture the middle-income customer who saw department-store shopping as a gateway to upward mobility. Macy's, Nordstrom, and their mall-anchoring peers succeeded because they offered controlled aspiration—brand curation, personal service, seasonal fashion events—at price points that felt accessible to families making $50,000 to $100,000 per year. That customer experienced the department store as a place where you could feel temporarily aspirational without overextending.

The model depended on three structural supports that held from 1945 through 2007. First, real wages for middle-income workers grew steadily, making the annual coat, the special-occasion dress, the upgraded sheets feel like attainable rewards rather than budget violations. Second, inventory depth and turn discipline allowed retailers to manage margins by controlling supply flow and seasonal marking—not through aggressive promotions, but through scarcity signaling. Third, the real estate model (mall anchor lease at high premium, recouped by customer density) worked because foot traffic was reliable and mall culture was still the default format for middle-class shopping.

Between 2008 and 2020, all three supports fractured simultaneously. Real wage growth stalled for workers without college degrees—exactly the core demographic of department-store customers—while the 2008 financial crisis taught middle-income households to rebuild savings and reduce discretionary spending. Meanwhile, e-commerce and big-box formats (Target, Costco, Walmart) began unbundling the department-store value proposition: you could get brand assortment on Amazon, basics at Walmart, and fashion at Zara, paying only for what you wanted instead of subsidizing the full curated experience. The department store's answer—aggressive markdowns and promotional bundling—worked temporarily but eroded margins and trained customers that the full-price business model was optional. By 2020, tariffs, supply-chain disruption, and inflation added a final stress layer: the cost of goods rose faster than the middle-income customer could absorb, and retailers faced the choice between absorbing margin compression or pushing prices higher and accepting traffic loss. Macy's, Bath & Body Works, and Nordstrom all tried both, and neither worked cleanly.

Why now

The affordability ceiling became visible in 2024–2026 because the three historical damage vectors finally converged. Macy's most recent data shows the core nameplate (the traditional department-store banner) growing at only 1.6% in Q1 2026, while its faster-growing banners—Bloomingdale's (10.2% comp growth) and Bluemercury (6.4%)—are both luxury-positioned, serving a different customer entirely. Bath & Body Works shows the specialty-retail variant of the same pattern: a 40-million-member loyalty base and nearly 2,000 stores could not prevent Q1 2026 sales from falling 3% because the core customer could not afford both the brand's existing price points and other necessities. ACCO Brands illustrates the everyday-equipment version: even school-driven seasonality and the back-to-school sell-in can only offset structural erosion when the company must resort to $60 million in cost-cutting just to stabilize EPS. What changed is that the middle-market retailer can no longer rely on growth or margin recovery to fix the equation. Labor costs are up, tariffs are structural (not cyclical), consumer discretionary is constrained, and competitive formats have already captured the customer's first purchase decision. The affordability ceiling is the point at which the mid-market customer stops trading up within the category and simply stops buying—or switches to competitors who compete on price instead of curation.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
The middle-income household shopping for apparel, home goods, office supplies, or specialty beauty is the one who bears the cost. This is not abstract economic pressure; it is the household deciding between a new outfit at Macy's and a home repair, between shopping at Bath & Body Works and a tank of gas, between back-to-school shopping at a specialty retailer and rebuilding emergency savings. The affordability ceiling hits when the product you used to buy as an attainable reward no longer feels attainable. For Macy's customers, that ceiling arrived around 2022–2024; for Bath & Body Works, it happened even as the company maintained loyalty messaging; for ACCO, it means back-to-school shopping moved earlier in the season or shifted to lower-price retailers. The human cost is that curated, branded retail—which used to feel like middle-class culture—became a luxury good, not an accessible one.
Who profits
Luxury competitors capture the customer's premium discretionary dollar. Bloomingdale's growth (10.2% comps) comes at Macy's expense; Bluemercury similarly serves a higher-income customer who is less constrained by the affordability ceiling. E-commerce and private-label retailers (Zara, H&M, Target) capture price-conscious customers by offering lower entry prices. Amazon and Walmart capture the convenience and value combination that middle-market departmental retail used to own. The real profiteer is the capital markets' preference for asset-light models: brands that own intellectual property and licensing but not stores (like Nike) or platforms that aggregate supply without holding inventory (like Amazon, Shein, TikTok Shop) both outperform the legacy department-store model. Cost-cutting companies like ACCO, which extracted $60 million in cumulative savings and targeted $100 million by end-2026, convert that value upward to management and shareholders, not downward to customers or workers.

The tension

The core tension is that the affordability ceiling exposes the retail model's dependence on customer wage growth that no longer exists. The postwar department store was architected around the assumption that customers would reach higher-income brackets over time and trade up within the store. When that assumption breaks—when the 35-year-old middle-income customer in 2026 has lower real wages than their parent did in 1995—the entire curated, margin-dependent model becomes fragile. Retailers responded by lowering prices (destroying margin), opening loyalty programs (subsidizing price with data), and diversifying into media and marketplace (which add complexity without fixing the underlying customer affordability problem). The externalized cost is that workers in both retail and supply-chain sectors face job cuts (Macy's closed stores; ACCO and Bath & Body Works both saw store count pressure), while customers in the once-aspirational middle class experience the psychological erosion of watching the 'nice' version of consumption move out of reach.

The surprising part

The most counterintuitive finding is that loyalty scale and store count do not prevent the affordability ceiling from breaking the model. Bath & Body Works has 40 million active loyalty members and operates nearly 2,000 stores across North America and internationally. That asset base should be a moat. Instead, Q1 2026 showed sales declining 3% even with that loyalty depth because the brand could not solve the underlying customer constraint: people with $50,000-$100,000 household income are not discretionary enough to support a $7+ billion specialty brand at scale. The second surprise is that Macy's recovery story (1.5% comparable sales growth, positive momentum) is actually a narrowing of the customer base: the strength is coming from Bloomingdale's and Bluemercury, which serve higher-income customers, and from Macy's Media Network ($188 million in 2025 revenue), which monetizes customer attention without selling more merchandise. The middle-market department store is not recovering; it is being replaced by a luxury-and-data business that shares the name but serves a different customer.

Why it matters

This matters to the ordinary person because the affordability ceiling marks the moment when 'retail' as a cultural experience—shopping as a middle-class activity, the store as a place to feel aspirational—became formally out of reach for the customer it was built to serve. The stores themselves will not disappear (Macy's is restructuring, not liquidating; Bath & Body Works is running transformation programs), but the social meaning has shifted. Where department-store shopping used to signal that you had arrived in the middle class, it now signals either nostalgia or that you have moved up into the luxury tier. For workers and local economies, the toll is more immediate: store closures, reduced hours, and the shift of inventory and merchandising power to companies like Amazon that do not operate the same physical-labor model. Politically and culturally, the affordability ceiling exposes the gap between what middle-income households can actually afford and what consumer capitalism promised them when income growth was reliable. The department store's decline is not just a retail problem; it is a marker that the postwar social contract—where steady work meant steady access to branded, curated, 'nice' things—has become a luxury good itself.

When middle-class wages stagnated but retail costs kept rising, the department store's century-long bet on affordable aspiration became economically impossible.
