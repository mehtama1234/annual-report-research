Trends · The Affordability Ceiling

Who Pays the Squeezed Majority

Working-class households are locking out of contractor services, specialty retail, and big-ticket projects—not by choice but by wage stagnation against price inflation. When affordability pressure forces retreat into DIY, value channels, and deferred maintenance, middle-market brands and mid-tier services lose volume and margin simultaneously.

The affordability ceiling is compressing the middle market. Wage growth has lagged cumulative inflation through 2024-2026, forcing working and lower-middle-class households into value channels while professional contractors and specialty retailers face demand collapse. Home Depot's comparable sales growth of 0.3% in FY2025 and 0.4% in Q1 2026 reflects the ceiling: repair traffic holds but large-ticket discretionary projects vanish. Bath & Body Works' 0.2% revenue decline and Robert Half's 2.5% permanent placement growth both show the same pressure. The shift is structural, not cyclical—the middle market cannot hold when consumers choose between paying for projects or paying for housing.

The mechanism

Housing affordability pressure forces households to defer large remodel projects and trade work. Home Depot reports that after adjusting for storm-driven anomalies, underlying demand remains pressured by housing affordability constraints. The company can maintain traffic through repair and maintenance, but large-ticket discretionary work is vanishing. Home Depot's pro-contractor strategy through SRS and GMS distribution—1,300+ SRS branches and 16,000 delivery assets with $400M cross-sell run rate—signals that management has accepted that consumer remodeling will not return to historical levels. The company is building around contractors and commercial customers rather than waiting for housing normalization.

Middle-income consumers retreat from specialty retail and branded goods into value channels. Bath & Body Works' FY2025 sales declined 0.2%, with direct channel dropping 2.4% and store traffic under pressure despite 40 million loyalty members. This is not a loyalty-program failure—it is a discretionary-spending collapse. PepsiCo's management noted that U.S. food and beverage demand moderated as consumer budgets tightened. Households are still eating and buying, but through cheaper formats and private-label alternatives. The company prioritized productivity savings and value positioning, not top-line growth.

White-collar hiring demand remains soft despite staffing-market improvement. Robert Half's permanent placement grew 2.5% adjusted YoY in Q2 2026, but this represents half the historical normalized rate after three consecutive quarters of negative growth. Protiviti consulting remains resilient only because enterprises need compliance and risk work regardless of hiring cycles. Working-class use of staffing services shrinks as companies switch to tighter hiring or gig arrangements. The bifurcation is visible: firms hire for mission-critical roles but freeze discretionary headcount.

Why now

The inflection point is post-2022 cumulative inflation. Nominal wage growth through 2025 has lagged cumulative price increases across housing, energy, food, and services since early 2022. Fed rate increases from March 2022 through July 2023 compressed housing affordability immediately; mortgage-rate pressure has persisted even as Fed cuts began in September 2024. By 2026, households with fixed or slowly-growing incomes face a permanent reduction in discretionary purchasing power relative to 2021. Home Depot's comparable sales tell the story precisely: 0.3% growth in FY2025 after housing-affordability constraints mounted. Bath & Body Works' decline accelerated as consumers cut discretionary purchases. Robert Half's permanent-placement growth inflected from negative to barely-positive in Q2 2026, but never recovered to historical growth rates. This is not a recession scenario—employment remains tight—but it is a permanent repricing of what working households can afford to spend on services and specialty goods.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Working and lower-middle-class households deferring home projects, cutting contractor use, and trading down from specialty retailers to value channels. Professionals cutting back on hiring help for personal services. Employers freezing permanent headcount while monitoring temp-staffing use. Smaller regional contractors and mid-market service providers who cannot compete on price and are squeezed by direct competition from Home Depot and other platform consolidators. Workers in retail, hospitality, and skilled trades facing wage pressure and reduced hours as consumer spending contracts.
Who profits
Ultra-low-cost retailers, value private-label suppliers, DIY and self-service operators, gig and contingent platforms, and efficient contractors who consolidate market share. Large platforms like Home Depot capture repair and maintenance traffic but see big-project revenue vanish. Specialty retailers like Bath & Body Works lose full-price sales and face either emergency transformation or margin compression. Staffing firms lose permanent placement demand and compete for contingent work on tighter margins.

The tension

The middle market cannot hold. Home Depot's pro-distribution strategy suggests management has accepted that consumer remodeling is unlikely to normalize. Bath & Body Works' Consumer First Formula is not a recovery playbook—it is an emergency transformation. Robert Half's permanent placement growth of 2.5% is half the normalized rate, suggesting employers expect sluggish white-collar hiring to persist. If affordability remains tight, the next phase is either accelerated consolidation (strong players buy weak players' customer bases) or permanent reshaping of the market into high-end and value-only segments with a hollowed middle. Communities that depended on construction activity, skilled trades, and specialty retail employment face structural job loss.

The surprising part

Home Depot is not in crisis, but it is structurally preparing for a permanently lower consumer-remodel environment. Rather than betting on housing normalization, the company is becoming more pro-trade, more delivery-dense, and more infrastructure-like. This is a winning move in an affordability-ceiling scenario, but it also signals management's actual view: consumer project demand is unlikely to return to historical levels, and the company is building a business that works without it. This is not a cyclical downturn response—it is a structural pivot.

Why it matters

The health of the middle-class economy depends on access to skilled labor, discretionary goods, and the confidence to invest in homes and experiences. When affordability pressure forces retreat into DIY and deferred maintenance, it signals deteriorating household financial security and the loss of the economic layer that historically supported skilled trades, mid-market services, and specialty retail brands. The concentration of retail into ultra-low-cost and ultra-premium segments leaves fewer employment opportunities in the middle for workers without advanced degrees.

Middle-income households are locking out of contractor services, specialty retail, and big-ticket projects due to sustained wage-growth lags, forcing suppliers to choose between value-segment survival and premium-niche escape.
