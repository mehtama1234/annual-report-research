AI Cultural Disintermediation

AI is not just doing the work of human experts — it is absorbing their authority. The platforms that own the AI layer are capturing the trust, the data, and the economic relationship that credentialed intermediaries spent careers building.

Between 2024 and 2026, AI capability crossed a threshold where it could perform judgment-level work — underwriting, customer escalation, diagnostic triage, creative optimization — at a quality acceptable to mass-market consumers and regulators. The effect is not efficiency gain alone. It is a transfer of cultural authority: the credentialed intermediary who previously held the relationship (the advisor, the agent, the specialist, the copywriter) is being repositioned as optional. The platform that owns the AI layer captures the relationship, the proprietary data generated by billions of decisions, and the pricing power that professionals formerly commanded. What makes this structural rather than cyclical is that the AI systems improve with each decision, compounding the moat faster than any human workforce can close the gap.

The mechanism

AI intermediaries perform judgment-level tasks at a cost and speed that makes human employment economically indefensible. Airbnb's AI assistant handled 45% of customer issues to resolution without escalating to a human agent, cutting support cost per booking 16% in a single year. Affirm's AdaptAI and BoostAI systems select financing offers and optimize merchant programs in real time across 26.8 million active consumers — decisions that previously required human underwriters to review individual files. Exact Sciences' Cologuard mail-in stool DNA test routes colorectal cancer screening through the postal system, removing the gastroenterologist who previously held the clinical relationship. Stagwell's Marketing Cloud grew 230% year-over-year by compressing agency workflow that previously required teams of human creatives and media planners.

The mechanism works because the AI system trains on the historical decisions of human experts — how they adjudicated claims, what risk factors they weighted, what trade-offs they accepted — and then reproduces that judgment at machine speed without retaining the humans. This is displacement of cultural authority, not only labor substitution. When an AI customer-service agent resolves your complaint, you never reach a human who can use discretion. When an AI credit system prices your loan, there is no underwriter to call. When a mail-in test replaces a specialist visit, the patient relationship has moved from a professional who could interpret edge cases to a platform that produces a binary result. The expert's accumulated context no longer enters the transaction.

The platforms that own the AI layer compound three advantages simultaneously: operational savings from reduced headcount, pricing power from absorbing what professionals charged separately, and proprietary data from every decision the system makes. Affirm keeps the interest margin and merchant fees while its models train on 6.7 transactions per active consumer per year. Airbnb keeps the booking commission while its models train on 533 million nights and seats booked in fiscal 2025. American Express keeps the payment flow and transaction data across $1.67 trillion in billed business while explicitly positioning to be embedded in agentic commerce flows that consumers may never directly initiate. The AI layer becomes the moat because it compounds with data and widens each time a human intermediary is removed from the pathway.

Why now

Three things converged between 2023 and 2026. First, large language models and domain-specific models crossed a quality threshold: after GPT-4's release in March 2023 and successive capability improvements through 2024, AI systems could handle customer support, underwriting, creative generation, and diagnostic screening at a reliability level acceptable for mass-market deployment. This was not gradual drift — it was a step change visible in deployment decisions by 2024 and showing up in financial results by 2025 and 2026. Second, post-2022 rate increases compressed unit economics across consumer finance, travel, and commerce, creating urgent pressure to cut support, underwriting, and agency costs per transaction. Third, the regulatory environment for AI-assisted decisions in healthcare screening (Cologuard's Medicare coverage) and consumer finance (CFPB guidance on algorithmic underwriting) had settled enough that companies could deploy at scale without waiting for further clarity. The combination of capability, economic pressure, and regulatory surface made 2024-2026 the deployment window — not just the experimentation window.

The tension

AI intermediaries trained on historical human decisions reproduce and amplify existing biases at scale, not case by case. When Affirm's model denies a loan, there is no underwriter to call who can weigh the circumstances not captured in the training data. When Airbnb's AI assistant closes a dispute, there is no human agent who can exercise discretion on a long-tenured host or guest. When Cologuard's result is ambiguous, the gastroenterologist who would have discussed it with the patient is not in the loop. The efficiency gain comes precisely from removing the friction that previously allowed for exceptions, negotiation, and context-dependent judgment. The platform's scale economics require uniformity. Human expertise was partly valuable because it could depart from the rule when the rule was wrong. AI intermediaries cannot do that by design, and the people affected have limited recourse when they cannot identify who made the decision or why.

The surprising part

The non-obvious thing is not that AI is replacing knowledge workers — that claim is everywhere. The non-obvious thing is that the displacement is happening fastest in the sectors where human intermediaries were most valued for trust, not for cost efficiency. Cologuard did not succeed because it was cheaper than a colonoscopy in pure dollar terms — colonoscopies are covered. It succeeded because patients avoid colonoscopies and a mail-in test removes the friction of the specialist visit entirely. Airbnb's AI assistant did not replace agents because agents were expensive relative to AI cost alone — it replaced them because 45% of issues could be fully resolved without the relationship context that agents provided. Affirm's underwriting AI did not win because human underwriters were slow — it won because the AI could run millions of real-time offer optimizations that no human workforce could match regardless of cost. In each case, AI disintermediation did not beat the human on price. It eliminated the need for the human relationship that consumers had assumed was irreplaceable.

Why it matters

When AI absorbs the judgment functions of doctors, financial advisors, customer-service agents, and creative professionals across healthcare, finance, hospitality, and marketing simultaneously, the professions that gave the middle class its pricing power become embedded services inside platform algorithms. The gastroenterologist's expertise is inside Cologuard's decision tree. The underwriter's experience is inside AdaptAI's offer selection. The customer-service agent's judgment is inside Airbnb's assistant. None of them can extract that expertise and sell it independently. The cultural authority they spent careers building is now owned by the platform. What follows is a bifurcation: human expertise becomes either a luxury good — direct access to a specialist for those who can afford premium fees — or an AI-mediated service for everyone else. The question is not whether AI can perform these functions. The evidence says it already can at scale. The question is whether the professionals who trained these systems will ever again be able to set their own terms for what that expertise is worth.

The companies that own the AI layer are not replacing human experts with cheaper labor — they are absorbing the cultural authority of those experts and making it proprietary to an algorithm that improves with every decision, so that the professionals who held that authority can never price it independently again.

The case against this trend

What would falsify it: A major company in these sectors either (a) explicitly investing in human intermediaries rather than replacing them, (b) finding that AI systems at scale reproduce judgment poorly and requiring human oversight that reduces efficiency gains to negligible levels, or (c) facing regulatory blocking of AI intermediaries in favor of human discretion, forcing a reversal of deployment

Companies that don't fit:

  • American Express Company — Despite AI positioning, AmEx's real growth driver remains its Membership Model built on premium consumer relationships, merchant partnerships, and high-income cohorts who explicitly value personal service and concierge benefits — human intermediation remains a core value proposition and premium-tier differentiator, not a displacement target
  • Exact Sciences Corporation — Cologuard is not replacing specialists but creating a new patient pathway alongside existing colonoscopy options; the data shows screening expansion, not specialist displacement — gastroenterologists still manage follow-up and treatment for all positive results, meaning Exact is a front-door diagnostic, not true intermediary replacement
  • Shopify Inc. — Shopify's AI investments (Catalog, Sidekick, Universal Commerce Protocol) are positioned as merchant enablement, not merchant replacement — the company explicitly depends on human entrepreneurship, brand-building, and marketing creativity as inputs to its platform, and has not shown ability to disintermediate the merchant-customer relationship itself

Honest caveat: The trend is strongest where AI can fully automate a decision pathway (credit underwriting, customer-support triage, basic marketing workflows) and weaker where human judgment remains gatekeeping (final medical decisions, brand strategy, high-touch financial advice). The packets show AI handling high-volume, repeatable judgment but do not prove complete replacement of intermediaries in situations requiring discretion, negotiation, or contextual exception-handling. Additionally, the trend conflates efficiency gains with cultural displacement — a company can reduce intermediaries from economics without eliminating them entirely or making them 'optional,' and the evidence shows most companies are layering AI onto existing professional structures rather than fully removing them. The trend is real but may be overstated in scope and speed of total displacement.
