AI Cultural Disintermediation

AI is absorbing the cultural authority of human experts—from customer service to underwriting to creative work—and the platforms that own the AI layer are capturing the economic relationship formerly held by intermediaries.

culturaltechnologicaleconomic
AI capability has crossed the threshold where it can perform judgment-level work at scale and sufficient quality to replace human intermediaries without hiring the professionals. This is not efficiency gain alone; it is a transfer of trust and economic authority from credentialed humans to algorithm-owning platforms. The intermediary—the advisor, the specialist, the agent—is being repositioned as a legacy service or luxury good.

The mechanism

AI intermediaries perform tasks previously requiring human judgment at speed and cost that makes human employment uncompetitive. Airbnb's AI assistant resolves 45% of customer issues without a human agent, cutting support cost per booking by 16%. Affirm's AdaptAI and BoostAI systems optimize credit offers and merchant programs in real time, replacing the need for human underwriters to make individual decisions. Exact Sciences' Cologuard strips the gastroenterologist from the screening pathway by making cancer detection mail-in and autonomous. Stagwell's Marketing Cloud automates workflow and creative optimization that traditionally required agency workers.

The mechanism works because the AI system trains on human behavior—how experts made decisions, what they prioritized, what trade-offs they accepted—and then reproduces that logic at machine speed. But this is cultural displacement, not mere automation. When an AI assistant handles your complaint, you never speak to a human expert. When an AI credit system denies your loan, you have no one to appeal to. When a mail-in test replaces a visit to a specialist, the patient relationship has shifted from professional guidance to platform convenience.

The platforms that own the AI layer capture three forms of value: operational savings from reduced headcount, pricing power by absorbing what experts charged separately, and proprietary data from the billions of decisions their systems make. Affirm keeps the interest margin and merchant fees. Airbnb keeps the booking commission. American Express keeps the payment flow and transaction data. The AI layer becomes the moat because it improves with data and compounds across the merchant base or customer base.

Why now

AI capability crossed the threshold in 2024-2026 where it can perform judgment tasks with sufficient reliability for mass-market deployment. Large language models and domain-specific models can now handle customer support, underwriting decisions, diagnostic screening, creative generation, and merchant optimization at a quality level acceptable to consumers and regulators. At the same time, labor costs, liability concerns, and the competitive pressure to reduce customer-acquisition costs or support cost per unit all created economic urgency to swap humans for AI systems. The confluence of capability and incentive is creating a rapid reordering of who holds expertise and who can charge for it.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Customer service agents, financial advisors, diagnostic specialists, creative professionals at traditional agencies, and any credentialed intermediary whose expertise can be encoded into training data and then replaced by a machine. These are not low-wage workers being undercut by cheaper competitors; these are middle-class knowledge workers—radiologists, underwriters, account managers, copywriters—whose professional authority and pricing power is being absorbed into platform algorithms. Their training data becomes proprietary intellectual property.
Who profits
Platform operators that own the AI layer: Affirm (credit underwriting), Airbnb (customer service and discovery), Exact Sciences (screening access), Stagwell (creative and workflow automation), Shopify (merchant discovery and commerce), American Express (payment routing and transaction data). These companies monetize the efficiency gain while also capturing the relationship and data that experts previously held.

The tension

AI intermediaries trained on existing human decisions reproduce and amplify existing biases, make errors at scale rather than individually, and strip accountability from outcomes. When an AI denies a loan, misses a cancer diagnosis, or produces a biased creative recommendation, the question of who is liable, who takes responsibility, and who the affected person can appeal to has no clear answer. At the same time, removing the human intermediary removes a friction point that previously allowed for exceptions, negotiation, and judgment calls based on context. The platform's scale efficiency comes at the cost of human agency and recourse.

The surprising part

The most non-obvious aspect is that AI disintermediation is not limited to white-collar work that was already struggling. It is happening simultaneously across healthcare (Exact Sciences), financial services (Affirm, AmEx), hospitality (Airbnb), and creative/marketing (Stagwell). What connects them is that human intermediaries were not primarily valued for their labor cost; they were valued for their perceived expertise, judgment, and trust. Once AI systems can credibly perform those functions, the cultural relationship flips. The intermediary becomes legacy infrastructure rather than irreplaceable expertise.

Why it matters

As AI absorbs the functions of doctors, financial advisors, lawyers, and creative professionals across healthcare, finance, and creative work, the professions that gave the middle class access to expertise become either luxury goods—human access for those who can pay premium fees—or AI-mediated services for everyone else. The cultural authority previously held by credentialed humans transfers irreversibly to algorithm-owning platforms. When all screening is mail-in, all credit is algorithmically determined, all customer problems are AI-resolved, and all creative work is machine-generated, human expertise no longer commands its own price. It becomes embedded in platform services. The question is not whether AI can replace these functions; it is whether those functions will ever again be held by humans who can set their own terms.

When platforms absorb the cultural authority of experts and make their judgment proprietary to algorithms, the people who held that authority become optional, while the platforms that own the AI layer capture the economic relationship forever.
