The Algorithm as Buyer: AI Agents Taking Over Commerce Discovery

AI agents are replacing human shopping behavior. Companies that own merchant data, payment routing, and agent interfaces will extract most of the value; everyone else becomes a supplier.

The entire e-commerce funnel—discovery, comparison, negotiation, payment, logistics—is being collapsed by AI agents operating on behalf of buyers. The winners will not be those with the flashiest storefronts or largest traffic volumes, but those sitting inside the agent's decision loop: companies that route traffic to merchants, orchestrate payments, and control the trust signals agents rely on. Shopify Payments, AmEx's closed-loop network, and Etsy's seller-reputation data all suddenly become strategic moats. Companies caught downstream of this shift—merchant who cannot integrate with agent platforms, mid-market brands without data infrastructure, open-network payment processors—will face margin collapse and visibility extinction.

The mechanism

For two decades, e-commerce was built on the assumption that humans make deliberate choices: find product, read description, compare alternatives, add to cart, checkout. Search engines, app stores, and marketplace listings optimized for human eyeballs. That era is ending. By Q2 2026, Airbnb had 45% of support issues started by an AI assistant resolving without human touch. Etsy reported live agent-checkout integrations with Google, Microsoft, and OpenAI. These are not beta pilots—they are production commerce flows.

The shift upends which layer captures value. The old winners—Google Search, Amazon, Instagram—owned the gateway to discovery. Conversion rates and traffic acquisition cost were the game. In agent-mediated commerce, the payoff moves to whoever sits between the agent and the merchant: payment routing, merchant ranking, trust calibration, and preferred-vendor logic. Shopify's $378.4B GMV and 68% Shopify Payments penetration means merchants already depend on Shopify to see transaction data and buyer behavior. If an AI from Google or Microsoft becomes discovery, merchants now depend on being ranked favorably by that agent—a power inversion. Shopify invested in Catalog and Sidekick not to attract humans to storefronts, but to make merchants legible and preferred to agents. That is the actual business.

Payment networks face the same reordering. American Express, with $1.67T in billed business and a closed-loop network connecting cardholders to merchants, has structural advantage over open-network processors. If an agent decides how to pay, it will choose the payment path with zero friction, fastest settlement, and richest data. AmEx's Q1 2026 narrative explicitly noted management discussions of agents discovering, deciding, and completing payments—and AmEx trying to be embedded in that flow. That is not speculative. It is an operational priority.

Etsy's position illustrates the data-leverage dynamic. The platform has 5.6M sellers and 86.5M buyers. Its value to agents is not the user interface; it is the seller-trust data. Agents need to know which sellers are reliable, which goods are authentic, which storefronts have low fraud. Etsy built that data over 20 years. An agent trained on Etsy's reputation signal will route preferentially to Etsy merchants—but only if Etsy can expose that data cleanly to agents. That leverage is what drives Etsy's partnerships with Google, Microsoft, and ChatGPT for agentic checkout.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Merchants feel the pressure first. If you sell on Etsy and the Google or ChatGPT agent does not surface your products, you disappear—you have zero agency in that decision. Shopify merchants will need to invest in Catalog data quality, structured product feeds, and seller-ranking optimization to get picked by agents. Smaller brands and mid-market sellers without technical sophistication or without integration with Shopify, Etsy, or agent-friendly payment processors will face sudden visibility collapse. Traditional e-commerce players without agent-integration infrastructure are left in a corner. Open-network payment processors (that do not offer closed-loop data or frictionless agent integration) will watch transaction routing flow to AmEx and Stripe, which do. The cost of merchant invisibility becomes existential.
Who profits
Shopify captures merchant data-quality dependence through Catalog and Sidekick. AmEx captures payment routing through closed-loop network advantage and agent-native payment flows. Etsy captures seller-matching through reputation data and platform-AI partnerships. Microsoft, Google, and OpenAI profit as agent infrastructure, but the real economic rents—sustainable competitive advantage and pricing power—accrue to whoever sits between agent and merchant. That is why Shopify's merchant-solutions revenue grew to $2.78B in Q2 2026 and why AmEx's new card acquisitions tilted so heavily toward fee-paying products. These companies are not selling to consumers anymore. They are selling merchant visibility and payment rails to agent platforms.

The tension

The societal tension is brutal. Frictionless agent-driven commerce is great for consumers (faster, smarter matches). It is catastrophic for most merchants. If only merchants integrating with Shopify's Catalog or Stripe's agent-friendly APIs get discovered, then only merchants with resources and technical teams survive. The 5.6M Etsy sellers and millions of small Amazon and Shopify sellers face simultaneous margin compression and visibility erasure. Payment networks fragment into winner-take-most: agents will standardize on AmEx or Stripe or a single fintech processor, not multi-rail. Regulatory scrutiny will follow because agent-driven routing creates new concentration points and new forms of algorithmic bias. If Microsoft's agent systematically ranks Microsoft-affiliated merchants higher, that is commerce manipulation in a new form.

The surprising part

The underestimated insight is that merchant data quality and infrastructure become more valuable than raw traffic. Shopify's advantage is not that the company owns storefronts anymore—it is that the company owns the structured product data, transaction history, and buyer behavior that agents need to make routing decisions. Etsy's strength is not design or cultural cool. It is the seller-trust data and the fact that agents need authentic goods to compete with commodity listings. AmEx does not benefit from more card-member spend. It benefits from being the payment layer embedded in the agent's decision logic at checkout. The company with the richest merchant-side or customer-side structured data, not the company with the flashiest interface or largest traffic volume, will own the agent layer. That is a quiet but complete inversion of the last 20 years of digital commerce.

Why it matters

This is about whether small-seller economics survive. If agents can only route to merchants integrated with a few major platforms, small producers, artisans, and niche sellers disappear from discovery—margin compression forces them out. Five million Etsy sellers and millions of Shopify-only sellers face extinction. At the same time, if agents route correctly, consumers get instant access to exactly what they need without browsing waste. The tension between accessibility (keeping small sellers viable) and efficiency (agents finding the best match instantly) determines whether agentic commerce concentrates wealth or keeps it distributed. Early evidence suggests that only merchants and platforms with the resources to build agent-compatible infrastructure will survive. That narrows the funnel.

Merchants are becoming suppliers to platforms that route algorithmic buyers to them—and only platforms with deep merchant data infrastructure will keep their suppliers visible.
