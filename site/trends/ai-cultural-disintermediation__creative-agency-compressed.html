The Machine Eats the Agency: AI Workflow Compression in Marketing

Stagwell's Marketing Cloud achieved 230% net-revenue growth while Omnicom's IPG merger justified itself on AI scale, not creative talent—showing that the agency model is compressing from labor-heavy to software-mediated, and the next intermediary is the platform, not the holding company.

The agency model is inverting. Traditional holding companies built scale through employed talent pools—planners, creatives, media buyers, analysts—staffed per account and coordinated through hierarchy. That model required redundancy and overhead. The new model compresses that workflow into software platforms and AI services: a single operator using programmatic media systems, generative tools, and optimization engines does the work that once took teams. Stagwell and other AI-native operators are winning share not by hiring more creative talent but by shipping workflow software and accepting lower labor per dollar of work. The acceleration is structural, not cyclical.

The mechanism

The agency's historical model rested on labor arbitrage. A large holding company employed armies of planners, creatives, media buyers, and analysts who staffed client accounts and coordinated across disciplines. Scale justified hierarchy—layers of account management, quality assurance, and creative oversight that ensured consistency and brand protection. This required redundant headcount and organizational complexity. Media fragmentation broke that model. When brands faced three networks and print, centralized planning worked. When they faced dozens of channels, streaming services, retail direct, and AI-enabled targeting, the centralized layer became a bottleneck. At the same time, software replaced humans. Programmatic platforms automated media buying, generative AI tools produced copy variations faster than human creatives, and measurement systems consumed data in real time instead of waiting for post-campaign analysis. Into that gap stepped Stagwell: explicitly AI-first, software-forward, and aggressively labor-efficient. The company claimed that 'software, services, and engineers were being embraced in the AI era,' not creative talent or brand relationships.

The compression is measurable in three layers. First, workflow automation: The Trade Desk's $13.4B in gross spend flowed through AI agents (Koa Agents) that handled media planning and optimization without human planning teams. Stagwell's Marketing Cloud (230% growth in 2025) embedded similar automation—the software orchestrated campaigns that once required multiple human roles. Second, creative production: generative AI systems produced first-draft copy, design layouts, and ad variations faster than any agency team could. The agency's role shifted from creation to curation—picking the best AI outputs rather than creating from blank canvas. Third, activation: programmatic buying systems routed spend to highest-performing channels automatically, eliminating the need for media-buying teams to manually negotiate placements. What once took 20 people and six weeks now takes software and three days. Stagwell's 10% organic growth in Q2 2026 versus Omnicom's 5% reflects this: speed matters more than scale when the underlying work is automated. Nexxen's 94% programmatic revenue concentration shows the math—distribution is increasingly software-driven, not human-negotiated.

The strategic response from incumbents reveals how complete the inversion is. Omnicom justified its $13B IPG merger not on creative talent synergies but on AI and data scale. That admission—that Omnicom's own future depends on platform capabilities, not on hiring better creatives—proves that the battlefield has shifted. Omnicom is no longer defending creative excellence; it is defending access to data infrastructure and technical talent. It is also losing: at Q2 2026, Omnicom posted 5% organic growth while Stagwell posted 10%, despite Omnicom being 15x larger by revenue. The speed gap is the thesis: Stagwell moves faster because it has no legacy hierarchy to negotiate, no bloated account teams to justify, and no need to balance the needs of hundreds of agency brands within a holding company. It can automate ruthlessly and pass the efficiency directly to clients.

Why now

Generative AI crossed an inflection point in 2024-2025. Large language models became capable enough to draft strategy memos, generate ad copy variations, and conduct audience analysis at scale. AI agents could execute media plans and optimize in real time. This was not possible five years ago in any deployable form. Simultaneously, client desperation for speed accelerated. Media windows close in hours, not weeks. Audiences shift platforms overnight. Attribution demands constant optimization, not quarterly reviews. The heavyweight hierarchy of traditional agencies became a liability. When Stagwell labeled 2025 an 'inflection year' and reported record net new business ($171M in Q2 2026 alone), the market was saying that the threshold had been crossed. The speed advantage of AI-native operators had become undeniable and irreversible. Omnicom responded by consolidating scale and justifying it on AI and data—a defensive move that signals the incumbent recognizes the game has changed and it is playing catch-up. The inflection is not coming; it is here.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Junior marketing staff face compression. Strategists, junior creative directors, media planners, and analytics roles are the first to absorb automation. The work their roles performed gets either eliminated or consolidated into software-mediated processes. Media budgets that once supported large account teams flow directly to platforms and AI-service providers. Agencies face margin pressure: they can either automate aggressively and compete on efficiency (the Stagwell path) or consolidate and hope scale insulates them (the Omnicom path). Both require headcount reduction. At the consumer level, the compression accelerates algorithmic messaging. Marketing becomes more template-driven, optimized for whatever captures attention fastest, rather than grounded in cultural insight or local narrative. Templates replace stories.
Who profits
Stagwell and AI-native agency operators capture client share by offering speed and cost efficiency. The deeper beneficiaries are the infrastructure platforms: The Trade Desk, Nexxen, and identity/data providers that become the actual intermediaries. Brands route budgets through them, agencies execute through them, and the platforms capture data, attention signals, and demand intelligence that no single agency sees. Stagwell profits too, but it is increasingly a services layer on top of platform infrastructure. Omnicom profits through defensive positioning—for Fortune 500 brands that want a consolidated, integrated partner and will pay for that integration. The true shift is from agencies-as-intermediaries to platforms-as-intermediaries: The Trade Desk's ecosystem, Stagwell's software stack, and data infrastructure become the new agency.

The tension

The core tension is whether compressing agencies into software eliminates brand judgment or simply removes overhead from a commoditized layer. If brand-building still demands strategic insight, creative intuition, and cultural fluency—capabilities that current AI struggles to deliver consistently—then aggressive compression trades short-term margin for long-term commoditization. Brands will get cheaper, faster campaigns that also feel generic and algorithmic. The second tension is scale: can the AI-native model handle Fortune 500 complexity? Large campaigns still require navigating internal stakeholders, brand politics, and risk management—work that current AI cannot do. If true, the AI-native model is limited to mid-market and below, and the largest clients remain loyal to integrated incumbents. The third, externalized tension: if agencies compress, millions of marketing and creative jobs disappear. The entry-level roles that trained generations of marketing professionals vanish. The cultural apprenticeship system collapses.

The surprising part

The most surprising move is Omnicom's own strategic admission. By justifying the IPG merger on AI and data scale, Omnicom is essentially surrendering its historical model. The merger is not really about creative talent or client relationships; it is Omnicom betting that size plus technical infrastructure can compete with Stagwell. That is a defensive play, not a growth play. Omnicom is hedging its bets because it recognizes that Stagwell's model—leaner, AI-first, less burdened by legacy structure—is winning. The market is validating this: Stagwell at 10% organic growth versus Omnicom at 5% is not a narrowing gap; it is an accelerating one. Omnicom is consolidating precisely because it cannot match Stagwell's speed without destroying its existing business model.

Why it matters

If agencies compress into software, marketing talent and creative judgment become concentrated in a handful of platforms and operators. Cultural meaning-making—the process by which brands tell stories and construct identity—shifts from diverse, locally-rooted creative shops to standardized, algorithmic systems optimized for engagement metrics. The diversity of creative perspective shrinks to whatever the algorithms reward. Meanwhile, millions of junior marketing and creative professionals lose the entry-level, training-ground roles that historically let them develop skills and contribute to culture. The apprenticeship system that built professional marketers for decades disappears. What remains is a winner-take-most dynamic where the largest platforms and operators control the tools and infrastructure, and smaller creative voices have no path to scale.

Stagwell's 230% marketing-cloud growth and Omnicom's AI-justified merger show that creative agencies are collapsing into software platforms—the next intermediary is the AI-driven workflow system, not the holding company.
