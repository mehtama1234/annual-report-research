The Machine Eats the Agency: AI Workflow Compression in Marketing

Stagwell's AI-driven Marketing Cloud grew 230% YoY while traditional agencies consolidate—a sign that software, not scale, may be the new intermediary.

Stagwell and other AI-native marketing operators are compressing the workflow value that historically required large creative teams, planners, and strategists into software platforms and AI-driven services. This is not about replacing creative talent outright—it is about re-intermediating the agency relationship itself: brands are not hiring fewer agencies, they are hiring AI-native ones that eliminate the labor overhead and hierarchical complexity of traditional holding-company structures. The question is whether this represents agency evolution or agency extinction.

The mechanism

The traditional agency model rested on a labor arbitrage: large holding companies employed armies of planners, creatives, media buyers, and strategists who would staff client accounts and coordinate execution across media, creative, and measurement. This required scale, hierarchy, and overhead. But that model grew vulnerable to two simultaneous pressures. First, media fragmentation exploded—instead of three networks and print, brands now navigate dozens of streaming services, social feeds, retail channels, and direct-to-consumer surfaces. Second, the tools to orchestrate that fragmentation became software. Programmatic buying platforms, AI optimization engines, data-management systems, and identity infrastructure started doing the work that teams of humans previously performed. Into that gap stepped companies like Stagwell, which explicitly positioned itself as 'a digital-first, AI-forward operator' that could win share from legacy holding companies not by hiring more people but by shipping more software.

Stagwell's The Marketing Cloud delivered 230% YoY net-revenue growth in 2025, and the company reported record net new business wins ($171M in Q2 2026) while simultaneously claiming that 'software, services, and engineers were being embraced in the AI era.' This is not a boutique claim—it is a structural assertion. Omnicom, the incumbent consolidator, justified its $13B+ IPG merger partly on AI and data scale, signaling that even legacy holding companies believe the battlefield has shifted from creative talent hoarding to software platform ownership. Yet Omnicom still posted only 5% organic growth in Q2 2026, while Stagwell posted 10%, demonstrating that speed and AI-native posture matter more than legacy scale when the underlying work has been automated or templated.

The compression plays out across three layers. At the workflow layer, platforms like The Trade Desk (which processed $13.4B in gross spend in 2025) have embedded AI agents—Koa Agents for media planning, buying, and optimization—that allow a single operator to coordinate campaigns that once required a planning team. At the creative layer, generative AI systems can now produce first-draft copy, layouts, and variations faster than any agency team, shifting the agency role from creation to curation and refinement. At the activation layer, programmatic buying and first-party data systems (Stagwell's digital-transformation segment grew 18% in Q2 2026) mean brands can route media spend and measure results without traditional media-buying teams. What once took 20 people and six weeks now takes software and five days. The agency still exists—but its model has been inverted from labor-heavy to software-heavy, and the companies capturing the margin are those that invested in platforms first.

Why now

The inflection point is twofold. First, generative AI has made workflow compression possible: large language models can draft strategy memos, generate ad copy variations, and even conduct basic audience analysis. AI agents can execute media plans and optimize in real time without human intermediation. This did not exist in any scalable form five years ago. Second, client desperation for speed has made efficiency more valuable than prestige. When media windows close in hours instead of weeks, when audiences shift platforms overnight, and when attribution demands constant optimization, the heavyweight hierarchy of traditional agencies becomes a liability. Brands want to move faster than the agency model structurally allows, and AI-native operators promise that speed. Stagwell's explicit framing of the 2025 year as an 'inflection year' built around AI applications and 'record net new business' is the market saying the time is now. Omnicom and other incumbents are responding by consolidating scale (the IPG merger) and acquiring technology: they are betting that size, data, and integration matter more than speed. But Stagwell's 10% organic growth versus Omnicom's 5% (in Q2 2026, the same quarter) suggests the market is rewarding the opposite bet. The challenger model—leaner, AI-first, software-mediated—is resonating with brands that no longer want to pay for organizational complexity.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Brand marketing teams pay for this transition through redundancy and uncertainty. Junior strategists, junior creative directors, media planners, and analytics roles are the first to absorb compression. Media budgets that once supported large account teams now flow to platform providers and AI-service layers. Agencies themselves face margin pressure: they can either cut headcount and compete on workflow software (the Stagwell play), or consolidate and hope scale plus data moats insulate them (the Omnicom play). Both paths exact a cost. At the consumer level, the compression accelerates the shift from artisanal, locally-grounded marketing toward template-based, algorithmic messaging optimized for whatever hooks attention fastest.
Who profits
Stagwell and other AI-native agency operators profit most obviously: they capture client share by offering speed and efficiency at lower labor costs. But the deeper profit beneficiaries are the infrastructure platforms: The Trade Desk, Nexxen, and identity/data providers that power the workflow. These platforms become the actual intermediaries. Brands route budget through them, agencies route execution through them, and the platforms capture data, attention behavior, and demand signals that no single agency can access. Omnicom profits too, but differently—not through speed but through holding onto scale and becoming the 'safe choice' for Fortune 500 brands that prefer a consolidated, integrated partner over a nimble startup. The core shift is from agencies as intermediaries to platforms as intermediaries: the software stack (Stagwell's Marketing Cloud, The Trade Desk's infrastructure) becomes the new agency.

The tension

The tension is whether compressing agencies into software eliminates the human judgment that actually builds brands, or simply removes overhead from an already-commoditized layer. If brand-building still requires strategic insight, creative intuition, and cultural fluency—things that current AI cannot consistently produce—then agencies that compress too aggressively will trade short-term margin for long-term commoditization. Brands will get cheaper, faster campaigns that also feel generic and algorithmic. The other tension is scale: Omnicom's IPG merger and Stagwell's aggressive growth both require that the new model can handle Fortune 500 complexity, not just mid-market brands. If large-scale campaigns still require human teams to navigate politics, stakeholders, and brand nuance, then the AI-native model hits a ceiling and retreats to mid-market and SMB. The final tension is externalized: if AI compresses agencies, what happens to the millions of marketing and creative jobs that disappear? The machine eats the agency, but society pays the cost.

The surprising part

The surprising move is that Omnicom—the incumbent that should be fighting tooth-and-nail to preserve its model—is itself embracing the compression narrative. By justifying the $13B IPG merger on AI and data scale, Omnicom is essentially admitting that the old holding-company model does not win anymore. The merger is not really about creative talent or client relationships; it is about buying enough data infrastructure and technical talent to compete with Stagwell. That means Omnicom is also abandoning its own model. The compression is not happening to agencies from outside—it is being driven from inside, by the incumbents themselves recognizing that size without software is a losing position.

Why it matters

If agencies compress into software, marketing talent and strategic judgment become concentrated in a handful of platforms and operators. Cultural meaning-making—the process by which brands tell stories and shape identity—moves from diverse, locally-rooted agencies to standardized, algorithmic systems. Diversity of creative perspective shrinks. At the same time, millions of junior marketing and creative professionals lose the entry-level, training-ground roles that historically let them develop skills and contribute to culture. The machine eats not just the agency's efficiency—it eats the apprenticeship system that built professional marketers for decades.

Stagwell's 230% marketing-cloud growth and Omnicom's AI-justified merger collapse the creative agency into software platforms, suggesting that the next intermediary is not a holding company but an AI-driven workflow system.
