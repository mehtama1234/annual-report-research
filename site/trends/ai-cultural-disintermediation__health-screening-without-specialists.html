Trends · AI Cultural Disintermediation

Health Screening Without Specialists

AI is absorbing the cultural authority of human experts—from customer service to underwriting to creative work—and the platforms that own the AI layer are capturing the economic relationship formerly held by intermediaries. In healthcare, this means diagnostic screening is shifting from credentialed specialists to autonomous mail-in testing systems.

AI capability has crossed the threshold where it can perform judgment-level work at scale and sufficient quality to replace human intermediaries without hiring the professionals. This is not efficiency gain alone; it is a transfer of trust and economic authority from credentialed humans to algorithm-owning platforms. The intermediary—the doctor, the specialist, the advisor—is being repositioned as a legacy service or luxury good.

The mechanism

AI systems trained on expert decisions reproduce that logic at machine speed, eliminating the need for human intermediaries. Exact Sciences' Cologuard removes the gastroenterologist from the screening pathway by making cancer detection mail-in and autonomous. The patient relationship shifts from professional guidance to platform convenience. Platforms that own the AI layer capture three forms of value: operational savings from reduced headcount, pricing power by absorbing what experts charged separately, and proprietary data from the billions of decisions their systems make. Exact Sciences keeps the screening revenue; the patient never speaks to a specialist.

Why now

AI capability crossed the threshold in 2024-2026 where it can perform judgment tasks with sufficient reliability for mass-market deployment. LLMs and domain-specific models can now handle diagnostic screening, customer support, and underwriting at a quality level acceptable to consumers and regulators. At the same time, labor costs, liability concerns, and competitive pressure to reduce cost per unit all created economic urgency to swap humans for AI systems. The confluence of capability and incentive is creating a rapid reordering of who holds expertise and who can charge for it.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Customer service agents, financial advisors, diagnostic specialists, credentialed healthcare providers whose expertise can be encoded into training data and then replaced by a machine. These are middle-class knowledge workers—radiologists, underwriters, specialists, doctors—whose professional authority and pricing power is being absorbed into platform algorithms. Patients who lose the option of human guidance and recourse. Healthcare systems that no longer employ screening specialists because mail-in testing is cheaper.
Who profits
Platform operators that own the AI layer: Exact Sciences (screening access through mail-in testing), Affirm (credit underwriting via AdaptAI and BoostAI), Airbnb (customer service and discovery), Stagwell (creative and workflow automation), Shopify (merchant discovery and commerce), American Express (payment routing and transaction data). These companies monetize the efficiency gain while also capturing the relationship and data that experts previously held.

The tension

AI intermediaries trained on existing human decisions reproduce and amplify existing biases, make errors at scale rather than individually, and strip accountability from outcomes. When an AI misses a cancer diagnosis, the question of who is liable, who takes responsibility, and who the affected person can appeal to has no clear answer. Removing the human intermediary removes a friction point that previously allowed for exceptions, negotiation, and judgment calls based on context. The platform's scale efficiency comes at the cost of human agency and recourse.

The surprising part

AI disintermediation is not limited to customer service work that was already struggling. It is happening simultaneously across healthcare (Exact Sciences), financial services (Affirm), and hospitality (Airbnb). What connects them is that human intermediaries were not primarily valued for their labor cost; they were valued for their perceived expertise, judgment, and trust. Once AI systems can credibly perform those functions, the cultural relationship flips. The intermediary becomes legacy infrastructure rather than irreplaceable expertise.

Why it matters

As AI absorbs the functions of doctors, financial advisors, and creative professionals across healthcare, finance, and creative work, the professions that gave the middle class access to expertise become either luxury goods—human access for those who can pay premium fees—or AI-mediated services for everyone else. The cultural authority previously held by credentialed humans transfers irreversibly to algorithm-owning platforms. When all screening is mail-in, all credit is algorithmically determined, and all customer problems are AI-resolved, human expertise no longer commands its own price. It becomes embedded in platform services.

When platforms absorb the cultural authority of experts and make their judgment proprietary to algorithms, the people who held that authority become optional, while the platforms that own the AI layer capture the economic relationship forever.
