Health Screening Without Specialists

Exact Sciences made $3.25B in FY2025 by removing the gastroenterologist from colorectal cancer screening. Affirm reduced customer-service headcount while driving credit decisions through AdaptAI and BoostAI. Airbnb resolved 45% of support tickets without human agents in Q2 2026. These are not cost reductions masquerading as AI improvement—they are transfers of cultural authority from credentialed professionals to algorithm-owning platforms that now capture the relationship, the data, and the pricing power the professionals previously held.

Platforms that embed AI into professional workflows are replacing the professionals themselves without hiring equivalents, converting expert judgment into proprietary model training data, and capturing the economic relationship that credentialed intermediaries previously owned. This is not efficiency; it is disintermediation.

The mechanism

Exact Sciences, Affirm, Airbnb, and Stagwell show the same underlying displacement. AI systems trained on expert decisions execute those decisions at machine speed without hiring the expert. Exact Sciences' Cologuard mail-in test replaces the specialist's judgment with a algorithm—the patient relationship shifts from 'I consulted an expert' to 'I ordered a test online.' Affirm's AdaptAI and BoostAI replace credit officers with machine learning that scores creditworthiness at the transaction moment. Airbnb's support AI resolves 45% of issues without human escalation. Stagwell's Marketing Cloud replaces traditional agency creative and strategic staff with 'agentic applications.' In each case, the relationship that formerly required hiring credentialed labor is now captured by the platform, the data from millions of decisions feeds the algorithm's training, and the pricing power shifts from the professional to the platform.

The transfer operates through a cultural reframing. When doctors were scarce, patients accepted 'you need a specialist.' Now platforms frame autonomy as progress: 'Skip the wait, screen at home,' 'Get instant credit decisions,' 'AI solves your support issue faster.' The professional's authority is not lost—it is absorbed into training data. Thousands of specialist decisions train the model. The specialist becomes redundant. The platform captures the recurring relationship, the data exhaust, and the pricing power. Exact Sciences keeps the screening revenue; Affirm keeps the underwriting spread; Airbnb keeps the customer support margin. The specialist never captures the relationship again.

Why now

AI capability crossed the threshold for judgment-level work between 2023 and 2025. Affirm's Q3 2026 disclosure that 'over 65% of approved loan applications were presented with 0% APR offers' shows AI determining credit terms at scale with sufficient accuracy for consumer deployment. Exact Sciences' FY2025 guidance frame shifted to 'screening more people than ever' without proportional headcount growth, indicating autonomous testing replaced screening staff. Stagwell's Marketing Cloud achieved 230% YoY revenue growth in 2025-2026 by positioning 'agentic applications' as core—traditional agencies with hundreds of creative staff are being displaced by software that coordinates workflow without them. The timing is 2023-2025 because three conditions aligned: (1) LLMs and domain-specific models crossed accuracy thresholds acceptable to consumers and regulators; (2) labor costs remained elevated post-pandemic without falling back; (3) company balance sheets had capital to deploy automation after inflation and rates shocks. When capability, economic incentive, and capital availability align, deployment accelerates.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Diagnostic specialists, financial underwriters, customer-service agents, creative professionals, and the middle-class knowledge workers who held these roles. Patients who lose the option of human consultation and recourse. Healthcare systems that no longer employ screening specialists because mail-in testing is cheaper and captures the relationship through the platform instead.
Who profits
Platform operators that own the AI layer. Exact Sciences captures screening access and patient data; Affirm captures credit decisions and underwriting spreads; Airbnb captures customer relationships without labor cost; Stagwell captures creative workflow automation; American Express positions itself inside agentic transaction flows; Shopify builds merchant discovery through AI. These companies monetize efficiency gains while also capturing data exhaust and the recurring customer relationship that professionals previously held.

The tension

AI intermediaries trained on human decisions reproduce and amplify existing biases, make errors at scale rather than individually, and strip accountability when outcomes fail. When an AI misses a cancer diagnosis or denies credit to a qualified borrower, the question of who is liable has no clear answer. Removing the human intermediary removes friction that previously allowed exceptions, negotiation, and judgment based on context. The platform's scale efficiency comes at the cost of agency and recourse.

The surprising part

AI disintermediation is not limited to customer service or routine judgment. It is happening simultaneously across healthcare (screening decisions worth $3.25B annually), financial services (credit offers worth $36.7B GMV), and creative work (agency replacement at 230% growth). What connects them is that human intermediaries were not primarily valued for labor cost—they were valued for cultural authority and trust. Once AI systems credibly perform those functions, the intermediary flips from irreplaceable expert to legacy infrastructure. The third surprise is that platforms are not framing this as replacement—they are rebranding it as progress, convenience, or 'agentic transformation,' which means markets and regulators lack language to even name what is happening.

Why it matters

As AI absorbs screening, credit, diagnosis, and creative judgment across healthcare, finance, and professional services, the professions that gave the middle class stable expertise-based careers become either luxury goods or embedded in platform services. The cultural transfer of authority from credentialed humans to algorithm-owning platforms is mostly irreversible—once a category is profitably AI-intermediated, no company will voluntarily rehire the specialist. When all screening is mail-in, all credit is algorithmic, and all customer problems are AI-resolved, expertise no longer commands its own price.

When platforms absorb the cultural authority of experts through AI training on professional decisions, the professionals become optional, the relationship becomes proprietary to the platform, and the pricing power transfers irreversibly.
