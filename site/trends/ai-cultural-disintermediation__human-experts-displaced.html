The Knowledge Intermediary Under Pressure: Experts in a World of Cheap AI Answers

Research firms and professional advisors face revenue collapse as clients expect AI-enabled delivery at legacy prices.

The credential economy is inverting. When AI floods markets with cheap answers, the human cost of displacement falls not on end-users but on the professional intermediaries who curated expert judgment—loan officers, research analysts, customer service agents, and consultants whose cultural authority is being converted into training data and workflow automation.

The mechanism

The research and advisory business operates on a fundamental assumption: information costs money because reliable interpretation requires scarce human expertise. Forrester Research built a $432M business on this premise—clients paid memberships and consulting fees to access curated analyst judgment, research reports, and advisory access to navigate technology change. The model assumed that even as information became cheaper, the scarcity premium on trusted interpretation would hold or grow. In 2025, that assumption broke. Forrester's revenue collapsed to $397M, contract value fell 6%, and the firm recorded $110.7M in goodwill impairment charges. Management blamed budget pressure and demand contraction, but the underlying erosion is cultural. AI now generates research summaries, market analysis, and strategic recommendations at near-zero cost. Clients still need guidance on technology decisions—the uncertainty is real—but they increasingly expect that guidance to be delivered through AI systems at commodity pricing rather than through human expertise at a trust premium.

This pattern is not unique to Forrester. Across the professional services and expert-intermediary economy, the AI disruption is inverted: it does not threaten end-users primarily, it threatens the intermediaries themselves. Loan officers are being displaced by Upstart's algorithms, which now assess creditworthiness at scale with higher accuracy and lower friction than human judgment ever could. Customer service agents are being replaced by Airbnb's AI systems, which handle inquiries faster and cheaper than human representatives. Creative professionals see their workflows being compressed by Stagwell's Machine, which automates media buying, targeting, and measurement. In each case, the human cost is borne not by the consumer—who gets faster, cheaper service—but by the professional whose credential and authority are being converted into training data. Korn Ferry charges clients for executive search expertise and interim talent sourcing; Gartner charges for curated interpretation and conference access; Omnicom charges for media-planning integration and data orchestration. All are discovering that scale AI systems can perform these functions faster, cheaper, and at increasing fidelity. The professional credential, once a social signifier of access and trust, is becoming a liability—a signal that a client is using legacy expertise instead of modern automation.

The economic pressure shows in different ways depending on the business model. Forrester is a direct subscription-and-membership play, so the contract-value decline is immediate. Gartner is larger and more diversified ($6.5B in revenue across insights, conferences, and consulting), with stronger sticky client retention at 85%, but management explicitly flagged that increased AI services adoption increases competition. Korn Ferry monetizes talent access and organizational design; the firm still grew 7% in its most recent quarter, but that growth is concentrated in interim staffing—the operationally valuable but lower-margin alternative to permanent executive search. Omnicom, the agency giant, is expanding into AI-driven marketing transformation and agentic commerce layers, but the underlying pressure is the same: AI can orchestrate fragmented media channels, optimize targeting, and measure performance faster than traditional creative and media planning. In all four companies, the pattern is identical: the core business of translating complexity into actionable judgment is being automated, and the human cost is falling on the intermediaries, not on the buyers of their services.

Why now

AI has reached fidelity and speed thresholds where it can perform the core functions of expert intermediaries—research synthesis, pattern recognition, decision support—faster and cheaper than human teams ever could. At the same time, enterprises are under budget pressure to justify consulting and advisory spending. The combination creates an inversion: institutions still need guidance on uncertainty, but they now expect that guidance to come from AI systems embedded in workflows at commodity cost rather than from human expertise at a trust premium.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Professionals whose cultural authority is being encoded into algorithms. Research analysts and senior consultants at Forrester, Gartner, and Omnicom face compressed compensation or layoffs. Loan officers and customer service representatives are displaced entirely. Creatives see their role downgraded to prompt-writing and model-training. The social cost is especially acute because the credential economy promised that specialized knowledge would always command a wage premium. AI is proving that promise wrong—the knowledge can be replicated, and the premium is collapsing.
Who profits
AI systems vendors (OpenAI, Anthropic, Palantir) who capture the market value of automated expert judgment. Clients who get faster, cheaper professional services. Companies that consolidate expert functions into AI-enabled workflows without maintaining expensive human overhead—Upstart profits from cheaper loan origination, Airbnb from cheaper customer service, Stagwell from cheaper media operations, Gartner and Omnicom to the extent they successfully transition to AI-powered advisory products.

The tension

The externalized cost is the collapse of the professional middle class. The research analyst, the management consultant, the loan officer, the media planner—these were jobs that provided six-figure salaries and social status to people who mastered complex domains. AI does not eliminate the need for judgment or interpretation; it eliminates the scarcity premium on human delivery. Institutions will still buy advisory services, but those services will be AI-delivered and priced at a fraction of historical rates. The retraining narrative claims professionals can migrate to 'higher-value' roles, but the data suggests a persistent wage compression across the knowledge economy. The deeper tension is cultural: if professional expertise can be replicated by algorithms, what justifies the credential as a social signifier? The answer, it seems, is that it no longer does.

The surprising part

The most non-obvious twist is that Gartner and Omnicom are simultaneously benefiting from and threatened by this shift. Gartner maintains client retention (85%) and is expanding into AI-powered advisory, so institutional spending on interpretation is not disappearing—it is migrating to AI-augmented delivery. Omnicom is explicitly building agentic marketing capabilities and AI-driven discovery services. Both companies are trying to be the AI layer, not the human expertise being displaced. But this only works if they can own the software and data moat around their AI systems. If clients can get equivalent advice from open-source models or general-purpose AI assistants, the traditional advisory business becomes a commodity. Forrester's goodwill impairment suggests management is pricing in that scenario—the firm's prior valuation assumed its analyst expertise would always command a premium, but that assumption is no longer defensible.

Why it matters

This pattern defines who gets lifted by AI and who gets left behind. Consumers benefit from cheaper, faster services. Owners of AI systems benefit from massive scale and reduced labor costs. But the middle class of expertise—the professions that required years of training, credential, and cultural status—is being hollowed out. If the credential no longer signals scarcity or command a wage premium, the entire incentive structure for entering knowledge work collapses. How do you persuade smart young people to spend six years training to be a consultant if an AI can do it faster and cheaper? The answer might be that you cannot, which means expertise itself becomes concentrated in the hands of a few large AI companies and the organizations that can afford to maintain hybrid human-AI teams.

AI is not eliminating the market for expert judgment; it is collapsing the wage premium for human delivery, shifting the professional intermediary from scarce expert to interchangeable labor.
