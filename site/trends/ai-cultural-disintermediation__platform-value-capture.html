Who Owns the Algorithm Owns the Relationship: Where the Value Goes

When AI absorbs a professional's judgment, the money does not stay with the merchant, the bank, or the host — it migrates to whoever owns the model that made the decision.

AI disintermediation is not a story about cheaper service; it is a story about where the economic relationship comes to rest. The company that builds and controls the model layer absorbs the margin, the data, and the recurring contact that a human loan officer, agent, or advisor used to own. Upstart runs credit decisions for 100+ banks that have outsourced their entire AI capability; Affirm's moat 'increasingly sits in data-driven approval and offer optimization'; Airbnb's AI resolves 45% of support issues before a human sees them — in every case the platform, not the point of service, keeps the spread.

The mechanism

The clearest case is Upstart, where AI is not a feature but the entire product. It 'connects millions of consumers to more than 100 banks and credit unions,' more than 90% of its loans are fully automated, and its approval logic, pricing, conversion, and fraud screening all run through one model layer. Those 100+ partners have effectively outsourced the single most valuable function a bank performs — deciding who is creditworthy — to Upstart's algorithm. The origination fee that once compensated a loan officer's judgment now flows to the company that owns the judgment. In 2025 Upstart grew originations 86% and revenue 64% while headcount rose only 18%; the economics detach from labor and attach to the model.

Affirm shows the same migration inside merchant checkout. Its own framing is that the moat 'increasingly sits in data-driven approval and offer optimization rather than in commodity loan origination alone.' Systems it calls AdaptAI and BoostAI select which financing offer to show, optimize conversion, and design merchant programs — work formerly split between a store's finance staff and a bank's underwriters. With 515,000 active merchants (up 44%), 26.8 million active consumers transacting 6.7 times a year, and its average cost of funds at a 3.5-year low, Affirm inserts itself as the decision layer between buyer and seller and collects the underwriting margin that used to sit at the bank.

Airbnb demonstrates the pattern on the cost side rather than the revenue side, but the destination is identical. Nearly 45% of support issues that begin with its AI assistant are resolved without a human agent, and support cost per booking has fallen about 16% year over year. The customer-service relationship formerly mediated by a call-center worker is now mediated by Airbnb's model — and the dollars that would have paid that worker are retained as platform margin on a business already generating $4.6 billion of free cash flow on $12.2 billion of revenue.

Shopify and Stagwell reveal the structural logic from the platform's own vantage point. Shopify explicitly describes 2025 as 'laying the rails for a new era of AI commerce,' preparing for a world where products are 'discovered, compared, recommended, and potentially purchased through AI systems and agentic workflows rather than only through traditional browser sessions.' Its own note that 'AI is lowering business-creation friction while increasing platform dependence' is the entire thesis in one line: as AI makes the merchant's job easier, it makes the merchant more dependent on the layer that owns the AI. Stagwell, an agency holding company, is racing to become that layer for marketing — its Marketing Cloud grew net revenue 230% year over year — precisely because it knows the alternative is being disintermediated itself.

The unifying economics: a human intermediary's value was bundled — trust, judgment, data, and the recurring contact all lived in one person and could not be separated. AI unbundles them, and the platform re-bundles them at the model layer. Whoever owns the model owns the approval decision, the transaction data that improves the next decision, and the customer relationship that produces the next transaction. The merchant, provider, and consumer keep the activity; the platform keeps the compounding asset.

Why now

The 2025-2026 filings are the first where these companies report the disintermediation as realized margin rather than aspiration. Upstart returned to GAAP profitability in Q2 2026 without adding equity capital and posted a record $193 million contribution profit at a 62% margin — the model layer is now demonstrably self-funding. Affirm reached net income of $100 million and adjusted operating income of 27% of revenue while its cost of funds hit a 3.5-year low. Airbnb quantified the human-agent displacement for the first time at 45% of support tickets and a 16% cost-per-booking decline. The economic structure stopped being a pitch and started showing up in the cash flows.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
The professionals whose judgment the model absorbed: the bank loan officers whose underwriting decision is now automated across 100+ institutions, the call-center agents whose 45% of tickets never reach them, and the merchants who gain convenience but 'increasing platform dependence' — trading ownership of their own customer relationship for access to the rails. The consumer pays too, in a subtler currency: the trust they once placed in a person is now placed in an opaque model they cannot interrogate.
Who profits
The platform that owns the model. Upstart captures origination fees across a network it does not have to staff; Affirm captures underwriting margin at 515,000 merchants; Airbnb keeps the support-labor savings as free cash flow; Shopify pulls payments penetration to 68% of a $378 billion GMV base as merchants deepen their dependence. In each case the recurring economic relationship — and the data that compounds it — concentrates at the layer that controls the algorithm.

The tension

The value that concentrates at the model layer is extracted from the very network the platform depends on. Upstart's 100+ banks are being trained to trust an outsourced brain; the day they build their own, or a regulator forces the credit decision back in-house, the spread evaporates. Affirm's merchants are one competing checkout button away from switching. The platform's power is real but rented — it lasts only as long as the participants find rebuilding the model themselves harder than paying the toll.

The surprising part

The most valuable thing the platform captures is not the fee — it is the data exhaust that makes the next decision better, which no individual banker or agent could ever accumulate. Upstart's conversion rate climbing from 15.1% to 19.4% is the model teaching itself across every partner's loans at once; a single bank's loan officer only ever sees one bank's applicants. The AI layer wins not because it is smarter per decision but because it sees every decision, and that vantage point is structurally impossible for the human it replaced.

Why it matters

The judgments that shape your life — whether you get the loan, what price you are shown, how your complaint is resolved — are migrating from people you could argue with to models owned by companies whose incentive is to keep the relationship, not to explain it. When the decision layer consolidates at a handful of platforms, the terms of your access to credit, commerce, and service are set by an algorithm you cannot see, owned by a company you do not deal with directly.

AI does not just do the intermediary's job — it moves the intermediary's paycheck, data, and customer relationship up one layer to whoever owns the model, and keeps them there.
