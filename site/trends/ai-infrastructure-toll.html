The AI Infrastructure Toll

Hyperscalers are paying premium rates to a small tier of specialty contractors, quarry operators, and component makers who control inputs that cannot be imported, automated, or stockpiled — and who can grow only as fast as their licensed labor does.

The AI buildout is a massive physical infrastructure program that converts software capital commitments into demand for crushed stone, copper, concrete, licensed electricians, backup generators, and precision circuit boards. The firms that own the scarce physical inputs — quarries with permitted reserves near data-center corridors, specialty contractors with proven execution depth, PCB manufacturers with qualified capacity — have pricing power that hyperscalers cannot route around. The toll is structural because the inputs are immobile, the labor is non-fungible, and every major hyperscaler is competing for the same constrained pool of execution capacity at the same time.

The mechanism

Every AI model requires computing power. Computing power requires data centers. Data centers require land, power, cooling, precision construction, electrical integration, and specialized personnel. That chain of requirements flows capital through an ecosystem that became overwhelmed when every major hyperscaler committed multi-year capital programs simultaneously starting in 2025.

The work cannot be outsourced, postponed, or automated at scale. A 500-megawatt data-center campus needs thousands of tons of aggregates for site preparation, concrete for foundations, rebar for structure, electrical distribution equipment, HVAC systems, fiber-optic cable, backup generators, and teams of licensed electricians, mechanical engineers, and specialty contractors to assemble it all. That work happens in specific locations where the aggregate quarry, the power grid connection, and the skilled labor all exist within hauling and commuting distance.

Hyperscalers signed multi-year capital plans starting in 2025. Their demand became visible and sticky in a single compressed window. Sterling Infrastructure said mission-critical projects made up 92% of E-Infrastructure backlog by Q2 2026. EMCOR's data-center construction contracts became the largest contributor to rising remaining performance obligations. Comfort Systems cited data-center and hyperscaler HVAC work as the driver of a record backlog that grew from $5.99 billion at year-end 2024 to $14.06 billion by Q2 2026. The bottleneck shifted from customer demand to contractor capacity.

The binding constraint moved from demand to execution depth. Comfort Systems management explicitly said execution depth and team availability, not customer demand, was the growth bottleneck even as organic revenue grew 51% in Q1 2026. A concrete crew that can pour a quarter-million-ton foundation does not appear overnight. An electrical contractor with experience fitting out a 50-megawatt power distribution system for a data center operates at a different technical depth than a residential installer. That creates a two-tier market where a small cohort of national firms — Comfort Systems, Sterling, EMCOR — can command outsized margins because they are the only ones who can deliver at the required speed, scale, and precision.

Why now

The inflection was not gradual. It was a single compressed window in 2025 when AI inference demand translated into simultaneous capital commitments from every major hyperscaler. Comfort Systems backlog was $5.99 billion at December 31, 2024. By Q2 2026, it was $14.06 billion. That $8 billion increase happened in 18 months. Sterling Infrastructure's E-Infrastructure segment posted 123% revenue growth in FY2025 — the first full year in which mission-critical AI and semiconductor projects dominated the backlog. TTM Technologies saw data-center and networking revenue grow 37% year over year in Q4 2025, then 91% year over year in Q2 2026 — each quarter faster than the last. Generac signed its first hyperscaler global supply agreement in early 2026, then secured a second on June 24, 2026, taking data-center backlog to $1.6 billion. EMCOR's remaining performance obligations rose $5.23 billion year over year to $17.14 billion, with data-center construction contracts named as the single largest driver. The visible milestone was mid-2026: the moment when infrastructure contractors stopped discussing AI as a pipeline opportunity and started reporting it as the majority of their signed backlog.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Cloud and AI companies absorbing massive simultaneous capex budgets that are bidding against each other for the same constrained pool of specialty contractors, licensed electricians, and quarry capacity. Electricity consumers funding grid expansion through regulated utility rates — Alliant Energy projects a 60% load increase by 2031. Communities hosting data centers that consume large volumes of water and power. Specialty subcontractors and smaller regional construction firms that lack the execution depth or hyperscaler relationships to win mission-critical work, and therefore exit the highest-margin market segment. Workers in licensed trades where demand is expanding faster than training pipelines.
Who profits
A small cohort of national specialty contractors with proven hyperscaler relationships and the labor bench to deliver at scale: Comfort Systems, Sterling Infrastructure, EMCOR. Their backlogs now extend years rather than quarters and carry margins that reflect genuine scarcity rather than cyclical upswing. Quarry operators with permitted aggregates reserves near data-center corridors — Martin Marietta generates 88% of segment gross profit from aggregates that cannot be shipped more than 50 miles, giving local operators pricing power on every site preparation contract. PCB and advanced-interconnect manufacturers with existing qualification relationships: TTM Technologies grew data-center and networking revenue 91% year over year in Q2 2026. Backup-power manufacturers like Generac that secured multi-year hyperscaler supply agreements before competitors could qualify at scale.

The tension

Labor scarcity is the structural ceiling. Comfort Systems — the clearest signal — said execution depth and team availability were the binding constraint even when organic revenue was growing 51% year over year. EMCOR said acquisitions are partly a way to acquire capability depth rather than just revenue scale. Sterling is deliberately reallocating resources away from lower-margin transportation contracts toward mission-critical E-Infrastructure work, not because transportation demand disappeared but because there are not enough qualified crews to chase both. You cannot train a licensed electrical crew in a quarter. Power is the secondary binding constraint. If grid buildout cannot keep pace with AI demand, overbuilt capacity in data-center construction and components becomes stranded, and the premium pricing that specialty contractors and material suppliers are commanding begins to erode. Generac is expanding facilities and acquiring vertically to increase large-megawatt generator capacity, but the system depends on the grid actually having room to accommodate what data centers will draw.

The surprising part

Crushed stone is the hidden chokepoint. Aggregates — the rock, sand, and gravel that go into every foundation, road, and site preparation project — cannot be economically shipped more than 50 miles. Martin Marietta generates 88% of segment gross profit from aggregates that are trapped by this freight limitation. That means every new data-center campus in Virginia, Texas, or Oregon depends on whether there is a permitted quarry with reserve capacity within hauling distance. If there is not, the project either waits for a new quarry permit — a process that takes years — or pays whatever the local operator charges. Martin Marietta and Knife River both named data-center siting as a new demand driver in 2026. A hyperscaler that spends $10 billion on servers and software can be held up by the local quarry operator sitting on the only reserves within hauling range of the preferred site. That quarry operator looks like a commodity supplier on a stock screen but functions as an infrastructure gatekeeper with local pricing monopoly.

Why it matters

The physical cost of AI expansion — land use, power consumption, water depletion, carbon footprint, and community disruption from construction — is being externalized to utility customers and local communities without their participation in the financial upside. The workers and firms delivering the physical infrastructure are seeing real backlog and real demand, yet they face structural labor constraints that mean they are being asked to scale faster than trade apprenticeship programs, immigration policy, or demographic trends allow. Comfort Systems, Sterling, and EMCOR keep naming workforce quality and team depth as their actual growth limit, not customer demand. When physical constraints bind tighter than customer intent, society is choosing to accelerate AI deployment before it has solved the human and material layer underneath. The quarry operator, the electrical contractor, and the licensed HVAC crew are not incidental to the AI story. They are the rate-limiting step.

The physical layer of AI — licensed labor, aggregates, backup power, and precision circuit boards — is being paid by hyperscalers at premium rates because every major cloud company committed capital simultaneously into a pool of contractors, materials, and components that cannot scale as fast as a software budget.

How the Toll Booth Got Built
Origin

The Inflection: When Construction Backlog Became a Structural Bet

Construction shifted from cyclical to structural between 2023–2025 as policy-driven semiconductor and data-center capex created multi-year demand backlogs that outpaced historical patterns. The inflection is measurable: backlog visibility jumped from 9–18 months to 3–5 years, tied to specific enterprise infrastructure projects rather than macro housing or commercial cycles.

Who profits

The Execution Premium: Why Only a Few Contractors Collect the Toll

As hyperscalers pour capital into data centers and grid infrastructure, specialty contractors with skilled labor, backlog visibility, and execution depth command pricing power and margin expansion that pure equipment vendors cannot reach. The winners extract a 10-25% net margin premium and command 1.4+ years of revenue visibility through locked-in backlogs.

The Sub-Dynamics Inside the Toll
Sub-pattern

The Quarry Chokehold: Local Stone, National Power

Crushed stone and gravel cannot move more than 50 miles economically, which means every AI data-center campus is held hostage to whoever owns the permitted quarry closest to the site—and they know it.

Sub-pattern

Dirt, Trenches, and High Voltage: The Site-Prep Tier

Data-center and semiconductor campuses require grading, underground electrical conduit, substation tie-ins, and mission-critical electrical integration—work that four specialty contractors (Sterling, Primoris, Dycom, MYR Group) have deliberately repositioned to dominate. Sterling's E-Infrastructure backlog is 92% mission-critical projects; Primoris and Dycom each record backlog of $13.9B and $11.9B; MYR Group's combined segments hit $3.16B. This tier now captures margin and execution moat where AI demand compresses timelines into 18–24-month site-work windows.

Sub-pattern

The Grid Gap: Backup Power as Permanent Infrastructure

AI data centers demand uninterrupted megawatt-scale power that grid capacity cannot supply. Generac's $1.6B data-center backlog and two hyperscaler global supply agreements prove backup power has shifted from contingency to architectural necessity, creating a durable enterprise-scale market.

Sub-pattern

The Board Under the Server: PCBs and the Physical Compute Stack

Between Q4 2025 and Q2 2026, TTM Technologies' data-center and networking segment accelerated from 19% annual growth to 91% year-over-year, capturing the manufacturing toll from AI infrastructure buildout. Hyperscalers cannot deploy accelerators without boards; TTM's capacity constraints prove the bottleneck is real and extractable.

Costs, Ceilings, and Fragility
Who pays

The Labor Ceiling: When $14 Billion in Backlog Meets the Shortage of Certified Electricians

Comfort Systems, EMCOR, MYR Group, and Primoris all report record backlogs in 2026, yet each names skilled-labor scarcity, not customer demand, as the binding constraint on growth. When a contractor cannot execute work that customers want to pay for because there are not enough licensed electricians and HVAC technicians in the market, the labor shortage itself becomes an infrastructure tax.

Who pays

Communities on the Hook: Ordinary Ratepayers Finance AI Buildout Through Regulated Utilities

When Alliant Energy signed 3.4 GW of data-center contracts, the cost got rolled into the utility's rate base—meaning residential and commercial customers across Iowa and Wisconsin are now subsidizing hyperscaler infrastructure through rate increases they never approved. A $13.4B capital plan for 2026-2029 financed through customer rates, not by the companies generating the demand.

The tension

What Breaks the Toll: Overcapacity, Grid Failure, and Stranded Build

The physical infrastructure toll chain collapses if AI demand softens, hyperscaler capex reverses, or grid buildout stalls—leaving contractors with multi-year backlogs that convert to inventory risk and materials operators with stranded volumes that cannot redeploy.
