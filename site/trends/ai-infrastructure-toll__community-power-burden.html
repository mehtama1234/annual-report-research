The mechanism

The AI infrastructure toll hides the subsidy inside regulated utility pricing structures. When Alliant Energy or any regional utility signs a 1 GW data-center contract, the capacity does not appear on a separate bill. Instead, it gets rolled into the utility's rate base—the total assets used to calculate allowed rates. The utility files a capital plan for regulatory approval, and costs get spread across all customers. Alliant's most recent capital plan for 2026-2029 totals $13.4B specifically to support large-load growth. That money comes from customer rates, not from the companies generating the demand. Residential customers in rural Iowa and Milwaukee suburbs pay higher bills to fund infrastructure serving distant hyperscalers who get priority access to that capacity at negotiated rates typically lower than what a new residential customer would pay for comparable power. The subsidy is structural and invisible.

Digital Realty's filings make the physical intensity and pace of buildout visible. Q2 2026 showed $307M annualized GAAP rent at 100% share plus $410M in hyperscale leases signed in July alone—$717M of new annualized rent in a single month. Each megawatt of capacity Digital Realty commits requires utilities like Alliant to upgrade substations, add generation, build transmission. All of that capitalizes into the rate base, spreading cost across the entire customer base. The pace is accelerating: renewal lease rate increases jumped from 6.1% cash in Q4 2025 to 25.4% in Q2 2026, showing hyperscalers' willingness to pay premium rates for scarce capacity. That premium signals urgency and reinforces the capital-plan push to build more generation and transmission. Communities paying through rate increases are financing infrastructure that makes the hyperscaler buildout possible.

The construction materials supply chain exposes the scale of upstream infrastructure buildout supporting data centers. Knife River's 2025 backlog ended at $1.0B with 89% tied to publicly funded projects, but AI data centers also drive demand through private construction. Commercial Metals deployed more than $2.5B into acquisitions of precast concrete platforms (CP&P and Foley) precisely to supply materials for power infrastructure and substations serving hyperscale buildout. Knife River's Q2 2026 backlog increased to $1.2B from $1.0B a year earlier, with management explicitly naming data centers, semiconductors, and energy buildouts as support. That backlog conversion depends on construction timing, but the financing is locked into capital plans and rate bases across the utility sector. Communities are not just paying for electricity; they are funding the entire physical layer of AI infrastructure through structured utility rate increases that disguise the transfer as normal cost-of-service recovery.

Why now

The inflection arrived in 2025-2026 because data-center density shifted from generic colocation to hyperscale, AI-specific capacity buildup. Before 2023, data centers were a known but bounded part of utility load. By 2025, utilities like Alliant and Duke were signing 1 GW+ commitments that would reshape their systems. Alliant's management stated 2025 was pivotal: agreements signed that year would significantly accelerate future energy demand. Expected load growth of 60% by 2031 is structural. Regulators in Iowa, Wisconsin, and other regions approved these capital plans because the political logic says growth equals good and energy demand signals economic development. But the actual cost—rate-base expansion hitting residential and commercial ratepayers—is now visible. Alliant's $13.4B capital plan is a named inflection point: this is not cyclical demand; this is a structural reallocation of utility assets toward hyperscaler capacity.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
The residential customer in Iowa or Wisconsin pays through higher bills. The nursing home using energy to heat water and cool patients pays. Small business owners pay. When Alliant Energy's $13.4B capital gets added to the rate base, regulators allow a return on that investment, and utilities collect returns through rate increases across all customer classes. A 2-3% annual rate increase that used to reflect inflation now reflects dedicated infrastructure for hyperscalers that individual customers will never use. The subsidy is compulsory and invisible: you cannot opt out of electricity, cannot negotiate rates, and cannot avoid funding infrastructure built specifically for companies optimizing elsewhere.
Who profits
Google, Meta, Amazon, and hyperscalers capture the value by contracting for power at negotiated rates—typically lower than what new residential customers pay, with priority access guaranteed by regulatory approval. Regional utilities profit by investing capital at a guaranteed regulated return, which is more stable than competing in spot markets. Contractors and materials suppliers (Comfort Systems, Sterling Infrastructure, Knife River, Commercial Metals) profit through accelerating backlog. Comfort Systems' Q2 2026 backlog reached $14.06B versus $8.12B a year ago. Sterling Infrastructure's E-Infrastructure revenue grew 123% FY2025. Everyone in the supply chain captures value except residential ratepayers, who bear infrastructure cost while hyperscalers capture monopoly value.

The tension

The tension emerges when rate-base expansion hits affordability friction. If every residential customer's bill rises 3-5% annually to support data-center infrastructure, political pressure will mount. Some states are seeing pushback against utility capital plans driven by hyperscaler demand. The second tension is transmission bottlenecks: utilities can add capacity, but cannot always get transmission corridors approved or built fast enough. If data-center demand outpaces grid buildout, scarcity drives even higher rates for everyone. The third tension is stranded assets: if AI demand eventually plateaus or hyperscalers shift location, utilities will have overinvested in specific corridors and spread losses across the customer base for decades. That risk is borne by ordinary ratepayers, not by hyperscalers, who can move if rates get too high.

The surprising part

The most non-obvious finding is that the AI infrastructure toll is more regressive than infrastructure stories typically are. Tech journalism celebrates the $13.4B Alliant capital plan as growth and opportunity. But that capital comes from a rate base serving both wealthy suburban customers and low-income urban customers equally. The subsidy damages low-income ratepayers most because they cannot move, cannot negotiate, and cannot find cheaper power. Meanwhile, hyperscalers optimize location across jurisdictions, playing states and utilities against each other to secure the best power deals. The asymmetry is total: communities bear all infrastructure cost while hyperscalers bear zero regulatory burden and maximum location flexibility. This is the inverse of traditional infrastructure subsidies, where communities benefited from railroad or highway buildout. Here, communities pay for infrastructure that generates zero local benefit.

Why it matters

This matters to anyone who pays an electricity bill, especially in regions attracting data-center buildout. You are already subsidizing the grid infrastructure powering AI services through your rates. In Iowa and Wisconsin, residential rates will rise faster than the national average to support Alliant's $13.4B capital plan. Unlike market transactions where you choose whether to buy something, you cannot opt out of electricity. The subsidy is compulsory. The value capture flows to distant tech companies. This is a permanent transfer of wealth from utility customers to hyperscalers, mediated through utility rate-base expansion and approved by regulators framing AI buildout as growth. The civic cost is that infrastructure investment decisions affecting community life are being made by corporate capital allocation, not by democratic processes.
