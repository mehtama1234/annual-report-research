Communities on the Hook: Who Really Pays for AI's Power Appetite

Residential and commercial electricity customers in AI-hosting regions are financing hyperscaler data centers through regulated utility rate bases they never approved.

The power infrastructure required by AI data centers is not market-priced and certainly not free. It is financed through regulated utility rate bases, meaning every residential and commercial electricity customer in AI-hosting regions becomes a subsidy engine for hyperscale buildout. Alliant Energy's filings show 3.4GW of contracted data-center demand being built into future rate increases. The financial burden lands on communities and ordinary ratepayers while the value capture flows to Big Tech.

The mechanism

The AI infrastructure toll operates through a mechanism that hides the subsidy inside regulated utility pricing. When Alliant Energy or any regional utility signs a large data-center contract for 370MW or 1GW of power, that capacity does not appear on a separate bill. Instead, it gets rolled into the utility's rate base—the total assets used to calculate allowed rates. The utility files a capital plan, regulators approve it, and the cost gets spread across all residential and commercial customers. Alliant's most recent capital plan for 2026-2029 totals $13.4B specifically to support large-load growth. That money comes from customer rates, not from the companies generating the demand.

Digital Realty's filings make the physical intensity visible. Q2 2026 showed total bookings of $307M annualized GAAP rent at 100% share, plus an additional $410M in hyperscale leases signed in July alone. That $717M in new annualized rent in a single month represents data centers that will require dedicated power infrastructure. Every MW of capacity that Digital Realty commits to requires utilities like Alliant to upgrade substations, add generation, and build transmission. All of that gets capitalized into the rate base, spreading the cost across the utility's customer base. The hyperscalers pay for their leases. The communities pay for the grid.

The construction materials supply chain reveals the scale of the physical buildout. Knife River's 2025 backlog ended at $1.0B, with 89% tied to publicly funded projects. But AI data centers also drive demand for concrete, rebar, and aggregates through private construction channels. Commercial Metals has deployed more than $2.5B into acquisitions and expansion of its precast concrete platform—precisely the materials needed to build the power infrastructure and substations that support hyperscale data centers. Knife River's Q2 2026 backlog increased to $1.2B from $1.0B a year earlier, with management explicitly citing data centers, semiconductors, and energy buildouts as backlog support. That backlog conversion depends on construction timing, but the financing is already locked into capital plans and rate bases across the utility sector.

Why now

The inflection has arrived because data-center density is moving from generic colocation to hyperscale, AI-specific capacity buildup. Before 2023, data centers were a known but bounded part of the utility load mix. By 2025, utilities like Alliant and Duke were signing 1GW+ commitments that would reshape their systems. Regulators in Iowa, Wisconsin, and other regions are approving these capital plans because the political logic says growth is good and energy demand is a proxy for economic development. But the actual cost—the rate-base expansion and the customer bills—is now hitting residential and commercial ratepayers. The political consensus around AI hides the distributional friction.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
The residential customer in Iowa or Wisconsin pays. The small business owner pays. The nursing home that uses energy to heat water and cool patients pays. When Alliant Energy builds $13.4B of capital to support 3.4GW of data-center load, that investment gets added to the rate base. Regulators allow a return on that investment. The utility collects the return through higher rates from all customers. A 2-3% annual rate increase that used to reflect general inflation now reflects dedicated infrastructure for hyperscale companies that the individual customer will never use. The subsidy is invisible but real.
Who profits
Google, Meta, Amazon, and other hyperscalers capture the value. They contract for power at a negotiated rate that is typically lower than what a new residential customer would pay. They get priority access to that capacity. They build out their AI businesses on top of infrastructure that their customers' electricity bills subsidize. Regional utilities like Alliant also profit—they get to invest capital at a guaranteed regulated return, which is more stable than competing in the spot market. The contractors and materials suppliers (Knife River, Commercial Metals) profit through backlog and demand. Everyone in the supply chain captures value except the residential ratepayer.

The tension

The tension emerges when rate-base expansion hits affordability friction. If every residential customer's bill rises 3-5% annually to support data-center infrastructure they do not use, political pressure will mount. Some states are already seeing pushback against utility capital plans driven by hyperscaler demand. The second tension is transmission bottlenecks: utilities can add capacity, but they cannot always get new transmission corridors approved or built fast enough. If data-center demand outpaces grid buildout, scarcity will drive even higher rates for everyone. The third tension is stranded assets: if AI demand eventually plateaus or if hyperscalers shift location, utilities will have overinvested in specific corridors and will spread that loss across their customer base for decades.

The surprising part

The most non-obvious finding is that the AI infrastructure toll is more regressive than people think. Tech journalists celebrate the $13.4B Alliant capital plan as growth and opportunity. But that capital comes from a rate base that serves both wealthy suburban customers and low-income urban customers in equal measure. The subsidy is most damaging to the least flexible ratepayers—those who cannot move, cannot negotiate, and cannot find cheaper power. Meanwhile, the hyperscalers can optimize their location across jurisdictions, playing states and utilities against each other to get the best power deals. The subsidy is unidirectional: communities to tech companies.

Why it matters

This matters to anyone who pays an electricity bill. You are already subsidizing the grid infrastructure that powers the data centers running ChatGPT and other AI services. Your rates are rising to pay for capital that the utility needs to serve hyperscale demand. If you live in a region with active data-center buildout (Iowa, Wisconsin, Texas, Virginia), your rates will rise faster than the national average. And unlike a market transaction where you choose whether to buy something, you cannot opt out of electricity. The subsidy is compulsory. The value capture is captured by distant tech companies.

Ordinary electricity customers are financing AI data center buildout through regulated utility rate increases, while hyperscalers capture the value and can move if rates get too high.
