The Board Under the Server: PCBs and the Physical Compute Stack

The AI buildout creates demand not only for chips and power but for the printed circuit boards, substrates, and RF components that sit inside every accelerator card and networking switch—and capacity constraints give manufacturers like TTM Technologies pricing power.

AI demand reaches the physical economy through a distinct but overlooked layer: the printed circuit boards, advanced substrates, RF components, and interconnect hardware that sit inside every server, accelerator card, and networking switch. TTM Technologies' data-center and networking segment grew 91% year-over-year to represent 40% of Q2 2026 sales, showing that AI infrastructure spending has already translated downstream into manufacturing capacity constraints. This page examines the component manufacturing layer as a toll point that sits upstream of hyperscaler deployment timelines and whose capacity limits can delay the buildout itself.

The mechanism

The AI infrastructure buildout is not only software, chips, and capital deployment. It is a physical manufacturing problem that starts the moment a hyperscaler decides to deploy an accelerator card or build a data center. Every GPU, every networking switch, every server chassis, and every interconnect layer requires a printed circuit board. Those boards are not commodity products. They are specialized substrates with RF components, power-delivery networks, thermal management layers, and signal integrity requirements that vary by end application. TTM Technologies manufactures these boards at scale for data-center networking, aerospace and defense, and medical electronics. The company is not producing commodity components; it is manufacturing the physical substrate layer that defines whether AI infrastructure can actually deploy at planned timelines.

The capacity constraint is real and binding. TTM's Q2 2026 data-center and networking segment grew 91% year-over-year, and management explicitly said the company was expanding into Europe to relieve capacity constraints. This is not theoretical upside. This is a manufacturer announcing that it cannot keep up with demand for the physical layer of AI infrastructure. That matters because hyperscalers cannot deploy servers without boards, boards cannot be ramped instantly (they require specialized equipment, skilled labor, and lengthy qualification cycles), and each month of delay in board production translates into delayed data-center availability. TTM is demonstrating that AI infrastructure spending can be gated not by capital availability or chip availability but by the manufacturing capacity of the physical-layer suppliers.

The pricing dynamic favors manufacturers in this position. When demand for a critical input exceeds available capacity, pricing power shifts to suppliers. TTM's gross margins and adjusted EBITDA margins (16.6% of sales in Q2 2026) remain healthy even as the company is operating at capacity. That is not a company with pricing pressure; that is a company capturing value from scarcity. The broader pattern is that hyperscalers will pay premium pricing to boards manufacturers to secure capacity and maintain deployment schedules. This is not parasitic; it is simply how supply-constrained manufacturing economics work when the downstream demand is inelastic.

Why now

The PCB manufacturing toll point crystallized in 2024-2025 because AI infrastructure spending moved from aspiration into actual deployment. Hyperscalers went from planning data centers to building them, which meant converting abstract capex into actual orders for accelerators, networking switches, and servers. Each one of those systems requires boards. TTM's 91% YoY growth in data-center and networking is direct evidence that the manufacturing demand wave has arrived. Additionally, geopolitical fragmentation has made supply-chain resilience a strategic priority, meaning hyperscalers are willing to pay premiums to ensure board supply is diversified and domestically available. TTM's European expansion is a response to that willingness.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Hyperscalers and their end customers (cloud providers, AI labs, enterprises) ultimately pay through higher equipment costs when board manufacturers have pricing power. The cost of a board sits inside the cost of a server or accelerator card, which sits inside the capex required for an AI data center. When board manufacturers can command premium pricing because of supply constraints, that cost trickles down into higher capex for the buildout, which eventually flows through to cloud pricing and AI service costs. End users of AI services may indirectly pay through higher API costs or higher cloud compute pricing when infrastructure costs spike.
Who profits
TTM Technologies and other specialized PCB and substrate manufacturers capture immediate value through volume growth and pricing power. The company is growing revenue at 37% and data-center revenue at 91% while maintaining healthy margins. Shareholders profit by holding a stock positioned at a critical manufacturing chokepoint. Component-equipment suppliers (the companies that make the machines that make the boards) also benefit because demand for their tools increases. End-to-end infrastructure suppliers like Broadcom and Cisco profit indirectly because their high-margin semiconductor and software sales depend on reliable board and physical-layer supply.

The tension

The deepest tension is that manufacturing capacity can overshoot. If TTM and other PCB manufacturers over-invest in capacity to meet 2025-2026 demand, and if hyperscaler capex moderates or shifts priorities in 2027-2028, the industry will have too much capacity and pricing power will evaporate. Additionally, the manufacturing layer is vulnerable to geopolitical disruption. Most advanced PCB manufacturing is geographically concentrated. Export controls on equipment, materials, or finished boards; trade policy shifts; or supply-chain disruptions could create artificial scarcity that undermines hyperscaler deployment plans. TTM's European expansion suggests management is betting against this, but the risk remains.

The surprising part

The non-obvious insight is that AI infrastructure spending can be constrained by manufacturing capacity at layers most narratives ignore. The conversation around AI infrastructure usually centers on chip availability (NVIDIA supply), capital availability, and power. But the physical interconnect and board layer can be just as limiting. A hyperscaler with capital, design talent, and NVIDIA chips still cannot deploy servers if boards are unavailable. This is a reminder that digital systems still depend on physical manufacturing chokepoints, and those chokepoints can extract significant economic value.

Why it matters

For ordinary people, the PCB manufacturing story matters because it reveals that AI infrastructure deployment speed is constrained by physical manufacturing, not only by digital capability. If board capacity is a binding constraint, AI service rollout is slower and more expensive than narratives suggest. For policymakers, the stake is whether domestic PCB manufacturing capacity is resilient enough to support strategic AI infrastructure without foreign dependencies or supply-chain fragility.

PCB and substrate manufacturers like TTM capture a hidden toll from AI infrastructure buildout because hyperscalers cannot deploy accelerators and networking switches without the boards that manufacturers provide at capacity-constrained rates.
