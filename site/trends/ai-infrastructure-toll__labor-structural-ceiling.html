The Labor Ceiling: When $14 Billion in Backlog Meets the Shortage of Certified Electricians

Comfort Systems, EMCOR, MYR Group, and Primoris all report record backlogs in 2026, yet each names skilled-labor scarcity, not customer demand, as the binding constraint on growth. When a contractor cannot execute work that customers want to pay for because there are not enough licensed electricians and HVAC technicians in the market, the labor shortage itself becomes an infrastructure tax.

The AI data-center buildout has exposed a secondary labor crisis in the skilled trades. Comfort Systems' backlog nearly doubled from $8.12 billion a year ago to $14.06 billion in Q2 2026. EMCOR's remaining performance obligations hit a record $17.14 billion. MYR Group's backlog reached $3.16 billion. Primoris added 2,800+ workers in 2025 and still reported tighter labor markets for certified journeyman linemen. These companies are constrained not by lack of demand but by labor depth. This creates a structural ceiling: capital can flow to build data centers at any pace hyperscalers demand, but the number of licensed electricians cannot be produced faster than multi-year apprenticeship programs allow. When Primoris reports record $13.9 billion total backlog while posting a $24.2 million net loss, the real story is that labor cost and execution capacity have eaten into project margins.

The mechanism

The labor ceiling operates through a simple economic chokepoint: hyperscalers are building data centers and power systems faster than the contracting industry can staff qualified workers. Comfort Systems' backlog nearly doubled to $14.06 billion. EMCOR's remaining performance obligations hit $17.14 billion. MYR Group's backlog reached $3.16 billion. None of these companies lack demand. Their constraint is labor depth. When Comfort Systems reports record results while describing backlog burn rates as high and execution capacity as the limiting factor, the real story is they have more work than they can staff. This creates a structural mismatch: capital flows instantly, but the number of licensed electricians, HVAC technicians, and certified journeyman linemen cannot be produced faster than multi-year apprenticeship programs allow.

Primoris illuminates the severity. The company increased its labor force by 2,800+ people in 2025, already a massive recruiting effort. Yet management explicitly highlighted tighter markets for certified journeyman linemen as a limiting factor. In Q2 2026, despite record $13.9 billion total backlog and $1.4 billion in natural-gas power-generation awards in a single quarter, Primoris reported a net loss of $24.2 million due to project margin pressure tied to labor productivity and execution challenges. The company was not losing money because demand collapsed. It was losing money because labor costs had exceeded project estimates and crew availability was constrained. EMCOR's data is even more explicit: Network and communications work tied to data-center electrical deployment was the strongest RPO driver. But EMCOR's gross margins improved through overhead leverage, not through cheaper labor. They executed more volume by pushing harder on existing teams, not by acquiring cheaper workers.

The supply side is the core toll mechanism. A journeyman electrician requires a multi-year apprenticeship (typically 4-5 years) plus ongoing licensing and recertification. An HVAC technician requires similar depth. These skills cannot be bought in the labor market at any price in any quarter. When Comfort Systems says it is growing teams while maintaining robust demand, that growth rate is hard-constrained by recruitment and retention speed. MYR Group pursued targeted acquisitions like Valley Electric and Comet Electric to gain execution capability—code for buying crews they cannot hire fast enough. Primoris built out its workforce by 2,800+ in a single year, yet still reported tighter labor markets. That is a structural ceiling, not a temporary shortage.

Why now

The labor ceiling became acute in 2025-2026 because the pace of AI data-center buildout exceeded the rate at which the skilled-trades labor force can grow. Comfort Systems' backlog doubled from $8.12 billion to $14.06 billion in one year. EMCOR's RPO grew by $5.23 billion year-over-year. Primoris added 2,800+ workers and still reported labor constraints. The inflection point was Q2 2026: simultaneously, all four major specialty contractors reported record backlogs while naming labor availability as the primary growth constraint. This is not a cyclical shortage. This is a structural collision between hyperscaler capital velocity and skilled-trades workforce growth rates that are constrained by apprenticeship program capacity. Underlying this is demographic: the electrician and lineman workforce is aging into retirement faster than younger cohorts are entering apprenticeships, and decades of cultural messaging steering young people toward college instead of trades has compressed the pipeline.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Three constituencies pay for the labor ceiling. First, hyperscalers and infrastructure operators pay through higher contractor pricing and longer project timelines. EMCOR's higher gross margin despite data-center record bookings comes from pricing power driven by scarcity, not operational efficiency. Second, end consumers pay indirectly through higher service costs for utilities, cloud infrastructure, and electricity as construction costs are passed through. Third, contractors and skilled workers themselves pay through compressed margins and overwork on existing crews. Primoris' Q2 2026 loss of $24.2 million came because labor cost and productivity pressure ate into project margins.
Who profits
The specialty contractors and staffing platforms positioned to supply or retain skilled labor. Comfort Systems is capturing value through backlog growth and higher billing rates. EMCOR and MYR are translating strong demand into earnings because they own the crews and can reprice work faster than smaller competitors. Regional contractors and staffing platforms that control local labor relationships gain negotiating power. Private-equity firms acquiring regional contractors to consolidate crews and scale labor capacity also profit. But this profit is temporary and dependent on sustained demand—the moment hyperscaler buildout slows, contractors holding expensive oversize labor forces will face margin pressure.

The tension

The labor ceiling is not a short-term supply-chain disruption—it is a structural, multi-year constraint that could materially slow the AI infrastructure buildout. If Comfort Systems' $14.06 billion backlog burns at a constrained pace because crews are fully booked, projects take longer, cost more, and start later. If Primoris cannot find certified linemen fast enough, grid hardening and renewable-energy infrastructure get delayed. If EMCOR's data-center electrical deployment hits labor walls, facility build-outs slow. None of these companies can price their way out if the underlying labor supply does not exist. The tension breaks when either hyperscaler demand reverses or when wage compression reaches levels that drive existing workers out of the field entirely.

The surprising part

Strong backlog growth and record revenue are not proving the system is working—they are proving the labor ceiling is real. Primoris reported record $13.9 billion total backlog in Q2 2026 while posting a $24.2 million net loss. That is not a demand problem; that is a labor-cost and execution-capacity problem. EMCOR raised guidance multiple times through 2026 while simultaneously reporting that the constraint is not winning more work—it is executing the work they have already won. Comfort Systems' management repeatedly emphasizes execution depth and team growth as competitive advantage, explicitly acknowledging that execution capacity is the scarce asset. The surprise is that this scarcity is creating an environment where contractors are getting more expensive and harder to access, not cheaper—the opposite of what usually happens during a buildout cycle.

Why it matters

The labor ceiling determines how fast the AI infrastructure actually gets built. If hyperscalers want to deploy new data centers and power systems on aggressive timelines but the contracting industry cannot find enough skilled workers, timelines slip, costs rise, and the infrastructure benefits of AI get delayed for populations that depend on them. It also matters for the skilled-trades workforce. Acute labor scarcity can temporarily drive wages up, but when it forces contractors into negative-margin work or project abandonment, it can collapse wages back down and destroy the wage premium supposed to make trades careers attractive. The balance between labor scarcity driving up worker pay and scarcity driving down contractor margins determines whether the AI buildout creates durable, well-paid jobs in the skilled trades or a boom-and-bust cycle.

When contractors report record backlogs while naming skilled-labor depth as the primary constraint on growth, the AI infrastructure buildout has hit a ceiling that cannot be purchased away—only stretched through higher wages, longer timelines, and margin compression.
