The Labor Ceiling: When Demand Is Not the Bottleneck

Every specialty contractor building AI infrastructure names skilled-labor scarcity, not customer demand, as the primary growth constraint.

The AI buildout has created a secondary labor crisis in the skilled trades. Comfort Systems, EMCOR, MYR Group, and Primoris all report record backlogs and soaring demand, yet management consistently identifies skilled-labor availability as the binding constraint on growth. When a company with $14.06 billion in backlog cannot execute all of it because there are not enough certified electricians, HVAC technicians, and project managers in the market, the labor shortage itself becomes a toll that slows the entire AI infrastructure timeline and increases costs across the ecosystem.

The mechanism

The AI infrastructure toll operates through a simple economic chokepoint: hyperscalers are building data centers, substations, power delivery systems, and electrical infrastructure faster than the contracting industry can staff qualified workers to execute the work. Comfort Systems' backlog nearly doubled from $8.12 billion a year ago to $14.06 billion in Q2 2026. EMCOR's remaining performance obligations grew to a record $17.14 billion. MYR Group's backlog hit a record $3.16 billion. Yet none of these companies are constrained by lack of demand. Their constraint is labor depth. When Comfort Systems reports record results driven by 'unmatched execution' while simultaneously facing backlog burn rates they describe as 'high,' the real story is that they have more work than they can staff. This creates a structural mismatch: capital can flow to build data centers and power infrastructure at any pace investors demand. But the number of licensed electricians, HVAC technicians, and certified journeyman linemen cannot be produced faster than multi-year apprenticeship programs allow.

Primoris illuminates the severity of this constraint. In 2025, the company increased its labor force by more than 2,800 people—already a massive recruiting effort. Yet management explicitly highlighted 'tighter markets for certified journeyman linemen' as a limiting factor on utility-side work. In Q2 2026, despite record $13.9 billion total backlog and $1.4 billion in natural-gas power-generation awards in a single quarter, Primoris reported a net loss of $24.2 million due to project margin pressure tied to labor productivity and execution challenges. The company was not losing money because demand collapsed. It was losing money because the labor cost to execute work on difficult sites, during weather challenges, and with constrained crew availability had exceeded project estimates. EMCOR's data is even more explicit. Network and communications work—much of it tied to data-center electrical deployment—was the strongest driver of RPO growth in both Q1 and Q2 2026. But EMCOR's gross margins improved 'through better overhead leverage,' not through lower labor cost. That means they executed more volume by pushing harder on existing teams, not by adding cheaper labor.

The supply side of this equation is the core toll mechanism. A journeyman electrician requires a multi-year apprenticeship (typically 4-5 years) plus ongoing licensing and recertification. An HVAC technician requires similar depth. These are not skills that can be bought in the labor market at any price in any quarter. When Comfort Systems says it is 'growing teams' while maintaining 'robust ongoing demand' and 'strong pipelines for the next several quarters,' that growth rate is hard-constrained by how fast they can recruit, train, and retain field personnel. The same applies to all the specialty contractors. MYR Group has pursued targeted acquisitions like Valley Electric and Comet Electric to gain 'execution capability and local market reach'—which is code for buying crews and experienced teams they cannot hire from the open labor market fast enough. Primoris built out its workforce by 2,800+ in a single year, yet still reported tighter labor markets for core skills. That is a structural ceiling, not a temporary shortage.

Why now

The labor ceiling has become acute in 2025-2026 because the pace of AI data-center buildout, power-delivery system expansion, and electrical infrastructure deployment has exceeded the rate at which the skilled-trades labor force can grow. Comfort Systems backlog doubled in one year. EMCOR's RPO grew $5.23 billion year-over-year. Primoris added 2,800+ workers and still reported labor constraints. This is not a correction; it is an acceleration. At the same time, the skilled-trades workforce faces structural headwinds: an aging demographic of electricians and linemen in their 60s retiring faster than younger cohorts are entering apprenticeships, decades of cultural messaging steering young people toward college instead of trades, and the wage growth required to attract workers into physically demanding jobs is now severe enough that it is squeezing contractor margins on fixed-price contracts.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Three constituencies pay for the labor ceiling. First, hyperscalers and infrastructure operators pay directly through higher contractor pricing and longer project timelines. When EMCOR reports 'higher consolidated gross margin' alongside data-center record bookings, that margin comes from pricing power driven by scarcity, not operational efficiency. Second, end consumers pay indirectly through higher service costs for utilities, cloud infrastructure, and electricity as the cost of construction and deployment is passed through. Third, and most painfully, contractors and skilled workers themselves pay through compressed margins, overwork on existing crews, and the hiring and retention pressure that forces wage competition for a finite labor pool. Primoris' Q2 2026 loss of $24.2 million came because labor cost and productivity pressure ate into project margins.
Who profits
The winners are the specialty contractors and staffing platforms positioned to supply or retain skilled labor. Comfort Systems is capturing value through backlog growth and higher billing rates. EMCOR and MYR are translating strong demand into earnings despite the labor constraint because they own the crews and can reprice work faster than smaller competitors. Staffing platforms and regional contractors that control local labor relationships gain negotiating power. Private-equity firms acquiring regional contractors to consolidate crews also profit by rolling up fragmented labor capacity into scaled platforms. But the profit capture is temporary and dependent on sustained demand—the moment hyperscaler buildout slows, the contractors holding expensive oversize labor forces will face margin pressure.

The tension

The core tension is that the labor ceiling is not a short-term supply-chain disruption—it is a structural, multi-year constraint that could materially slow the AI infrastructure buildout. If Comfort Systems $14.06 billion backlog burns at a constrained pace because crews are fully booked, projects take longer, cost more, and start later. If Primoris cannot find certified linemen fast enough, grid hardening and renewable-energy infrastructure get delayed. If EMCOR's data-center electrical deployment hits labor walls, the pace of new facility build-outs slows. None of these companies can simply price their way out if the underlying labor supply does not exist. The tension breaks when either the hyperscaler buildout demand reverses or when wage compression reaches levels that drive existing workers out of the field and creates a true labor shortage rather than just a tight market.

The surprising part

The surprising part is that strong backlog growth and record revenue are not proving that the system is working—they are proving that the labor ceiling is real. Primoris reported record $13.9 billion total backlog in Q2 2026 while posting a $24.2 million net loss. That is not a demand problem; that is a labor-cost and execution-capacity problem. EMCOR raised guidance multiple times through 2026 while simultaneously reporting that the constraint is not winning more work—it is executing the work they have already won. Comfort Systems' management repeatedly emphasizes 'unmatched execution' and 'growing teams' as the source of competitive advantage, which is explicitly acknowledging that execution capacity (labor) is the scarce asset, not customer demand. The surprise is that this scarcity is creating a toll environment where contractors are getting more expensive and harder to access, not cheaper. That is economically the opposite of what usually happens during a buildout cycle.

Why it matters

The labor ceiling matters because it determines how fast the AI infrastructure actually gets built. If hyperscalers want to deploy new data centers and power systems on aggressive timelines but the contracting industry cannot find enough skilled workers, timelines slip, costs rise, and the infrastructure benefits of AI (lower latency, more computing capacity, better power delivery) get delayed for broad populations that depend on them. It also matters for the skilled-trades workforce itself. Acute labor scarcity can temporarily drive wages up, but when it forces contractors into negative-margin work or project abandonment, it can collapse wages back down and destroy the wage-premium that is supposed to make trades careers attractive to young workers. The balance between labor scarcity driving up worker pay and labor scarcity driving down contractor margins determines whether the AI buildout creates durable, well-paid jobs in the skilled trades or whether it creates a boom-and-bust cycle that leaves workers worse off.

When contractors report record backlogs while naming skilled-labor depth as the primary constraint on growth, the AI infrastructure buildout has hit a ceiling that cannot be purchased away—only stretched through higher wages, longer timelines, and margin compression.
