The Inflection: When Construction Backlog Became a Structural Bet

Construction shifted from cyclical to structural between 2023–2025 as policy-driven semiconductor and data-center capex created multi-year demand backlogs that outpaced historical patterns. The inflection is measurable: backlog visibility jumped from 9–18 months to 3–5 years, tied to specific enterprise infrastructure projects rather than macro housing or commercial cycles.

Starting in 2023–2024, construction and materials companies moved into a structural regime where AI infrastructure, semiconductor manufacturing, and cloud-facility buildout locked in multi-year demand. Comfort Systems backlog grew from $5.99B (end 2024) to $14.06B (Q2 2026). Sterling Infrastructure's combined backlog reached $5.62B with 92% from mission-critical work. Granite's CAP hit a record $7.0B. This is not cyclical; it is capacity-constrained physical buildout tied to government policy and enterprise capex horizons of 5–10 years. The constraint shifted from winning bids to executing them—labor and skilled trades became the limiting factor.

The mechanism

The 2023–2024 policy inflection arrived through three channels: the 2022 CHIPS Act began funding distribution and actual fab projects in 2023; hyperscalers accelerated data-center capex announcements (Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud, Amazon AWS all disclosed multi-year $10B+ infrastructure budgets); geopolitical tension around Taiwan and China trade policy accelerated U.S. semiconductor reshoring. This was not organic demand recovery. It was state-backed industrial policy and tech-company strategic capex hitting construction companies at the same time.

Construction backlogs exploded asymmetrically. Comfort Systems' backlog more than doubled in 18 months—a pattern unprecedented in its operating history. Sterling Infrastructure shifted 92% of its E-Infrastructure backlog into mission-critical work (data centers, fabs, manufacturing) and explicitly reallocated resources away from lower-margin transportation projects. Granite's CAP grew 32% to $7.0B, with management calling out federal portfolio work, rail, and data-center site development as new demand sources. These were not small contract wins; they were phase-gated, multi-year campuses with 5–10 year execution timelines.

The materials substrate showed the same pattern. Martin Marietta's aggregates demand shifted visibly toward infrastructure and nonresidential construction, especially data-center site work. Q2 2026 shipments grew 17% to 61.6M tons; aggregates were 88% of gross profit. Comfort Systems reported record operating cash flow of $1.14B in Q2 2026 despite high burn rates, signaling that labor and execution—not financing—became the constraint. This is the structural signal: companies that had never managed multi-year backlogs suddenly were doing so, and the bottleneck flipped from 'will we win projects' to 'can we execute them.'

Labor scarcity became explicit. Comfort Systems management highlighted execution quality and team depth as drivers of record results, with explicit risk warnings about taking on commitments that exceed labor resources. Sterling flagged resource reallocation away from transportation toward higher-margin mission-critical work—a rational allocation that revealed where margin and labor supply constraints were tightest. This is the structural inflection: backlog visibility is real, but converting backlog to revenue now depends on whether skilled-trades depth, staffing, and project execution can keep pace. The constraint is no longer demand; it is capacity.

Why now

Three inflection dates matter: June 2022 (CHIPS Act passage, setting up FY2023–2024 funding), late 2022–early 2023 (hyperscaler data-center capex announcements from Microsoft, Google, Amazon), and 2023–2024 Q3/Q4 (when these announced projects began hitting construction procurement). The timing convergence is the inflection. In prior cycles, housing led construction demand (2000s cyclical, 2010–2015 recovery). This time, enterprise capex and government policy led simultaneously, pulling through site work, electrical integration, and materials demand in concentrated geographies (Texas, Arizona, N.Carolina, Ohio semiconductor corridors). By late 2024 and into 2025, construction backlog visibility had inverted from cyclical (9–18 months) to structural (3–5 years). That shift is the origin of the toll.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Semiconductor manufacturers (Intel, TSMC, Samsung), hyperscalers (Microsoft, Google, Amazon), and equipment vendors absorb higher capex. Government subsidizes through CHIPS Act and infrastructure spending. End-customers pay through higher chip costs, cloud service margins, and equipment prices. Materials prices rise in high-demand geographies (aggregates near data-center sites climb due to local scarcity). Construction labor wages rise sharply due to sustained demand competing for skilled trades. Small contractors not positioned on these projects lose labor to higher-margin work. Consumers and taxpayers ultimately absorb the cost through device prices, service subscriptions, and tax burden.
Who profits
Concentrated beneficiaries: Comfort Systems, Sterling Infrastructure, and Granite Construction capture execution margin during the multi-year buildout window. Sterling's E-Infrastructure segment posted 123% revenue growth and 91% operating income growth in Q4 2025. Martin Marietta's aggregates business profits from volume and local pricing power in high-demand corridors. Specialized trade contractors (HVAC, electrical) in positioned geographies command premium labor rates. This is a narrow window: the 5–10 year policy-driven capex wave creates compressed returns for companies that can execute during that window. Once the capex peak passes, backlogs normalize and margins compress.

The tension

Policy reversal is the catastrophic risk. If geopolitical tension with China recedes, semiconductor reshoring loses urgency. If hyperscaler capex budgets contract or shift globally, backlogs turn into revenue headwinds. If U.S. trade policy changes, manufacturing reshoring bets become stranded. Operational risks compound: labor availability remains constrained; project execution delays are real (Granite flagged southeast weather in Q2 2026); wage inflation compresses margins if productivity does not keep pace. Housing and commercial real estate stress continue to drain labor from construction, creating bidding pressure on skilled trades. For construction, structural backlogs only convert to profit if execution holds, labor stays available, and policy does not reverse.

The surprising part

The counter-intuitive shift: construction stopped being a cyclical sector for a narrow set of companies. Historical 9–18 month visibility jumped to 3–5 years tied to specific campuses. Sterling admitted reallocating resources away from low-margin transportation toward higher-margin mission-critical infrastructure—a dramatic mix shift that signals margin arbitrage is real but narrows the winners. Comfort Systems' backlog more than doubled in 18 months, something that should have required years of demand recovery in prior cycles. This is not uniform sector recovery; it is a winner-take-most shift toward companies positioned on data-center and semiconductor geographies. The companies left out of this wave face a demand vacuum.

Why it matters

This pattern documents where industrial policy capex actually lands—not in venture capital or announcements, but in construction backlogs, materials demand, and labor scarcity. Semiconductor campuses, data centers, and AI infrastructure buildout are measurable through physical construction, not just tech narratives. Communities gain sustained construction activity, job creation, and wage pressure in positioned regions. Labor in skilled trades (electricians, HVAC, site work) becomes scarce and valuable. Public policy is getting executed: CHIPS Act funding translates directly into fab construction timelines and backlog visibility. The hidden cost: construction and materials prices rise; labor becomes contested; regions without these projects face economic divergence.

Policy-backed semiconductor and data-center capex (2023–2025) flipped construction from cyclical to structural, creating multi-year backlogs and capacity constraints that companies like Comfort Systems, Sterling, and Granite had never managed, but concentrated in narrow geographies and subject to policy reversal.
