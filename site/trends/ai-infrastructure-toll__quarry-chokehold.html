The Quarry Chokehold: Local Stone, National Power

Crushed stone and gravel cannot move more than 50 miles economically, which means every AI data-center campus is held hostage to whoever owns the permitted quarry closest to the site—and they know it.

AI-driven data-center construction is routing billions through aggregates companies because physics makes local quarry position an unbypassable toll gate. Vulcan Materials, Martin Marietta, and Knife River hold permitted reserves that hyperscalers cannot negotiate around. The result is a three-year window where regional aggregates operators collect involuntary premiums on every cubic yard of infrastructure, while hyperscaler procurement watches construction costs climb because there is literally no alternative source. A 500-acre data-center campus requires 2-3 million cubic yards of fill and base aggregate at $20-25/ton delivered—$40-75 million in non-negotiable costs on a $5-10 billion project that cannot be delayed.

The mechanism

The physics of aggregates economics creates a structural chokehold that digital-era procurement cannot escape. Crushed stone, sand, and gravel are low-density bulk materials. Transportation cost scales with distance far faster than value does. A truckload of stone worth a few hundred dollars cannot profitably move more than 50 miles—this is not friction that logistics innovation will dissolve. It is a permanent physical constraint embedded into construction cost structure. When a hyperscaler announces a $20 billion data-center campus on a specific 500-acre site, that construction schedule and cost floor are immediately hostage to the nearest permitted quarry operator. Martin Marietta Materials operates 400 quarries and distribution yards across 28 states. In fiscal 2025, aggregates generated 88% of the company's gross profit—not a diversified portfolio but a specialized quarry business. The company explicitly frames its strategy around aggregates demand tied to infrastructure, nonresidential construction, energy, and data centers. That last item is new language, reflecting reality that every megawatt of compute infrastructure requires a foundation of locally-sourced rock.

Vulcan Materials shipped 227 million tons in 2025 and achieved $11.33 cash gross profit per ton, proving the model works on disciplined volume and price discipline, not exotic leverage. Knife River ended 2025 with 1.3 billion tons of reserves and backlog that was 89% publicly funded—metrics that suggest not tight supply but carefully positioned incumbents. All three companies have built reserve bases, permitting depth, and geographic footprints to own the local scarcity that data-center construction requires. They did this over decades in advance of clear AI demand signals. Today, as hyperscalers sprint to build campuses on compressed 18-month schedules, that incumbent positioning becomes a chokehold. A challenger wanting to open a new quarry today faces 5-10 year permitting cycles, environmental reviews, and community opposition. The incumbents can expand existing sites at lower friction. That permit depth is an asset that cannot be replicated or purchased.

The trigger is sudden capital intensity of AI infrastructure. Five years ago hyperscalers built data centers at measured pace tied to incremental compute demand. Today, trillion-dollar AI procurement timelines compressed the cycle into 18-month sprint schedules. A hyperscaler announcing a 500-acre campus needs excavation, foundation work, and site development in parallel, all requiring massive aggregates inputs on compressed timeline. That urgency inverts the buyer-seller dynamic. Normally a construction buyer might shop stone from multiple regions. Today they announce a site, geology is fixed, and they depend on the permitted quarry closest to that location. If that quarry has capacity and prioritizes the work, they can charge premium rates. If the quarry does not exist yet, the timeline extends by years. Either way, the hyperscaler cannot negotiate the physics away.

Why now

The AI infrastructure buildout crystallized in 2025-2026 as hyperscalers committed multi-year capital plans simultaneously. Data-center construction intensity reached levels never seen before outside defense spending. Martin Marietta and Knife River explicitly named data-center demand as a new volume driver in Q2 2026 earnings. Q2 2026 Martin Marietta aggregates shipments reached 61.6 million tons, up 17% year-over-year, with average selling price of $22.74/ton—pricing that reflects demand tightness and urgency from construction projects. Knife River Q2 2026 showed aggregate pricing improvements of 8% on product-mix-adjusted basis with backlog increasing sequentially to $1.2 billion. Vulcan maintained cash gross profit per ton above $12 in Q2 2026 despite weather disruptions in Texas. The inflection point is that hyperscaler buildout is neither a prediction nor a forecast—it is visible in real-time in aggregates company backlogs, shipment volumes, and pricing power in Q2 2026.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Every construction company, general contractor, and construction manager working on a hyperscaler data-center campus pays higher aggregates costs because they have no alternative source and no time to find one. Those costs flow into construction budgets, project timelines, and ultimately the cost basis of the data-center asset. Construction teams that budgeted $15/ton for aggregate delivery are now negotiating at $22-25/ton because quarry operators know the project cannot move without their stone. Smaller construction firms and regional builders that need aggregates for other projects (warehouses, mixed-use, industrial sites) also face tightened supply queues as peak AI-driven demand pushes marginal projects backward.
Who profits
Vulcan Materials, Martin Marietta Materials, and Knife River Corporation profit directly. Q2 2026 Martin Marietta raised full-year guidance to $7.2B-$7.4B revenue and $2.36B-$2.5B adjusted EBITDA. Knife River reported Q2 2026 aggregate pricing improvements and raised guidance for revenue and volumes. Vulcan maintained price realization above $12 per ton despite input-cost inflation. None are reporting margin compression. They are reporting margin expansion or stability in an environment where input and freight costs are rising. Commercial Metals also benefits indirectly. CMC manufactures from 98% recycled material and serves construction sector. As data-center construction pulls rebar and fabrication into compressed timelines, CMC's backlog and pricing expand. Q3 2026 showed metal margins improving by $111/ton year-over-year as selling prices rose $130/ton while scrap increased only $19/ton.

The tension

The chokehold is temporary but visible. For 2-4 years while major hyperscaler campuses are under construction in waves, aggregates demand remains elevated and regional quarries operate at high utilization. Pricing power persists. After buildout completes, demand normalizes and prices soften. Quarry operators are aware of this cycle. The tension is whether they invested in capacity expansion that will be stranded when it reverses. Martin Marietta spent $450M on the QUIKRETE exchange and acquired New Frontier for 8M additional tons of annual capacity. Knife River spent $244.5M on growth initiatives in H1 2026, including $184.4M on acquisitions—$428.5M betting the surge lasts. If AI infrastructure buildout cools sharply in 2028-2029, those capital projects become marginal. If buildout extends 2-3 more years as expected, investments look prescient. There is also a permitting and environmental tension. Expanding capacity at existing sites requires amending permits. The companies are using recent capital to expand now, before permitting backlogs lengthen. This is a race against regulatory friction.

The surprising part

The non-obvious part is that aggregates prices matter more to total data-center project cost than most narratives admit. A 500-acre campus requires 2-3 million cubic yards of aggregate. At $20-25/ton delivered, that is $40-75 million in costs on a $5-10 billion project. It looks small as a percentage. But aggregates are among few project inputs with zero substitution and no negotiation. You cannot use less stone. You cannot use different material. You cannot import from a cheaper region. That inflexibility makes it a toll. Hyperscalers negotiate commodity inputs down—power, cooling, fiber, labor. Aggregates do not bend to that leverage. This is the layer of AI buildout that operates like a natural-resource play, not a technology play. The second surprise is that permitting advantage is durable. A startup wanting to open a new quarry faces 5-10 year cycles. Incumbent operators expanding existing sites face much lower friction. That permit depth creates an unbypassable moat that money cannot purchase—only time and prior decision-making can replicate it.

Why it matters

A person should care because this illustrates how even a digital-era phenomenon like AI infrastructure depends on old-economy physical constraints that cannot be automated away. The progress of AI, the competitiveness of cloud computing, and the pace of data-center expansion are ultimately limited by the speed at which rock can be extracted from ground, trucked 30-50 miles, and compacted into foundations. Permitting, environmental review, and local opposition to quarrying become indirect constraints on AI deployment. If aggregate supply tightens enough, it can compress data-center construction timelines and affect cloud-service availability and cost. Permitting reform that accelerates aggregate extraction could unlock billions of dollars of faster infrastructure deployment. This is the overlooked infrastructure chokepoint—not power or fiber, but dirt and stone. The physical constraints in modern economy often hide in boring industries. Quarries, trucking, permitting, and local supply chains do not capture venture capital or cultural attention. But they are hard constraints on how fast and how cheap AI infrastructure can actually be built.

Every cubic yard of AI infrastructure passes through a quarry that the hyperscaler does not own and cannot replace, and the quarry operator knows it.
