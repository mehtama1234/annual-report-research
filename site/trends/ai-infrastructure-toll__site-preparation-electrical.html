Dirt, Trenches, and High Voltage: The Site-Prep Tier

Data-center and semiconductor campuses require grading, underground electrical conduit, substation tie-ins, and mission-critical electrical integration—work that four specialty contractors (Sterling, Primoris, Dycom, MYR Group) have deliberately repositioned to dominate. Sterling's E-Infrastructure backlog is 92% mission-critical projects; Primoris and Dycom each record backlog of $13.9B and $11.9B; MYR Group's combined segments hit $3.16B. This tier now captures margin and execution moat where AI demand compresses timelines into 18–24-month site-work windows.

Site-preparation and mission-critical electrical work have bifurcated into a high-margin specialty tier within industrial construction. Data-center, semiconductor, and advanced-manufacturing customers pay premium rates for contractors who can execute grading, utilities integration, substation tie-ins, and complex on-site electrical systems in parallel, with labor capacity and execution discipline as the binding constraint. Sterling, Primoris, Dycom, and MYR Group have each repositioned capital and labor allocation toward this segment since mid-2025, competing on project-execution depth and the ability to bind civil work with electrical systems in integrated delivery.

The mechanism

Data centers, semiconductor fabs, and advanced manufacturing cannot be deployed to raw land. They require site grading and slope stabilization, stormwater management, underground electrical conduit integration, high-voltage substation tie-ins, and precision earthwork that links civil infrastructure to mission-critical power delivery. Sterling Infrastructure has captured this opportunity by explicitly repositioning away from commodity transportation work toward what it calls 'E-Infrastructure Solutions'—a bundled offering of site development plus electrical services. In Q2 2026, Sterling reported that mission-critical projects (data centers, semiconductor facilities, manufacturing) represented 92% of E-Infrastructure backlog, and the company disclosed delivering integrated site and electrical services on two data-center campuses simultaneously. This is not an ancillary capability; it is Sterling's growth engine, accounting for the company's first-time-ever 20%+ adjusted EBITDA margin in 2025.

Primoris and Dycom approach the same tier through different entry points but land in the same economic pool. Primoris, historically anchored in utilities and gas operations, closed the PayneCrest Electric acquisition on May 1, 2026, explicitly extending into 'industrial, manufacturing, advanced facilities, and data-center services.' That acquisition followed a 2,800-person labor hire during 2025, with management candidly highlighting 'tighter markets for certified journeyman linemen' as an operating constraint. Dycom, a telecom-and-fiber contractor, acquired Power Solutions (a Mid-Atlantic data-center electrical contractor) and announced National Technology Integrators to enter structured cabling and inside-plant electrical work within critical facilities. The language differs (Primoris: 'advanced facilities'; Dycom: 'inside the fence'), but the economic reality is identical: there is now a distinct market segment where specialized electrical and site-execution contractors extract premium margins by wrapping mission-critical infrastructure around hyperscaler data centers.

MYR Group sharpens the two-sided economics. The company's 'Transmission and Distribution' segment covers high-voltage transmission, substations, overhead and underground distribution, and clean-energy projects ($1.27B Q2 2026 backlog)—the utility-side backbone that must expand to feed data-center loads. Its 'Commercial and Industrial' segment explicitly serves 'data centers, airports, hospitals, manufacturing plants, processing facilities'—the customer-side electrical integration ($1.89B Q2 2026 backlog). MYR's record $3.16B total backlog (Q2 2026), split roughly evenly across segments, demonstrates that site and electrical work is being booked from both ends simultaneously: utilities expanding grid capacity to support data centers, and data-center operators hiring electrical contractors to integrate that expanded supply into their facilities. This dual pull distinguishes the site-preparation tier from generic infrastructure work—it exists because AI and semiconductor demand create a specific project type that requires both utility-scale electrical expertise and complex on-site systems.

Labor capacity has become the binding constraint, reshaping competitive advantage and project economics. Dycom explicitly stated that 'customers are extending contract durations to lock in skilled workforce capacity'—a direct admission that electricians, linemen, site engineers, and equipment operators are no longer abundant. Primoris increased its labor force by 2,800 people in 2025 and explicitly emphasized the advantage of self-performing the vast majority of work. Sterling's adjusted EBITDA margin exceeded 20% for the first time in company history in 2025, a quality shift achieved not only through resource reallocation away from lower-margin transportation work, but also because mission-critical projects command premium rates when skilled labor is scarce. The site-preparation tier is therefore not primarily a volume story; it is a labor moat story, where contractors who maintain specialized field teams, project management depth, and execution discipline win repeated awards at improving margins.

Project sequencing and multi-year visibility reinforce tier economics. Sterling reported $7.0B+ visibility when combining signed backlog ($4.33B Q2 2026), unsigned awards, and future-phase opportunities. Dycom's 2.2x book-to-bill (with $11.9B backlog against quarterly run-rate revenue of ~$2B) implies four-to-five years of forward work visibility. Primoris booked $1.4B of natural-gas power-generation awards in a single quarter (Q2 2026) and closed 2025 with total backlog of $11.9B. This multi-year visibility allows contractors to invest confidently in labor hiring, equipment staging, and project management infrastructure, further concentrating competitive advantage among firms that already have scale. Primoris's Q2 2026 net loss of $24.2M despite record backlog and strong utilities demand, however, shows that 'project redesign, sequencing, labor productivity, sub-surface conditions, and weather' can rapidly flip project economics—a reminder that backlog is a leading indicator, not a guarantee of margin conversion.

Why now

AI and semiconductor buildout have compressed the data-center and fab expansion timeline from a 5-year cadence (similar to the 2010-era buildout) into 18–24-month site-work windows. Hyperscalers (Google, Amazon, Microsoft, Meta) and semiconductor manufacturers (Intel, TSMC, Samsung) are now allocating capex at densities and speeds that demand rapid site preparation and electrical integration. Simultaneously, the electrical grid itself is under structural strain. Utilities are expanding transmission and substations to support data-center load growth (from near-zero in 2010 to multi-gigawatt commitments today) while managing aging infrastructure replacement and electrification (EVs, building heating, industrial process conversion). This dual squeeze—simultaneous site-prep and electrical-integration pull from both data-center operators and utilities—created a distinct high-margin segment that did not exist in comparable form in 2020. The repositioning is concrete and recent: Sterling deliberately shifted from transportation work to E-Infrastructure between 2024 and Q2 2026; Primoris closed the PayneCrest acquisition on May 1, 2026; Dycom announced National Technology Integrators in mid-2026. All four contractors increased headcount and visibility in this segment between 2025 and mid-2026, confirming real-time capital allocation into this window.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Hyperscalers (Google, Amazon, Microsoft, Meta), semiconductor manufacturers (Intel, TSMC, Samsung), and advanced-manufacturing operators pay for site-preparation and electrical work through capital budgets for data-center and fab campuses. Some hire Sterling, Primoris, Dycom, or MYR directly as general or specialty contractors. Others fund the work indirectly through utilities (which recover costs via tariff recovery and rate-base expansion) or through public-private partnerships on power-generation facilities that feed data centers. A manufacturing engineer at a chip-fab campus knows that before the fab building is erected, 18–24 months of site work must complete: grading, stormwater, electrical substations, underground utilities. That work cannot be deferred; it is paid from the same capex bucket as the fab building itself.
Who profits
Sterling Infrastructure, Primoris Services, Dycom Industries, and MYR Group capture margin in this tier. Sterling's adjusted EBITDA margin exceeded 20% for the first time in 2025, driven by E-Infrastructure mix. Dycom achieved 2.2x book-to-bill and 46.5% year-over-year backlog growth (fiscal Q1 2027) with margins expanding through data-center and fiber infrastructure work. MYR Group's Commercial and Industrial backlog reached $1.89B (Q2 2026), with the segment expanding alongside utility-side Transmission and Distribution work. Primoris booked $1.4B of natural-gas generation awards in Q2 2026 alone. These four firms are not alone—Quanta Services, MasTec, Granite Construction, and others also compete—but they have been most explicit about repositioning capital toward mission-critical infrastructure. Margin uplift is real: contractors charge premium rates when customers have tight schedules, labor is scarce, and mission-critical infrastructure cannot fail or slip. Sterling's intentional abandonment of low-margin transportation work exemplifies this positioning.

The tension

The site-preparation tier faces multiple breaking points. First, labor productivity on complex electrical projects is not stable. Primoris's Q2 2026 loss of $24.2M despite record backlog demonstrates that project redesign, sequencing, labor productivity, sub-surface conditions, and weather can flip project economics from favorable to distressed. Second, multi-year backlogs assume project starts and phase-gate timing remain intact, but if data-center or semiconductor capital plans slip, backlog can become stale faster than revenue recognition suggests. Third, the labor pool is finite. Dycom and Primoris are both increasing headcount aggressively, but if certified linemen and project engineers become truly scarce, wage inflation could compress margins across the tier. Fourth, regulatory and permitting delays on large campuses (environmental review, utility interconnection, local approvals) can slow project starts and extend timelines, reducing the execution efficiency that contractors count on when quoting margin. Finally, if AI capex momentum slows—which would reverse the demand pull-through—site-preparation work becomes a trailing indicator, deprioritized in favor of maintenance and lower-margin work. Sterling's intentional resource reallocation away from transportation is a bet; if it misfires, the company has made itself smaller and more dependent on a single end-market.

The surprising part

Site-preparation and electrical work are not scalable in the traditional sense. Dycom can deploy 40 operating companies across all 50 states, but that structure reflects the local, project-specific nature of fiber and communications work. Primoris and MYR Group have deep geographic footprints, but they compete on project-by-project wins, not manufacturing scale or algorithmic leverage. This is a labor-intensive, margin-differentiated business where competitive advantage flows from project execution discipline, labor relationships, and the ability to absorb complexity—not from technology or process efficiency. That stands in sharp contrast to AI and semiconductor industry narratives, which stress algorithmic innovation, compute scaling, and software leverage. The truth is that data centers and fabs require both: cutting-edge cooling, power delivery, and architectural systems from equipment vendors like Vertiv and Eaton, but also Sterling, Dycom, and Primoris to dig trenches, pull cable, wire substations, and build the foundation. The site-preparation tier is booming not because technology improved but because demand density improved—data centers are being built faster and closer together than ever, which means contractors can maintain utilization and labor deployment without sacrificing project discipline or safety. The margin pool is not infinite, and it is entirely dependent on labor availability and field execution skill, not on technological breakthroughs.

Why it matters

Data-center and semiconductor buildout speed affects cloud service availability and cost (which shapes productivity and equity), digital-infrastructure resilience (which affects national security and disaster recovery), and computation capacity distribution across geographies (which determines whether AI and compute remain concentrated in a few hyperscaler hubs or diffuse more broadly). Site-preparation capacity is a real binding constraint. If contractors cannot dig, grade, and integrate electrical systems fast enough to match hyperscaler buildout plans, data-center deployment slows, cloud costs may rise, and innovation concentrates further in well-served regions. Conversely, if site-preparation becomes commoditized and margins compress, contractors may under-invest in labor, safety, and execution discipline, risking project failures, cost overruns, and delays that harm companies trying to build infrastructure and, by extension, users who depend on it. The labor pool is especially important: the United States does not have infinite certified linemen, electricians, and project managers, and if site-preparation work pulls the most skilled field talent away from other infrastructure (water systems, power grids, telecommunications), there are hidden resource-allocation tradeoffs across different types of physical infrastructure that nobody is explicitly managing.

Site-preparation and mission-critical electrical work have bifurcated into a high-margin specialty tier where labor capacity and execution discipline command premium pricing from data-center and semiconductor operators competing for speed and reliability.
