Pricing Into the Habit: When Inelastic Demand Is the Business Model

Where demand is structurally inelastic—addiction, chronic disease, medical necessity—companies stop innovating and start pricing. Earnings grow because customers have no exit, not because the business improved. This is the moment when pricing power becomes naked.

Once a company controls inelastic demand, it discovers that price increases flow directly to earnings with zero reinvestment. Philip Morris grew combustibles revenues 9.5% in Q2 2026 while shipment volumes declined—pure pricing extraction on addicted customers. BrightSpring raised revenue per prescription to $314.20 on flat script counts while gross profit per script jumped to $27.50. Cencora's U.S. Healthcare Solutions operating income grew 22% year over year, powered by specialty utilization and pharmacy pricing discipline. The habit is the business now, not the product.

The mechanism

Inelastic demand is not a metaphor. It means quantity demanded does not move when price rises because the customer cannot switch, has no alternative, or faces death or disability if they do not pay. A diabetic cannot choose not to use insulin when the price rises 20%. A cancer patient on specialty infusion therapy cannot call a competitor. A patient on chronic opioid management cannot quit because the copay went up. A smoker addicted to nicotine feels the price rise but cannot simply walk away. Once demand is this inelastic, price increases decouple entirely from value creation. The customer pays more because they have no choice, not because they received something better. The company pockets the difference.

Philip Morris International is the model in its purest form. The company grew combustibles net revenues 9.5% in Q2 2026 while shipment volumes declined. This is not a story about winning new customers or growing the smoking population. It is a story about extracting more cash from a shrinking base of addicted consumers. Management did not invest in better product development or marketing. It changed the price list. Because nicotine addiction is one of the most inelastic demand systems on earth, customers absorbed the increase. Earnings per share jumped from $4.52 to $7.26 in a single year—a 60% increase—not because Philip Morris made better cigarettes but because the company realized it could harvest substantially more cash from existing, trapped customers.

BrightSpring's pharmacy model shows the same extraction in healthcare. In Q2 2026, prescriptions dispensed were flat—approximately 43 million per year with zero growth—while revenue per script rose to $314.20 and gross profit per script climbed to $27.50. The company did not invest in new clinical services or better medications. It raised prices on a captive patient population of chronically ill people who have no choice but to take their medications. A cancer patient on specialty pharmacy cannot call another provider. A dialysis patient cannot switch on a whim. A patient managing multiple chronic conditions cannot refuse to have their medications filled. BrightSpring controls the infrastructure, and price power comes with that control. The home-health census grew 54% year over year, but that census growth is from acquisitions and market consolidation, not from better care—it is from scale that translates directly into pricing leverage.

Cencora operates the middle layer of this extraction system. The company's U.S. Healthcare Solutions operating income grew 22% year over year while the company managed specialty pharmaceutical utilization, pharmacy pricing, and provider contracts. Cencora does not sell the drug; it controls the flow of the drug. Providers and pharmacies depend on Cencora for uninterrupted access to medications. Manufacturers depend on Cencora to reach providers and patients. This middle position means Cencora can raise prices on both sides—specialty pharmacy margins climbed because Cencora manages scarcity and utilization. The company does not have to invest in drug development or clinical innovation. It just has to manage the choke point. Adjusted diluted EPS reached $16.00 for FY2025 on $321 billion in consolidated revenue because the distribution system itself is now the pricing lever.

Why now

Three forces collided in 2025-2026 to make addiction pricing visible as a naked strategy. First, growth scarcity made investors desperate for earnings that do not require reinvestment. When a company raises prices on flat volumes and pockets the margin, that feels like a win to capital markets. Second, healthcare consolidation concentrated chronic-disease management and pharmacy into a handful of large platforms—BrightSpring, Cigna, Cencora, CVS—each with less competitive pressure and more unilateral pricing power. A patient or provider can no longer play one pharmacy against another because consolidation has eliminated choice. Third, nicotine categories stopped their secular decline in 2024-2025 and stabilized at a lower but durable level. Philip Morris no longer has to fight daily volume loss; it can harvest from a known, stable customer base. That stability makes the pricing strategy sustainable. The addiction is permanent, the customer is locked in, and elasticity is zero. Therefore price increases flow directly to earnings without any reinvestment. For the first time, investors treat this not as squeezing a dying category, but as harvesting an entrenched habit.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Concrete people pay in specific ways. A smoker who bought a pack for $8 five years ago now pays $12 today, and the price rises every quarter. A patient on specialty cancer therapy pays $5,000 per month through insurance premiums and copays, and that copay rises annually even though the underlying drug did not improve. A Medicare patient on chronic prescriptions pays through higher insurance premiums and out-of-pocket costs as consolidated pharmacies like BrightSpring raise revenue per script. A family managing a loved one in home health pays through insurance deductibles and through reduced access to other services as consolidated providers consolidate and raise prices faster than inflation. A low-income smoker feels the price most acutely because cigarettes already consume 10-15% of disposable income in working-class households. The burden falls on the least able to absorb it: addicted smokers with the lowest incomes, chronically ill patients without negotiating power, elderly and disabled people with no alternatives, families in lower-income geographies where consolidation is highest.
Who profits
Four groups capture the harvest. First, the companies themselves: Philip Morris grew EPS 60% in one year; BrightSpring raised revenue per script on flat volumes; Cencora's operating income grew 22%. These are not innovation wins—they are pricing wins on captive demand. Second, shareholders of those companies who receive buybacks and dividend growth funded by cash extracted from price increases on inelastic demand. BrightSpring repurchased 1.5 million shares; Philip Morris maintains a high dividend; Cencora has returned significant capital to shareholders. Third, private-equity sponsors and financial buyers who backed these roll-ups understanding that chronic-disease management and pharmacy are low-growth, high-margin, price-disciplined businesses where the real return is in consolidation and pricing discipline, not in operations. Fourth, financial intermediaries like Cigna and CVS that control both supply (as a pharmacy benefit manager or pharmacy operator) and demand (as an insurer), allowing them to keep more of the rent at every step. The profit pool has narrowed into a smaller set of hands.

The tension

The model breaks when price increases hit affordability walls or regulatory backlash. Philip Morris faces this in territories where excise taxes have already made cigarettes a luxury good—in some European markets and Australia, a pack costs $25-30 USD equivalent. At some price point, even addicted consumers stretch packs, switch to bootleg supplies, or shift to lower-cost nicotine formats. BrightSpring faces it when state Medicaid programs simply refuse to pay higher rates. Medicaid pays for roughly 30% of home health and specialty pharmacy, and if a state legislature caps reimbursement, BrightSpring loses volume and margins compress. Cencora faces it when employers and government programs push back against pharmaceutical inflation. The Inflation Reduction Act included drug-pricing language; Medicare negotiation proposals have been tabled multiple times; multiple states have passed price-transparency laws. At some threshold, the inelastic-demand assumption breaks. When a patient cannot afford both medication and food, when a family chooses to skip doses to stretch prescriptions, when a state legislature passes drug-pricing controls, the pricing engine stalls. The second tension is political. Nicotine pricing is already a political issue in multiple countries. Healthcare pricing is under constant legislative threat. Companies are harvesting from this demand precisely because they know regulators cannot touch it yet. But that premium on untouchable demand is exactly what makes regulators eventually touch it. The longer the harvest continues without reinvestment, the more politically vulnerable the business becomes.

The surprising part

The surprise is not that companies raise prices. The surprise is that investors celebrate 60% EPS growth that comes from extracting more cash from declining volumes while treating it as innovation rather than redistribution. Philip Morris's earnings jumped on declining cigarette shipments, and the market called it pricing discipline and margin expansion, not what it is: harvesting a captive customer base. BrightSpring raised revenue per script on flat volumes with zero clinical improvement, and the market applauded it as operating leverage. Cencora's operating income grew 22% while the company simply managed pharmacy pricing and specialty utilization in a consolidated market. None of this is operational excellence. None of this requires investment. All of it depends on customers having no exit. The financial system has normalized the idea that earnings growth without any value creation—pure extraction from inelastic demand—is a legitimate business strategy worth a premium multiple. That normalization is itself the story.

Why it matters

This is how systematic inequality gets baked into healthcare and consumer goods. The people most addicted to nicotine are low-income smokers who cannot quit; they pay rising prices. The people most dependent on chronic-disease medications are sicker, poorer populations without negotiating power; they pay more each year. The people most vulnerable to home-health and specialty-pharmacy pricing are the elderly and disabled who have no alternatives and no voice. The pricing engine extracts value specifically from the most vulnerable populations because those populations have zero elasticity—they have to pay or die. The business model works perfectly as long as the victims have no political voice. But when a political coalition decides that addiction pricing is unjust—when it becomes a campaign issue, a legislative priority, or a regulatory focus—the entire financial structure collapses because the economics depend entirely on captive demand having no choice and no voice. That moment is coming.

When demand is truly addictive or medically necessary, companies stop creating value and start extracting cash—raising prices on captive customers and rewarding the harvest with higher stock multiples until regulation or affordability finally breaks the model.
