EchoStar: The Harvest That Ate Itself

EchoStar posted $15.0B in revenue and still wrote a going-concern warning into its own annual report. When the captive base shrinks faster than price hikes can offset, and the escape plan (owned 5G) fails, the harvest does not slow down gracefully. It ends in deconsolidation.

EchoStar is the clearest proof that the cash harvest has a hard floor. Pay-TV subscribers fell from 6.998M at FY2025 year-end to 6.391M by Q2 2026, a 607K drop in two quarters, as price increases finally exceeded perceived value. The company faced $7.321B in current debt and finance-lease obligations against $1.883B in cash ($2.984B including marketable securities), forcing substantial-doubt going-concern language into its FY2025 annual report. The 5G buildout that was supposed to replace pay-TV economics failed its own test: EchoStar migrated all customer traffic off its owned network onto AT&T by November 15, 2025. By June 30, 2026, substantially all of the Pay-TV segment was deconsolidated. The spectrum, orbital slots, and Hughes assets retain value; the consumer franchise does not.

The mechanism

EchoStar ran the harvest in its purest form: extract recurring cash from millions of legacy satellite pay-TV subscribers (6.998M DISH TV and SLING TV at FY2025 year-end) while burning billions to build a facilities-based 5G network that would eventually replace the declining video economics. The design required milking an aging base through price increases at the same time as funding a wireless buildout that could earn the money back later. Short-term extraction was supposed to subsidize long-term optionality.

The failure surfaced in 2025-2026 when the base shrank faster than management had modeled. Pay-TV subscribers fell 607,000 in two quarters (6.998M to 6.391M via 6.632M in Q1 2026). The price increases meant to raise revenue per customer instead accelerated churn, and the leavers were not switching to EchoStar's own 5G service; they were exiting to streaming, wireless-only, and broadband. Meanwhile the 5G bet failed its core strategic test: by November 15, 2025, EchoStar had migrated all customer traffic off its owned network onto AT&T and moved to a hybrid MNO model, abandoning the facilities-based ambition. A company that spent years building its own network ended up renting AT&T's while still owing the debt for infrastructure it no longer used.

The balance-sheet arithmetic then closed the trap. FY2025 ended with $7.321B in current debt and finance-lease obligations, including 2026 maturities of $2.0B in July, $1.377B in August, and $2.750B in December, against $1.883B in cash ($2.984B with marketable securities). The FY2025 annual report stated the company did not have the cash on hand, projected cash flows, or committed financing to fund its obligations over the next twelve months, and that this raised substantial doubt about its ability to continue as a going concern. Operating losses were $(17.723)B for the year, driven by impairment write-downs, while the company still booked $15.005B in revenue. By June 30, 2026, substantially all of the Pay-TV segment had been deconsolidated; the deconsolidated entities now merely lease capacity on EchoStar's satellite fleet, a shift that reflected restructuring mechanics rather than any organic recovery.

Why now

The unwind is not a slow decline; it is a discrete break dated to late 2025. For years a harvester can raise prices on a captive base and keep cash flow positive even as the base erodes, but there is a point where the annual increase exceeds perceived value and subscribers leave in waves rather than a trickle. EchoStar hit that point in 2025-2026, losing 607K pay-TV subscribers in two quarters. The hedge (owned 5G) was supposed to catch the fall, but the company conceded it could not compete with AT&T's installed network and moved all traffic onto AT&T by November 15, 2025. Those two failures collided with a hard 2026 maturity calendar ($2.0B July, $1.377B August, $2.750B December) and only $1.883B of cash, and the FY2025 annual report carried explicit going-concern language. The honest read is that the harvest model has no graceful exit: when capex-driven optionality meets an accelerating subscriber cliff and a maturity wall in the same year, there is no positive cash flow left to harvest and no optionality left to monetize.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
The remaining DISH TV and SLING TV subscribers who stayed through years of price increases and now face restructuring and forced migration. EchoStar employees, many of whom worked on the abandoned 5G buildout. Suppliers and contractors who relied on EchoStar as a customer and now carry payment risk through the restructuring. Rural households and enterprise and government users who depended on satellite connectivity through Hughes. And the broader competitive ecosystem that had looked to EchoStar as a fourth-carrier and technology alternative.
Who profits
Creditors holding EchoStar debt, who negotiate recovery through restructuring, and the buyers of the underlying assets. AT&T is the most direct beneficiary, absorbing the migrated customer traffic and tied to EchoStar's spectrum monetization along with SpaceX. Financial buyers who can strip spectrum, orbital slots, satellite capacity, and Hughes from the failed operating company profit from salvage value. No one profits from the consumer franchise itself, because it is unprofitable and shrinking; the value that survives is asset scarcity, not franchise durability.

The tension

The harvest's structural paradox is fully exposed here: extracting more cash from a shrinking base requires raising prices, and raising prices accelerates the departure of marginal customers, which at scale becomes a death spiral. EchoStar tried to hedge with owned 5G, but the hedge failed and forced it onto AT&T anyway. It then faced $7.321B in current obligations against $1.883B in cash with no optionality left to monetize. The real cost lands on customers who lose service, employees who lose jobs, and suppliers who lose a customer; the balance sheet loses paper value through impairments, but the capital that built the spectrum and orbital slots was already spent. Recurring revenue only helps if it comfortably outruns maturities, capex, and restructuring drag, and here it did not.

The surprising part

The most striking fact is that EchoStar generated $15.0B in revenue while losing $14.5B net and carrying $7.321B in current obligations plus $18.659B noncurrent. Revenue was never the problem; returns were. This is the inverse of the usual insolvency story: the company had ample top-line scale but structurally negative returns on the capital it had already sunk into a network it then abandoned. When a harvester's base shrinks faster than it can raise prices, the leverage taken on assuming revenue growth or margin expansion becomes a trap, because that growth never arrived. A company can have millions of paying customers, recurring enterprise and government revenue, and valuable spectrum, and still be forced into deconsolidation because the cash never comfortably cleared the maturities.

Why it matters

This is a case study in stranded infrastructure and the human cost of an extraction strategy that fails. EchoStar's spectrum, orbital slots, satellite fleet, and Hughes connectivity are still strategically valuable, but that value cannot be realized through the consumer franchise. Millions of pay-TV customers are losing service through restructuring, rural communities lose a satellite connectivity option, and the workforce that built the owned 5G network is being disbanded. The infrastructure that once connected remote America is being carved up by creditors and financial buyers. It is the negative case against the whole cash-harvest posture: when the most extractable strategy is squeezing a captive, shrinking base rather than building durable value, the eventual failure damages not just shareholders but customers, workers, and communities.

EchoStar tried to fund a wireless future by harvesting legacy pay-TV cash, and discovered that neither the harvest nor the wireless future could clear a $7.3B maturity wall, so it deconsolidated the franchise and moved its customers onto AT&T's network.
