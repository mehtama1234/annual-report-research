The Last Mile Lock-In: Broadband, Cable, and Captive Connectivity

Where physical infrastructure creates a de facto duopoly at the household level, operators can raise prices on captive customers even as substitute services proliferate.

When physical infrastructure creates local market power, the operator can extract escalating value through price increases on broadband and connectivity services, even as video subscribers leave and bundling economics collapse. Charter and Comcast demonstrate that the household gateway remains a captive relationship—customers have few alternatives—and both operators are using that captivity to maintain household revenue while legacy video services decline.

The mechanism

The fundamental infrastructure monopoly operates at the neighborhood level, not nationally. Most US households and businesses have at most two fixed-line broadband options: cable (Charter, Comcast) or telephone lines (AT&T, Verizon DSL). Fiber penetration remains low outside urban cores. This duopoly, often a monopoly in suburban and rural passings, creates captivity: a household cannot easily switch to a competitive alternative if they want reliable broadband. This captivity is the foundation of the cash harvest.

Charter and Comcast use this captivity to execute a price-maintenance and wallet-expansion strategy. Video subscribers erode—Charter lost 172K Internet customers in Q2 2026, but the household relationship remains sticky because the alternative is no broadband at all or a much slower technology. Both operators respond by raising broadband prices and offering mobile convergence bundles. The household pays more for connectivity (which the operator sells as essential) to compensate for declining video revenue. The operator's revenue per household stays flat or rises even as video declines, because the captive customer has nowhere to go.

Liberty Broadband exposes the capital-layer version of this monopoly. Liberty's $9.85B in Q1 2026 assets consist almost entirely of its 25% voting stake in Charter. This wrapper structure demonstrates how recurring household captivity can be sliced across multiple public entities: Charter monetizes the customer, Liberty captures returns through ownership governance and tax planning. The margin extraction happens at multiple layers. When Charter raises broadband prices, the rent flows to Charter's cash, then filters up to Liberty's Charter stake, then gets allocated through financing structures and exchangeable-debt puts. Every layer of the structure depends on the underlying fact that households have nowhere else to go.

Why now

Broadband has become truly essential—necessary for work, education, and entertainment in ways it was not in 2015. Simultaneously, fixed-line operators face maturity (wireless maturity) and substitution (streaming video, software communication). This convergence creates pressure on operators to maximize value from the one remaining essential and captive service: connectivity itself. Video bundles no longer hold customer relationships; broadband does. As that becomes obvious, operators raise broadband prices aggressively because the elasticity is low—customers need it and cannot easily switch.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Households and small businesses that depend on fixed-line broadband for connectivity. In most areas, they have at most one or two realistic options. When the local operator raises broadband prices by 5-8% annually—which both Charter and Comcast have done—customers pay it because the alternative is to lose productivity, education access, or entertainment. The burden falls most heavily on price-sensitive households that do not have wireless alternatives or fiber options.
Who profits
Charter Communications, Comcast Corporation, and their shareholders. Both operators are using captive broadband pricing to maintain household economics as video erodes. Broadband gross margins (after network costs) remain very high—50%+ in many cases—and the infrastructure has mostly been built and is being milked rather than expanded aggressively. Liberty Broadband's Charter stake multiplies the profit by layering governance and tax benefits on top of the underlying household captivity.

The tension

The infrastructure monopoly is fragile in two ways that are just now becoming visible. First, fiber-overbuilds are expanding in selected markets (Verizon's Frontier acquisition, for example), which will eventually erode Charter's and Comcast's duopoly in those geographies. Second, regulatory backlash against broadband pricing is mounting—states and the FTC have opened proceedings into broadband pricing practices. If regulators mandate price ceilings or require facility-sharing, the operator's ability to extract escalating value from household captivity will collapse. The external cost is a consumer expectation that connectivity is an essential service, which creates political and social pressure for price limits. The operator's attempt to harvest cash from captivity is directly creating the political willingness to regulate that captivity away.

The surprising part

The most non-obvious insight is that the infrastructure monopoly does not exist at the national or regional level—it exists at the neighborhood or census-tract level. A customer cannot switch from Charter to Comcast. They can only switch between broadband and not having broadband. This hyperlocal monopoly is invisible to national competitive analysis but decisive for pricing power. Also surprising: neither Charter nor Comcast is using captive pricing to invest heavily in next-generation network. They are using it to maintain cash returns while the asset base ages. That suggests the operator believes the monopoly will remain durable for at least 5-10 years, but not so durable that reinvestment is justified.

Why it matters

If fixed-line broadband pricing is determined by local monopoly power rather than competition, broadband becomes a regressive tax on households that cannot afford wireless alternatives or fiber overbuilds. It also discourages investment in next-generation networks because the operator can harvest cash from the existing infrastructure longer than innovation would otherwise merit. The long-term risk is that broadband pricing becomes politically untenable, leading to regulation or utility classification that fundamentally alters the cash-harvest model. Some households will eventually have wireless or fiber alternatives; others will not. Those without alternatives will either pay escalating prices or demand public intervention.

The household that needs broadband has no choice but the local cable operator, which means that operator can raise prices regardless of competitive or technological change, converting the captive relationship into reliable cash extraction.
