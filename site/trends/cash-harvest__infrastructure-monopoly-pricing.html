The Last Mile Lock-In: Broadband Captivity and the Household Gateway Monopoly

Where physical infrastructure creates hyperlocal duopoly at census-tract level, operators raise broadband prices on captive customers regardless of competitive or technological change. Charter lost 172K Internet customers in Q2 2026 but added 406K mobile lines—the household relationship persists even as video erodes.

Infrastructure monopoly operates at neighborhood level, not nationally. Most US households have at most two fixed-line broadband options: cable or DSL telephone. Fiber penetration remains low outside urban cores. This hyperlocal duopoly creates captivity—a household cannot switch to competitive alternative if they want reliable broadband. Charter and Comcast monetize captivity through price maintenance and wallet expansion. Video subscribers erode, but household relationship stays sticky because alternative is no broadband. Both operators respond by raising broadband prices and offering mobile convergence. Household pays more for essential connectivity to compensate for declining video revenue. Revenue per household stays flat or rises even as video declines.

The mechanism

The fundamental infrastructure monopoly exists at neighborhood or census-tract level. A household cannot switch from Charter to Comcast. It can only choose broadband or not having broadband. This hyperlocal monopoly is invisible to national competitive analysis but decisive for pricing power. Charter lost 172K Internet customers Q2 2026 but had no loss of relationship captivity because the loss was erosion, not defection to competitor. Customers cannot buy broadband from someone else.

Charter and Comcast execute price-maintenance and wallet-expansion strategy using captivity. Video subscribers erode (Charter's video customer base is implied declining through video-revenue pressure), but household relationship remains sticky. Alternative is no broadband at all or much slower technology. Both operators respond by raising broadband prices (data not disclosed granularly but captured in broadband revenue maintenance despite customer erosion) and offering mobile convergence bundles. Household pays more for connectivity sold as essential to compensate for declining video. Q2 2026 showed Charter added 406K mobile lines while losing Internet customers, proving the household is being upsold mobile to offset broadband attrition risk.

Charter's capital structure proves the monopoly harvesting model. FY2025 revenues $54.774B against Adjusted EBITDA $22.708B (41.4% EBITDA margin) with principal debt $94.6B—the company is not reinvesting aggressively to build next-generation networks or compete on capability. Instead, the company is harvesting existing infrastructure, maintaining pricing on captive customers, and returning capital through buybacks. Q2 2026 free cash flow was $969M despite net income $1.292B and Adjusted EBITDA $5.449B—the company prioritizes share repurchases and debt reduction over network evolution because the underlying belief is that the monopoly will remain durable for 5-10 years without reinvestment-level capex.

Why now

Two forces converged to make broadband captivity pricing explicit starting in 2025-2026. First: broadband became truly essential for work, education, and connected-device infrastructure in ways it was not in 2020. The digital-divide awareness and broadband-subsidy programs (ACP) that expanded 2023-2024 actually validated broadband necessity while creating political cover for operators to raise prices on the remaining unsubsidized customer base. Operators argued necessary infrastructure investment justified pricing increases. Second: video erosion became undeniable. Traditional bundle logic assumed video was the anchor; streaming proved video was commodity. Charter's disclosure that it is managing 'connectivity-first' customer value (Q1 2026 framing) instead of video-plus-broadband proves the operator has accepted video is non-strategic. That acceptance freed operators to explicitly price broadband as essential utility rather than bundle component. Q2 2026 showed the logic: lose Internet customers to broadband commoditization, offset through mobile bundling, maintain household revenue through price increases on connectivity that customers cannot escape.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Households and small businesses dependent on fixed-line broadband for connectivity. In most areas, they have at most one or two realistic options. When operator raises broadband prices 5-8% annually (Charter's broadband pricing power is captured in EBITDA maintenance despite customer erosion), customers pay it because alternative is lost productivity, education access, or connectivity. Burden falls most heavily on price-sensitive households without wireless alternatives or fiber options. Workers and communities losing video jobs as operators harvest rather than reinvest.
Who profits
Charter and Comcast and their shareholders. Both operators use captive broadband pricing to maintain household economics as video erodes. Charter's 41.4% FY2025 EBITDA margin, free cash flow $5.004B, and Q2 2026 buybacks prove the extract. Broadband gross margins (after network operating costs) remain very high—50%+ in operator filings—and infrastructure has mostly been built and is being milked rather than expanded. Liberty Broadband's Charter stake multiplies profit by layering governance and tax benefits on underlying household captivity.

The tension

The infrastructure monopoly is fragile in two ways becoming visible in 2026. First: fiber-overbuilds are expanding in selected markets (Verizon's Frontier acquisition added fiber-access alternatives). This will erode Charter's and Comcast's duopoly in those geographies where it occurs. Second: regulatory backlash against broadband pricing is mounting. FTC and state attorneys general opened broadband-pricing proceedings in 2025-2026. If regulators mandate price ceilings or require facility-sharing, operator ability to extract escalating value from household captivity will collapse. The external pressure is consumer expectation that connectivity is essential service, creating political and social willingness to regulate captivity away.

The surprising part

The most non-obvious insight is that the infrastructure monopoly does not exist at national or regional level—it exists at neighborhood or census-tract level. A customer cannot switch from Charter to Comcast. They can only switch between broadband and not having broadband. This hyperlocal monopoly is invisible to national competitive analysis but decisive for pricing power. Also surprising: neither Charter nor Comcast is using captive pricing to invest heavily in next-generation network. Charter's capital expenditures were $11.659B in FY2025 against $54.774B revenue (21.3% of revenue). That level of capex maintains existing infrastructure but does not build next-generation capability. The operators clearly believe the monopoly will remain durable for 5-10 years but not so durable that aggressive reinvestment is justified. That is the bet underlying the harvest strategy: we can extract cash now because customers have nowhere to go, but we cannot bet on this lasting forever.

Why it matters

If fixed-line broadband pricing is determined by local monopoly power rather than competition, broadband becomes regressive tax on households that cannot afford wireless alternatives or fiber overbuilds. Discourages investment in next-generation networks because operator can harvest cash from existing infrastructure longer than innovation would merit. Long-term risk is that broadband pricing becomes politically untenable, leading to regulation or utility classification that fundamentally alters cash-harvest model. Some households eventually will have wireless or fiber alternatives; others will not. Those without alternatives will either pay escalating prices or demand public intervention.

The household that needs broadband has no choice but the local cable operator, which means that operator can raise prices regardless of competitive or technological change, converting the captive relationship into reliable cash extraction.
