The Fee That Never Stops Growing: Premium Networks and Captive Cardholders

Once a customer is locked into a network—whether it is AmEx's closed loop, Synchrony's merchant relationships, Marriott's loyalty system, or VeriSign's domain registry—the operator can raise fees steadily because switching costs are real and the benefit seems sticky enough to prevent churn.

Platform economics creates a different kind of captivity than traditional markets. American Express has delivered 30 consecutive quarters of double-digit card fee growth by raising fees on premium products where the customer relationship is tightly bound to travel, dining, and status benefits. Synchrony, Marriott, and VeriSign show variations on the same pattern: once embedded in a network, customers resist switching even as fees rise, and the operator captures the rents by steadily extracting value rather than competing on price. The fee is not for a better product; it is for the fact of being embedded.

The mechanism

Platform fees depend on network effects and switching costs, not on intrinsic product superiority. A credit card cardholder does not choose American Express because the card itself is better than Visa; they choose it because the closed-loop network guarantees premium merchant partnerships, exclusive dining programs, and high-touch customer service. Leaving the network means losing not just the card, but access to all those embedded benefits. American Express exploited this by building premium-tier products where the annual fee was not just a price but a signal of membership in an exclusive ecosystem. It then raised those fees consistently—30 consecutive quarters of double-digit growth—because the cost of leaving outweighed the savings. Similar dynamics apply across Synchrony's merchant partnerships (where a customer financed at a retailer faces friction switching to a generic bank card), Marriott's loyalty structure (where status thresholds and point accumulation create path dependence), and VeriSign's domain registry (where switching the .com nameserver would break email, website routing, and SSL certificates). In each case, the fee is sticky not because the customer loves the fee but because the customer is locked into the system.

The economics differ from traditional service markets in a critical way. In competitive markets, fee increases trigger customer defection because switching costs are low. A checking-account fee increase drives customers to another bank. A domain registration fee increase prompts a move to a different registrar. But platforms operate differently. American Express has built such a tight relationship between card membership, merchant access, and lifestyle positioning that fee increases become bundled with status signaling. Raising the Platinum card fee from $695 to $800 is not framed as a price increase; it is framed as adding new benefits (higher credit limits, new perks, exclusive experiences) that make the fee seem justified. Synchrony does not charge higher merchant-financing rates because its underwriting is better; it charges them because a retailer customer embedded in the system (with POS integration, customer data, dynamic decisioning) faces switching costs if they want to move to a competitor. Marriott keeps raising co-branded card fees by negotiating better terms with JPMorgan Chase and American Express because the loyalty-program members will absorb the fee rather than lose their point balances and elite status. VeriSign raised its .com wholesale registry fee from $10.26 to $10.97 in 2026 with minimal controversy because registrars and domain holders have no real alternative to the DNS system itself. In all cases, the captivity is real and the fee-raising power is durable.

The cash extraction is permanent and accelerating because the platforms have learned how to layer fees without triggering visible churn. American Express does not report what percentage of cardholders drop premium tiers after fee increases because the decline is minimal; it is instead absorbed into the fact that 70% of new accounts are on fee-paying products. Synchrony reports 15.08% net interest margins and $102.2B in receivables without disclosing customer defection to competitors because the merchant partnership structure is sticky. Marriott collects $60M in quarterly co-branded credit-card fees (a new revenue stream entirely separate from room nights) because elite members have too much status accumulated to leave. VeriSign can announce a registry-fee increase with confidence in the annual report because there is no alternative for operators of .com domains. The result is that fee inflation becomes a permanent operating strategy rather than a temporary value-capture. The company extracts 20-30% annual fee increases through product refresh (AmEx Platinum refresh), margin expansion (Synchrony net interest margin up 30bp YoY), or base-fee elevation (VeriSign registry wholesale increase), and the consumer simply absorbs it as the cost of remaining inside the network.

Why now

Platform fee-harvesting accelerated after the 2022-2023 rate-regime inflection forced institutional capital to retreat from low-risk fixed income. When the Fed raised rates, Treasury yields moved from 0-2% to 4-5%, but that didn't solve the problem for customers already embedded in platform ecosystems—they still had to pay AmEx fees, still had to use Synchrony financing at retailers, still needed Marriott points to travel. The rate regime shift did not create alternatives; it locked customers in place because the cost of switching platforms exceeded the cost of absorbing higher fees. American Express explicitly capitalized on this by making 70% of new 2025 accounts fee-paying products. Synchrony's 15.08% net interest margin (up 30bp YoY) shows the company charging higher rates into an environment where customers cannot easily shop for alternatives. Marriott sanctioned co-branded card fee increases specifically because members saw value in the loyalty system even as fees rose. VeriSign's 68% operating margin and the confidence to announce 2026 fee increases (from $10.26 to $10.97) show domain operators operating from a position of certainty. Across all four, the key shift is that fee-raising is no longer apologized for or justified by cost increases—it is celebrated as a business model. This marks the transition from fee-harvesting as opportunistic to fee-harvesting as deliberate corporate strategy.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
The cardholders, depositors, merchants, and domain registrants who are locked into the networks. An American Express Platinum cardholder paying $800 annually has fewer choices than a checking-account holder—leaving AmEx means losing dining credits, travel insurance, airport lounge access, and status. A furniture retailer using Synchrony financing has embedded the tool in their POS system and trained their sales floor to offer it; switching to a bank lender would require retraining and system changes. A Marriott Elite member with 50,000 accumulated points and elite status has real switching costs to another hotel chain. A domain registrant dependent on .com has no realistic alternative for core DNS infrastructure. All four operator groups are charging fees explicitly because their customers have nowhere else to go without real tangible loss—loss of benefits, loss of efficiency, or loss of essential infrastructure access. The consumer or merchant does not necessarily pay in a rate increase; they pay by losing the option to leave.
Who profits
The platform operators capture the rents directly. American Express' 2025 revenue of $72.2B with $10.8B in net income and $15.38 EPS shows the model at scale. Synchrony's $102.2B in receivables with 15.08% net interest margin and $3.6B in annual net earnings means the company is monetizing captivity. Marriott's ability to add $60M in co-branded credit-card fee revenue alongside core room operations shows the diversification of fees. VeriSign's $1.6566B in revenue with $1.121B in operating income (68% margin) means the registry operator is pure cash harvesting. These are not competitive businesses growing by offering better products or lower prices. They are cash-extraction machines growing by raising fees inside protected networks. Shareholders benefit from the steady march of margin expansion, buyback programs, and dividend increases. American Express raised full-year guidance in mid-2026 despite no macroeconomic tailwinds because card fees are uncorrelated to external conditions. This is how cash harvest disguises itself as growth.

The tension

The model eventually breaks when either the switching cost becomes visible to regulators or when a new technology makes the old moat obsolete. American Express' closed-loop premium positioning could be disrupted by fintech alternatives offering status signaling at lower cost (we have not seen this yet). Synchrony's merchant-finance model could be disrupted if fintech platforms embed more directly into POS systems or if BNPL becomes standardized to the point of being commodity. Marriott's loyalty dependence could be disrupted if a new hotel operator builds a larger network and seduces members with better point earning or redemption. VeriSign faces the most dramatic tension: if AI systems start navigating directly to services without domains, or if new TLDs proliferate and erode .com's primacy, the registry fee power diminishes. A second, more immediate tension: if regulators start examining platform fee practices as a cost-of-living issue, the political risk to steady fee increases rises. American Express' premium posture gives it some regulatory cover (the fees are voluntary), but Synchrony's merchant-financing model and VeriSign's infrastructure role make them more vulnerable to scrutiny. The final tension is that fees, once raised, are sticky downward. If any of these platforms face real competition, they cannot easily lower fees without signaling weakness.

The surprising part

The surprise is not that platforms raise fees; it is how little resistance they encounter and how explicitly they frame fee raising as growth strategy. Most consumer-facing companies hide price increases or justify them with product improvements. American Express celebrates 30 consecutive quarters of double-digit card-fee growth as a competitive achievement in its annual report. Synchrony does not describe its business as high-interest lending; it describes it as platform monetization of merchant relationships. Marriott quantifies co-branded credit-card fees as a separate revenue line growing independently of room nights. VeriSign announces registry-fee increases in SEC filings with confidence because there is no alternative. These operators have moved past the pretense that fees are justified by cost increases or product enhancement. They are openly admitting that they are harvesting rents from captive customers and the market rewards them for it. This transparency is a signal that the platforms believe the networks are durable enough to withstand customer resistance or regulatory scrutiny.

Why it matters

Platform fee inflation represents a shift in how costs are distributed. Consumers and merchants pay fees not for better service, but for the unavoidable cost of staying inside an essential network. This drives up the cost of participating in modern commerce without creating new value; it is pure redistribution. American Express customers absorb higher card fees because the ecosystem is valuable enough. Synchrony's merchants accept merchant-finance fees because the POS integration drives conversions. Marriott members stay loyal despite steadily rising co-branded card fees because the status and points are accumulated over years. DNS users pay higher registry fees because there is no alternative. For society, this matters because it shows how essential digital and financial networks have become monopolistic rent-extraction machines. The fee is not competitive; it is compulsory. That compounds cost-of-living pressure for anyone embedded in these networks, and it raises questions about whether the platforms should be regulated as utilities rather than permitted to extract rents freely.

Platform operators now openly harvest fees from locked-in networks—American Express through premium cards, Synchrony through merchant relationships, Marriott through loyalty status, VeriSign through DNS infrastructure—because switching costs are high enough to make price competition irrelevant.
