The Capex Debt: What Buyback-Over-Investment Leaves Behind

Companies that choose to return capital to shareholders instead of reinvesting in productive capacity may appear virtuous in the short term, but they're creating infrastructure deficits that will constrain supply, pricing power, and competitive resilience when cycles turn.

The financial discipline that looks like optimization—minimizing capex, maximizing shareholder returns—creates a compounding infrastructure debt. ExxonMobil distributed $37.2B to shareholders while investing $29.0B in capex in 2025; ConocoPhillips disposed of $5B in assets specifically to fund buybacks; Planet Fitness raised prices 50% after finding that membership growth softened. When commodity cycles turn, infrastructure shortages emerge, or new demand surfaces, the supply base may lack the capacity to respond because years of buyback-over-capex choices eroded the productive foundation.

The mechanism

The buyback-over-capex choice appears rational at each decision point because capital returns are tangible, immediate, and benefit current shareholders, while productive reinvestment is uncertain, remote, and benefits future shareholders (or future management teams). ExxonMobil distributed $37.2B in 2025 while operating cash flow reached $52.0B and capex was $29.0B—technically sustainable because distributions came from operations, not from borrowing. But the math reveals the trade-off: if the company had maintained capex at its historical 35-40% of operating cash flow and allowed distributions to flow from the remainder, it would be investing substantially more in production capacity, refining throughput, LNG infrastructure, and cost-reduction technologies. Instead, the company chose to hold capex flat while sending excess cash to shareholders. Over 5-10 years, this discipline produces returns for investors but leaves production capacity, refining yield, and cost position vulnerable to supply disruptions, maintenance backlogs, or competitive pressure.

ConocoPhillips's $5.0B asset-disposition program specifically to fund buybacks illustrates the same pattern in sharper form. Disposing of producing assets to return capital rather than reinvesting the proceeds is economically negative in most frameworks because it shrinks the productive base while depleting cash. But under current capital-allocation practice, reducing scale to increase per-share returns is treated as optimization. The company distributed $9.0B while investing $12.6B in capex in 2025—a more balanced profile than ExxonMobil's, but still characterized by a willingness to reduce productive assets rather than commit new capital to growth. The strategic tension emerges when a new supply deficit emerges or a competitor (perhaps not bound by the same buyback discipline) invests more aggressively in production. ConocoPhillips may find itself squeezed on upside optionality, production response time, and cost position.

Planet Fitness's pricing discipline offers a consumer-side parallel. The company raised its new-member entry price by 50% and found that the increase was 'durable'—the 2025 data showed 1.1M net new members despite the price hike. But management explicitly paused a planned second increase on the Black Card when 'same-club growth softened' and said it was testing 'pricing, member experience, and retention.' This reveals the capacity constraint. The company optimized for shareholder returns by raising prices, and the demand curve was flat enough that it worked—for a while. But there is clearly a ceiling. If competition intensifies, if churn accelerates at higher price points, or if the market shifts toward equipment or digital offerings that don't require a club membership, Planet Fitness's pricing model hits a wall. The company has been capturing cash flow from existing members rather than investing in new locations, modernized equipment, or competitive product features. When that works, it works. When it doesn't, the company lacks the productive capacity (newer clubs, better equipment, differentiated experience) to respond.

Why now

The buyback-over-capex model became dominant in 2010-2020 as interest rates remained low, capital was abundant, and equity markets rewarded companies for growing per-share earnings through buybacks rather than organic growth. For energy companies, low crude prices in 2015-2017 made organic growth investments uneconomical, so returning cash made sense. For consumer companies, private-equity ownership and activist pressure normalized the idea that extracting cash from existing operations (rather than reinvesting) was the correct management priority. In 2024-2026, with energy supply tight again and capital costs rising, the trade-off is becoming visible. Companies that minimized capex are now facing higher maintenance costs, production bottlenecks, and less flexibility to pursue new opportunities. But the damage is already sunk. A company cannot quickly rebuild the productive capacity that was traded away for buyback economics over a decade.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Consumers and workers pay when supply constraints emerge. If ExxonMobil's underinvestment in refining leaves gasoline supply tight, retail prices rise. If ConocoPhillips disposed of too much productive capacity, energy prices may stay elevated longer. If Planet Fitness underinvested in newer club experience and equipment, members experience churn to competitors or digital alternatives. Workers in these businesses also pay because underinvestment often goes hand-in-hand with hiring freezes, wage discipline, and workforce reduction—the company is extracting cash from existing operations rather than building new capacity that would require new hires. Customers in downstream supply chains pay as input costs remain sticky (oil and gas prices) or as substitutes become necessary (Planet Fitness members switching to home equipment or Peloton).
Who profits
Shareholders of the current generation, especially those who sold after the stock appreciated from buyback-driven per-share earnings growth. Activist investors and private-equity sponsors profit by orchestrating the buyback, collecting the dividend, and then exiting before the capacity deficit becomes visible. Management teams profit through stock options vesting as stock price rises. But future shareholders (and the company itself) absorb the cost when reinvestment becomes urgent and capex must suddenly spike, or when competitive pressure mounts because the company lack modern assets.

The tension

The debt is invisible until it isn't. For several years, buyback-over-capex works because the existing asset base still generates cash flow, the stock price rises, and analysts and boards celebrate the 'capital discipline.' But at some point, equipment ages out, maintenance costs rise, production declines, or competitive pressure mounts. Then the company must choose between a painful capex surge (which crushes cash flow and per-share earnings) or continued underinvestment (which loses market share). There is no free solution. The years of buyback discipline created a real economic deficit that must eventually be addressed. The risk is that by the time the deficit is visible, it's too late. The company either squanders cash on emergency capex that generates poor returns, or it slides into slower growth or market-share loss because it built the wrong productive base.

The surprising part

The buyback discipline persists even when management knows better. ExxonMobil executives have lived through multiple energy cycles and surely understand that infrastructure underinvestment is risky. ConocoPhillips management is sophisticated about capital allocation. Yet both companies are choosing to distribute capital rather than reinvest. This suggests that the drivers are not ignorance but rather external pressure: activist investors, private-equity owners, or shareholder-return expectations that override long-term strategic thinking. The surprising part is not the existence of the tension. It's how openly companies are trading long-term productive capacity for short-term shareholder returns, and how little scrutiny that trade-off receives from boards or regulators.

Why it matters

The buyback-over-capex model is self-correcting only through economic pain. If energy companies underinvest and production declines, energy prices rise, potentially fueling inflation or constraining growth elsewhere in the economy. If consumer-service companies extract cash from existing operations and fall behind on capital refresh, they lose competitive position or raise prices to extract more from existing customers. The societal cost is that productive capacity does not expand as fast as demand might require, competitive dynamics may stall (because the incumbent underinvests rather than a new entrant builds better alternatives), and workers in these industries may face hiring freezes or wage pressure even as labor is genuinely scarce. The macro effect is slower productivity growth and higher pricing power for entrenched incumbents—exactly the opposite of what a healthy capital-allocation system should produce.

Years of choosing shareholder returns over reinvestment leave companies with aging assets, maintenance backlogs, and reduced capacity to respond when the next cycle turns.
