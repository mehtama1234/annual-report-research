The Commodity Whiplash Transfer

When nitrogen prices spike or cocoa costs surge, integrated giants no longer absorb the pain — they pass it downstream through contracts engineered to shift risk onto the next tier. The farmer, the packager, the small contractor, and the household bear the hit so CF Industries, Glencore, and Freeport-McMoRan do not have to.

The modern commodity system runs on asymmetric contract power. Large producers with hedging infrastructure and pricing leverage — CF Industries, Glencore, Freeport-McMoRan — have rebuilt their commercial structures around pass-through clauses, fixed-price exposure for counterparties, and marketing arms that profit from the same supply disruptions that hurt non-hedged buyers. When commodity prices spike, the pain travels downstream to whoever signed the wrong contract or lacks the scale to hedge. When prices fall, fixed-contract holders like Cleveland-Cliffs absorb the lag while their automotive customers collect the benefit. The transfer is not random; it is designed, enforced in each contract cycle, and increasingly institutionalized.

The mechanism

The transfer runs through contract-power asymmetry at each tier of the supply chain. CF Industries sits at the top of the nitrogen chain. It produced 10.1 million tons of gross ammonia in 2025 at full utilization and collected $1.46 billion in net earnings because global nitrogen supply was tight and Iran-related disruption kept it that way. The farmers buying CF's urea and UAN have no equivalent hedging infrastructure. They adjust acreage, accept higher per-acre input costs, or reduce yield. The cost lands with them.

One tier down, the packager and converter layer operates under pass-through contracts that insulate them partially — but not fully. Crown Holdings posted a best-ever $2.092 billion adjusted EBITDA in 2025 because its aluminum and steel supply contracts include pass-through clauses that push metal-cost moves to beverage brands. The brands push those costs to shelf prices. Mondelez did exactly that: 2025 organic net revenue grew 4.3%, but volume/mix fell 3.7%, meaning the growth was entirely price. The consumer absorbed higher snack prices and bought fewer units.

Fixed-price contracts create the sharpest whiplash for industrial buyers without pricing power. Cleveland-Cliffs locks 35–40% of flat-rolled steel shipments under fixed annual automotive contracts. When material costs spiked in 2025, Cliffs could not pass the increase through until contracts rolled over. The result: a $1.4 billion GAAP net loss on $18.6 billion in revenues, with adjusted EBITDA of only $37 million. Meanwhile, the automakers holding fixed-price contracts received stable input costs while Cliffs bore the full margin compression. The asymmetry is not accidental — it is the negotiated outcome of contract cycles where large OEM buyers have more leverage than a single steel producer.

Glencore's marketing business is the structural answer to this dynamic. Rather than absorb commodity volatility as a producer, Glencore built a marketing engine that profits from the same disruptions that hurt non-hedged buyers. H1 2026 Marketing Adjusted EBIT jumped 142% to $3.3 billion because Middle East conflict created freight premiums, energy-product spread opportunities, and commodity dislocation that Glencore's physical assets, inventory positions, and logistics network could monetize. The DRC cobalt-quota regime is another example: Glencore shifted production priority toward copper when cobalt economics deteriorated, cutting cobalt output 46% in H1 2026 while copper rose 15%. That kind of production pivot requires global infrastructure most buyers cannot replicate.

The result is a layered transfer system with predictable losers at each level. At the upstream end, integrated producers with low breakeven costs capture the full benefit of supply tightness. In the middle, converters like Crown Holdings pass costs through via contract but face their own sourcing and freight pressures when global supply chains dislocate. At the downstream end, consumer-brand operators like Mondelez can push price increases to the shelf, but only until volume declines signal consumer saturation. The final absorber — the farmer, the household, the small contractor without hedging — has no further tier to pass the cost to.

Why now

Two specific inflection points make this transfer more visible and more institutionalized than before 2022. First, the post-2022 interest-rate environment forced companies to manage working capital and commodity exposure more deliberately. When borrowing costs rose above 5%, carrying unhedged commodity inventory became genuinely expensive. Large operators with treasury departments and hedging programs gained a structural advantage over smaller buyers who could not afford the same risk-management infrastructure. Second, the supply-chain crises of 2021–2022 showed every major commodity producer exactly which contracts left them exposed and which protected them. By 2024–2025, those contracts had been renegotiated with explicit pass-through clauses and pricing-lag protections installed on the side with the most bargaining power. CF Industries' Q1 2026 language about Iran-related disruption exposing fertilizer supply fragility is not a description of a new shock — it is a description of a structural scarcity that management is explicitly managing and pricing around. Glencore's H1 2026 marketing results show the same: the marketing business did not accidentally earn $3.3 billion from Middle East conflict benefits; it was positioned to do so because management explicitly built the trading book and logistics infrastructure to monetize disruption. These are institutional choices made between 2022 and 2024 that are now generating the documented results in company filings.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Farmers buying nitrogen fertilizer: when CF Industries earns $1.46 billion on tight nitrogen markets, the cost per acre of fertilizer rises and farmers absorb it directly, adjusting planting decisions and yields. Consumers buying packaged snacks: Mondelez's 2025 volume/mix fell 3.7% against 4.3% organic revenue growth, meaning consumers paid more and bought less. Households at the end of the food chain have no hedging option. Cleveland-Cliffs and the automotive supply tier: Cliffs is a counterintuitive example because it is a large manufacturer — yet it is the one absorbing the commodity whiplash that its auto OEM customers avoided through fixed-price annual contracts. When material costs spike, a company with $18.6 billion in revenue and a $1.4 billion net loss is still structurally the downstream absorber relative to the OEMs it supplies. Small and mid-size industrial buyers: any manufacturer buying aluminum, steel, or chemical inputs on spot or short-term contracts faces the same compression without the working-capital depth or contract leverage to defer it.
Who profits
Integrated commodity producers with low breakeven costs: Freeport-McMoRan Q2 2026 unit net cash cost of $1.97 per pound against realized copper prices of $6.17 per pound is the clearest single data point. That $4.20 per pound spread — on 710 million pounds of copper sales in a single quarter — shows what the upstream position earns when commodity markets are tight. CF Industries earns on the same principle in nitrogen: high utilization, low gas costs relative to benchmark nitrogen prices, and captive North American supply create durable margin. Commodity traders with physical infrastructure: Glencore's marketing arm earned $3.3 billion of EBIT in H1 2026 specifically because it holds the physical assets, logistics contracts, and inventory positions to profit from the disruptions that hurt non-hedged buyers. Converters with pass-through contracts: Crown Holdings protects its own margin by passing aluminum and steel cost moves through to beverage brands. Its best-ever $2.092 billion EBITDA in 2025 reflects a business model that insulates converter economics from the same cost swings that hit its customers.

The tension

Consumer volume is the circuit breaker. Mondelez's 2025 results show the limit precisely: organic net revenue grew 4.3%, but volume/mix fell 3.7%. The revenue growth was not demand growth; it was price passed through until enough consumers bought fewer Oreos and Cadbury bars to register in the volume line. Mondelez entered 2026 focused on restoring volume rather than depending on further price carry, which is a direct admission that the pass-through chain hit its ceiling. When volumes fall far enough, margin pressure reverses direction: consumer brands are forced to trade on price to recover units, packagers face lower throughput, and upstream producers lose the demand anchor that supported their pricing. The transfer mechanism is not permanent; it runs until affordability fails at the household level, and then the compression travels back up the chain. The operators with the least flexibility — those with fixed assets, high fixed costs, and no pricing buffer — absorb the reversal first.

The surprising part

The most counterintuitive data point is Cleveland-Cliffs. A company with $18.6 billion in revenues and roughly 25,000 employees across North America is, in this system, the downstream absorber — not the upstream profiteer. Cleveland-Cliffs lost $1.4 billion in 2025 not because it lacked volume or customers, but because its fixed-price annual automotive contracts locked it into receiving prices that did not move when material costs did. The auto OEMs holding those same contracts collected the stability benefit. That inversion — where a vertically integrated steel manufacturer is structurally positioned as the commodity-risk absorber relative to its own customers — shows that size alone does not determine which side of the transfer you end up on. Contract structure, customer leverage, and the direction of pricing power in each bilateral relationship determine who absorbs the whiplash. Glencore's 142% jump in marketing EBIT from the same market disruptions that hurt Cliffs is the other half of the same truth: the companies that designed their commercial structures to monetize volatility rather than just produce through it are in a categorically different position.

Why it matters

The pattern documented in these company filings has a direct household consequence. The farmer who cannot hedge nitrogen costs, the consumer who pays more for snacks while buying fewer, the small manufacturer buying steel on spot — each is absorbing price volatility that the counterparty on the other side of a contract has specifically engineered away. That is not a market failure in the conventional sense; it is the intended outcome of contract structures built by parties with unequal access to financial infrastructure. The disparity is widening, not narrowing. Post-2022 interest rates made hedging more valuable and more expensive, which means the advantage of having hedging infrastructure grew precisely when access to it became more unequal. The companies with the most sophisticated financial operations — CF Industries, Glencore, Freeport-McMoRan — end 2025 with strong earnings and strong cash returns to shareholders. The households and small operators at the end of those supply chains end 2025 with higher input costs, compressed margins, and no equivalent mechanism for relief.

Commodity price swings no longer land on the balance sheets of integrated producers — contract engineering, hedging infrastructure, and pricing-power asymmetry have systematically routed the risk to the tier with the least ability to push it further downstream.
