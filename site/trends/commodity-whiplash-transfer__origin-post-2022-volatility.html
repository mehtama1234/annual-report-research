Trends · The Commodity Whiplash Transfer

Why Commodity Pain Stopped Being Shared

Pre-2022, integrated supply chains absorbed commodity volatility through contracts and inventory buffers. Post-2022 geopolitical fragmentation shattered that model, turning pass-through to the next link the new default operating posture.

A structural shift occurred between 2021 and 2022. Iran fertilizer sanctions, DRC cobalt quotas, and Middle East energy disruptions broke the inventory buffers and long-term contract structures that had previously let commodity cost swings be absorbed across integrated supply chains. Now each link passes the pain forward rather than absorbing it — and the final recipient is always the consumer or smallest producer who cannot hedge.

The mechanism

Before 2022, commodity companies operated within a post-Cold War consensus: integrated supply chains with long-term contracts, strategic reserves, and geographic diversification could absorb price swings and supply shocks. A fertilizer producer like CF Industries would sign multi-year ammonia contracts with ammonia users, build inventory buffers against feedstock disruption, and manage through cycles. A miner like Glencore would use its dual industrial-and-marketing model to smooth volatility, holding inventory and trading positions when supply tightened. A processor like Mosaic would commit to stable raw-material sourcing (phosphate rock, ammonia) and pass those costs on gradually to customers.

The 2022-2024 period shattered that contract. Iran's removal from global fertilizer markets after sanctions tightened cut a material source of phosphate and potash globally, forcing importers to scramble for alternatives. DRC's cobalt quota regime (prioritizing cobalt extraction over production in some cases) created artificial scarcity that forced miners to choose between commodities and ration production. Middle East energy disruptions spiked natural-gas and crude costs, raising production costs across petrochemicals, fertilizers, and refined products. These were not temporary disruptions; they were structural breaks in the geographic and policy framework that had managed commodity flows.

The response was immediate pass-through. CF Industries' pricing power shifted sharply: instead of trying to stabilize customer relationships through price smoothing, the company passed volatile ammonia and nitrogen costs directly to customers and used strong cash generation to buy back stock and raise dividends. Glencore's marketing business (adjusted EBIT of $2.9B in 2025) grew increasingly profitable precisely because supply disruption created trading opportunities — the company could monetize scarcity rather than manage through it. Mosaic's experience was the harshest: sulfur became a binding constraint, with contract prices swinging from $440/ton to $705/ton across quarters, and the company had no choice but to pass those costs forward, curtail production when margins turned negative, and accept inventory and working-capital swings that overwhelmed reported profitability. By H1 2026, Glencore's cobalt production had fallen 46% due to DRC prioritization, not because demand collapsed, but because the quota forced producers to hold inventory and sell at spot rather than under contract.

Why now

The geopolitical fragmentation is structural, not cyclical. Cold War ended with a period of relative stability in commodity supply chains: Middle East oil flowed consistently, Africa's mines exported freely, Russia's energy was integrated into Europe, and long-term contracting became possible because the political risk seemed manageable. That era ended between 2021 and 2023. Iran sanctions broke fertilizer flows, Russian energy was weaponized, DRC implemented quota regimes, and China's rare-earth policies tightened access. The inventory buffers that used to smooth these shocks were drained during COVID; long-term contracts were renegotiated at much higher prices; and each participant learned that holding inventory or absorbing a cost spike was economically worse than passing it forward immediately.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
The final consumer and the smallest operator in every supply chain. A farmer buying nitrogen fertilizer now faces CF's full pass-through of ammonia costs plus the margin it demands. A battery maker buying cobalt faces Glencore's spot pricing instead of the contract rates that used to exist. A plastic manufacturer buying polymers from a company like LyondellBasell faces Middle East energy shocks, petrochemical spread volatility, and raw-material pass-through without any contractual cushion. For farmers and small businesses without hedging capability, this means earnings volatility, margin compression, and inability to invest in growth. For consumers, it means food prices rise faster when fertilizer costs spike because there is no buffer in the supply chain to absorb the swing.
Who profits
The commodity producers and traders positioned closest to the source of scarcity. CF Industries captured $1.46B of net income in 2025 despite a constrained global ammonia market because it owns production assets and can pass through all costs instantly. Glencore's marketing business made $2.9B in adjusted EBIT in 2025 precisely because supply disruption creates profit opportunities for traders who can move inventory, optimize routing, and capture the spread between regional prices. LyondellBasell benefits from its North American feedstock advantage when Middle East energy costs rise — it can produce cheaper than European competitors and capture the spread. The ultimate winner is whoever sits at the chokepoint: a cobalt producer under DRC quota, a natural-gas producer feeding ammonia plants, a broker matching sulfur buyers to available inventory.

The tension

This model breaks if geopolitical risk reverses or if demand destruction forces a reset. If Iran comes back to global trade, phosphate supplies surge and prices collapse, invalidating the pass-through-immediately strategy. If cobalt demand falls faster than DRC's quota regime persists, miners are left with inventory and declining prices. If energy prices fall sharply due to LNG supply surge or energy transition, petrochemical margins compress and the trading profit evaporates. The deeper tension is customer push-back: if enough farmers, battery makers, or end-users integrate backward or find substitutes, they break the pricing power that the pass-through model depends on. Lastly, if regulatory pressure builds around commodity-price volatility affecting food security or energy transitions, governments may impose price controls or long-term contracting requirements that reverse the shift back toward buffer-based pricing.

The surprising part

The truly non-obvious insight is that commodity-price volatility is now a feature of the business model, not a bug to be managed. CF Industries, Glencore, and other commodity producers are not trying to reduce price swings anymore; they are trying to capture the spread of that volatility. This is why Glencore explicitly defends its marketing business and calls it a profit engine during disruption. It is why CF uses strong cash generation to fund buybacks rather than build inventory buffers. They have learned that volatility is profitable for those with the balance sheet to absorb it, and that passing through costs immediately locks in those profits. The old model (absorb the shock, smooth the customer relationship, recover over time) is gone because the shocks are too frequent and the customers have too little patience.

Why it matters

This shift cascades into food prices, energy costs, and industrial investment. When fertilizer costs spike and are passed through immediately, food prices rise faster and farmers cannot absorb the shock. When energy costs spike and are passed through immediately, petrochemical and manufacturing costs rise, rippling into packaging, mobility, and construction. The buffer that used to exist — allowing time for adjustment, contracting, and pass-through — is gone. Supply shocks now hit end consumers and small producers within quarters rather than years, creating more turbulent demand and higher volatility in sectors that feed real people.

Post-2022 geopolitical fragmentation broke the long-term contract and inventory-buffer model, turning commodity companies from shock absorbers into shock transmitters that pass all pain forward.
