The Contract-Lag Trap

Fixed-price annual steel deals turn mills into shock absorbers: they eat the loss for a full cycle when input costs move against them, and miss the upside when spot prices spike.

A steel mill that signs a year of fixed-price contracts is betting the market will not move against it before renegotiation. When scrap and ore stay cheap that bet prints money, but when demand and pricing turn while contracts are locked, a high-utilization mill can still post a net loss because it cannot reprice mid-cycle. The mills that thrive are the ones whose contracts happen to lag on the way up, not on the way down.

The mechanism

A mill signs annual fixed-price contracts covering a large slice of volume. Cleveland-Cliffs sells roughly 35% to 40% of flat-rolled shipments this way; Nucor sells about 85% of its sheet to contract customers. Once inked, those prices do not move until the next negotiation, so realized economics lag the spot market by a full contract cycle in either direction.

When the market falls, the mill keeps shipping at contract prices set higher, which briefly protects it, but the more damaging case is when contract prices were set low and stay locked while demand weakens elsewhere. Cleveland-Cliffs shows the worst version: full-year 2025 revenue of $18.6 billion produced a $1.4 billion GAAP net loss and just $37 million of adjusted EBITDA. Management blamed weak automotive production, lagging fixed annual contract pricing, and the final year of a Cleveland-Cliffs slab contract that had become value-destructive.

The upside case is Steel Dynamics in 2026, where selling prices moved faster than input costs. In Q2 2026 its average external steel selling price rose $105 per ton to $1,298 while average ferrous scrap cost rose only $7 to $404, widening the metal spread; in Q1 2026 the price rose $86 to $1,193 while scrap rose $22 to $396. When the price leg leads and the cost leg lags, margin expands.

Commercial Metals shows the same spread mechanics on the construction side: in Q3 2026 its North America Steel Group metal margins improved $111 per ton year over year, as average selling prices climbed $130 per ton while scrap rose only $19. But CMC's full-year 2025 net earnings were only $84.7 million, a reminder that the strong 2026 spread is a rebound off a subdued base, not steady-state profitability.

Nucor is the counter-design. About 85% contract-oriented sheet damps volatility relative to a pure spot mill, which cushions it in both directions. Its steel mills segment swung from $516 million of pre-tax earnings in Q4 2025 to $1.128 billion in Q1 2026 to $1.556 billion in Q2 2026, showing that even a heavily contracted book still rides the cycle once contracts reset.

Why now

Cleveland-Cliffs closed 2025 as the clearest proof of the trap: a Q4 2025 adjusted EBITDA loss of $21 million capped a full year of $18.6 billion in revenue, a $1.4 billion net loss, and only $37 million of adjusted EBITDA, with management naming lagging fixed annual contract pricing and an expiring value-destructive slab contract as the causes. In the same window, Steel Dynamics and Commercial Metals were on the favorable side of the same lag, with Steel Dynamics' Q2 2026 selling price up $105 per ton against a $7 scrap increase and CMC's Q3 2026 metal margin up $111 per ton year over year. Identical contract structures produced opposite outcomes within a single quarter's window depending on which way the lag pointed.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
The mill absorbs the loss when contracts are locked below where the market moves, as Cleveland-Cliffs did through 2025 while still running high volume. Its automotive and distributor customers, who negotiated the fixed prices, get certainty at the mill's expense in a down cycle and pay above spot in an up cycle.
Who profits
Mills whose contracts happen to lag on the way up capture the spread, as Steel Dynamics and Commercial Metals did in 2026 when selling prices outran scrap costs. Heavily contracted producers like Nucor trade away extremes for stability, profiting from lower volatility rather than from timing.

The tension

Fixed-price contracts are sold as a stabilizer, and for the customer they are, but they force the mill to hold the volatility the customer offloaded. A structure meant to reduce risk simply relocates it to the party least able to reprice mid-cycle.

The surprising part

A mill can run near full utilization, ship record physical tonnage, and still post a billion-dollar net loss purely because its prices were set a cycle ago. Cleveland-Cliffs did not fail for lack of volume in 2025; it lost $1.4 billion on $18.6 billion of revenue because its contract book, not its furnaces, was underwater.

Why it matters

The steel that goes into cars, bridges, data centers, and grid buildout is priced through contracts that decouple a mill's survival from its actual output, meaning strategically important domestic capacity can be pushed toward losses by contract timing rather than by any real drop in demand for what it makes.

Fixed-price steel contracts hand the customer certainty and hand the mill the volatility, so a fully utilized producer can lose a billion dollars simply because it locked its prices one cycle too early.
