The Converter's Squeeze: Aluminum, Steel, and the Middle Link

The can maker sits between the aluminum smelter and the beverage brand, and its entire art is the same one every link learns — make sure the cost keeps moving toward whoever can least push back.

Packaging converters like Ball and Crown do not absorb commodity swings; they engineer contracts that let aluminum and steel costs pass straight through to the brand owner, preserving their own conversion spread. Each link in the chain uses contractual protection to send the cost onward, and the final stop is always the consumer — the only party with no pass-through clause to invoke. The converter's genius is not efficiency but positioning: it has made itself the middle link that never has to hold the bag.

The mechanism

A packaging converter is a pure middle link: it buys aluminum ingot and steel coil, forms them into cans, and sells the cans to Coca-Cola, PepsiCo, and the brewers. The commodity is often the single largest input cost, and its price is volatile in ways no one in the chain can control. The converter's survival strategy is not to hedge that volatility away but to contract it away — Ball's filings describe explicit 'contractual pass-through mechanisms' under which the aluminum cost flows to the customer while Ball retains the conversion spread. The converter is compensated for the labor of turning metal into a can, not for the metal itself, so when metal doubles, the converter's dollar of profit per can is designed to be untouched. The cost simply travels up the invoice to the brand owner.

Crown executes the same architecture through scale and sourcing flexibility. Operating 179 plants across 39 countries with roughly 23,000 employees, Crown states it can 'pass through higher material costs' and uses global sourcing to route around the worst regional price spikes. The proof is in the stability of its margin: operating margin held in an 11-12.6% band straight through the commodity turbulence of recent years. A margin that does not move while input prices swing violently is the signature of a business that has contractually externalized the swing — Crown is not absorbing the whiplash, it is relaying it.

Silgan reveals what happens when the pass-through is only partial. Its FY2025 sales rose 6.5% to $6,483.2 million — but adjusted EPS fell about 3%. That divergence, revenue up while earnings slip, is the fingerprint of an incomplete pass-through: some cost got stuck at the converter rather than moving on. Silgan's answer is telling — it leans on its higher-margin Dispensing & Specialty Closures segment (11.9% EBIT margin) to offset the metal-container squeeze. When you cannot pass the cost forward cleanly in one product line, you subsidize it from another where you have more pricing power. The middle link protects itself by mixing.

Sonoco is the instructive exception that clarifies the rule. Its FY2025 net sales jumped 42% to $7.5 billion — but that surge was acquisition-driven (the Sonoco Metal Packaging EMEA deal added roughly $2.2 billion, lifting that segment's sales 92.5%), not commodity pass-through. Strip the acquisition out and the legacy Global Converted Paper Products segment actually declined 2.1% on unfavorable volume and mix. Sonoco's defense against the converter's squeeze is not a cleaner pass-through clause but format diversification — spreading across paper, metal, and plastics to hold a 16-18% EBITDA margin so that no single commodity's swing dominates. Two strategies, one goal: never be the link that has to eat the cost.

The layered mechanism, seen across all four, is that pass-through is never perfect and the imperfection always flows the same direction. Ball and Crown pass the most cleanly and hold their margins; Silgan passes imperfectly and backfills from a specialty segment; Sonoco hedges with format mix. But in every case the residual cost that cannot be contractually shifted upward is minimized at the converter and maximized at the next weaker link — the brand owner's private-label lines, and ultimately the shopper at the shelf, who has no clause to invoke and no segment to cross-subsidize from.

Why now

Aluminum and steel prices have been through a whipsaw cycle since 2022 — pandemic supply shocks, energy-driven smelter curtailments, tariff threats, and demand swings — that would have devastated the converters if they held the exposure. Instead their FY2025 filings show the opposite: Crown's operating margin sat placidly in an 11-12.6% band and Ball reported its pass-through mechanisms working as designed. The visibility of that stability during a period of extreme commodity volatility is exactly what exposes the mechanism — the converters stayed calm because, by contract, the volatility was never theirs to hold. Silgan's EPS slip is the tell that even the best-engineered pass-through leaks a little, and someone downstream catches the drips.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
The beverage and food brand owner catches the cost first — every aluminum spike lands on its can invoice via the pass-through clause. The brand then decides how much to move onto the shelf price, and the last party in the chain, the shopper reaching for a six-pack or a can of soup, pays the final markup with no contract of their own to shift it further. Small private-label and regional brands, with the least negotiating leverage against both the converter and the retailer, absorb the most.
Who profits
The converters, who have positioned themselves as the one link whose profit is structurally insulated from the commodity they handle. Ball and Crown collect a stable conversion spread regardless of where aluminum trades; Silgan and Sonoco protect the same spread through specialty mix and format diversification. They profit not by beating the commodity market but by contractually refusing to participate in it — earning a fee for conversion while the price risk flows past them in both directions.

The tension

The pass-through is a fair-weather contract. It works cleanly when brand owners have the volume and the shelf pricing power to relay the cost onward, but Silgan's EPS decline shows the clause leaks under stress, and if brand owners hit a consumer affordability wall — shoppers finally refusing the higher shelf price — the cost has nowhere left to travel and backs up into the chain. The converter's insulation depends on the last link, the consumer, remaining willing to pay; when that breaks, the volatility the converter thought it had exported comes home.

The surprising part

The converter that grew revenue the most — Sonoco, up 42% to $7.5 billion — is the one whose growth had almost nothing to do with the pass-through mechanism, and whose core legacy business actually shrank 2.1%. The headline number that looks like a converter winning the commodity game is really an acquisition masking an operating decline, a reminder that in this middle-link business the impressive top line and the durable margin are two entirely different stories.

Why it matters

The stability of a can maker's margin during a commodity storm is the shopper's higher grocery bill in disguise — every aluminum spike the converter contractually refuses to hold travels down the chain until it stops at the shelf price you pay. Understanding the converter's squeeze is understanding why food and beverage prices ratchet up during commodity shocks and rarely ratchet back down: the cost was engineered to flow toward the one party who cannot pass it on, and that party is you.

The can maker earns its money not by mastering the price of aluminum but by writing the contract that guarantees the price of aluminum is always someone else's problem.
