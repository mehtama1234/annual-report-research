The Converter's Squeeze: Aluminum, Steel, and the Middle Link

Ball kept its conversion margins flat at 15%+ through 2024-2026 aluminum volatility. Crown Holdings held operating margin in an 11-12.6% band. Silgan's adjusted EPS fell 3% while revenue rose 6.5%—a tell that not all pass-through mechanics work. These converters do not absorb commodity swings; they engineer contracts that push the cost to the next link. The final link is always the consumer.

Packaging converters survive commodity whiplash by contractually shifting price risk upstream to manufacturers and downstream to brand owners, not by hedging or absorbing volatility themselves. Their competitive advantage is not innovation but contract design and positioning as the one link whose margin is structurally protected. When cost cannot move forward fast enough, Silgan backfills from higher-margin segments. The residual cost that cannot be passed always lands on the consumer or the weakest link.

The mechanism

A packaging converter sits between the aluminum or steel producer and the brand owner, and operates as a pure pass-through vehicle for commodity cost. Ball's filings explicitly describe 'contractual pass-through mechanisms' where aluminum cost flows to the customer while Ball retains only the conversion spread. Crown, with 179 plants across 39 countries, states it can 'pass through higher material costs' through global sourcing and long-term contracts. The mechanism is transparent: when aluminum doubles, the converter's invoice to Coca-Cola or PepsiCo increases, but Ball's dollar of profit per can remains untouched by design. The converter is paid for labor and overhead, not for commodity exposure.

The second level is when pass-through is imperfect. Silgan FY2025 shows this clearly—revenue up 6.5% to $6,483.2M, but adjusted EPS down 3%. That divergence is the fingerprint of incomplete pass-through: some aluminum cost got stuck at Silgan rather than flowing to the brand owner. Silgan's response is telling—it backfills margin pressure from its Dispensing & Specialty Closures segment, which has 11.9% EBIT margin. When you cannot pass a cost cleanly in one product line, you subsidize it from another where you have pricing power. That strategy only works if you own multiple margin tiers.

Sonoco reveals the third strategy—format diversification. Its FY2025 sales jumped 42% to $7.5B, but that surge was acquisition-driven; the legacy Global Converted Paper Products segment declined 2.1% on unfavorable volume and mix. Sonoco's hedge against the converter's squeeze is not a cleaner pass-through clause but exposure to paper, metal, and plastics simultaneously, holding a 16-18% EBITDA margin so that no single commodity's swing dominates. The core insight is that pass-through is never perfect and imperfection always flows the same direction—from converter toward brand owner toward consumer.

The layered mechanism across all four converters reveals the same architecture. Ball and Crown pass cost the most cleanly and hold their margins; Silgan passes imperfectly and uses segment mix to hedge; Sonoco diversifies format. But in every case the residual cost that cannot be contractually shifted upward is minimized at the converter and maximized at the next weaker link—the brand's private-label lines, and ultimately the shopper at the shelf with no pass-through clause to invoke.

Why now

Aluminum and steel prices whipsawed sharply in 2022-2025 (pandemic supply shocks, energy-driven smelter curtailments, tariff threats, geopolitical disruption), creating stress test conditions for pass-through mechanics. The 2025 annual filings showed the mechanism working: Crown's operating margin sat placidly in 11-12.6% band and Ball reported pass-through mechanisms operating as designed, revealing that the converters never held the volatility—it traveled onward. Silgan's EPS slip proved even the best-engineered pass-through leaks, and someone downstream catches the drips. This is a snapshot story—the data shows the mechanism exists and how it operates during commodity volatility, not a multi-year trend. The 'why now' is that commodity turbulence from 2022-2026 provided the stress test that made the mechanism visible in filings rather than hidden in contract fine print.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Brand owners (Coca-Cola, PepsiCo, beverage and food companies) absorb pass-through costs first via higher can invoices. They then decide how much to move to shelf price. Small private-label and regional brands, with the least negotiating leverage against both converter and retailer, absorb the most. The final party is the shopper reaching for a six-pack or can of soup—the only party with no pass-through clause, paying the final markup with no contract to shift it further.
Who profits
The converters, who have positioned themselves as the one link whose profit is structurally insulated from commodity price. Ball and Crown collect stable conversion spreads regardless of where aluminum trades; Silgan and Sonoco protect the same spread through specialty mix and format diversification. They profit not by beating the commodity market but by contractually refusing to participate in it—earning a fee for conversion while the price risk flows past them in both directions.

The tension

The pass-through is only sustainable when brand owners have the volume and shelf pricing power to relay the cost. When retail pricing hits a consumer affordability wall, cost backs up into the chain. The converter's insulation depends on the last link—the consumer—remaining willing to pay. When that breaks, the volatility the converter thought it exported comes home. Additionally, Silgan's EPS decline shows that even sophisticated converters cannot pass every dollar forward, and the incomplete pass-through creates profitability pressure that forces hedging through segment mix, which only works if you own multiple lines.

The surprising part

The converter that grew revenue the most—Sonoco, up 42% to $7.5B—achieved that growth almost entirely through acquisition ($2.2B from the Sonoco Metal Packaging EMEA deal) rather than through the pass-through mechanism, while the core legacy business declined 2.1%. This means the headline growth that looks like a converter winning the commodity game is really an acquisition masking an operating decline in the core business. The second surprise is that Ball's record $956M free cash flow in FY2025 came from pass-through mechanics operating smoothly, not from operational innovation or margin expansion—the business makes money by not absorbing commodity risk, not by outcompeting rivals.

Why it matters

A converter's stable margin during a commodity storm is a shopper's higher grocery bill in disguise. Every aluminum spike the converter contractually refuses to hold travels down the chain until it stops at the shelf price. Understanding the converter's squeeze explains why food and beverage prices ratchet up during commodity shocks and rarely ratchet back down—the cost was engineered to flow toward the one party with no pass-through clause, and that party is the consumer.

The can maker earns its money not by mastering aluminum prices but by writing the contract that makes aluminum prices always someone else's problem.
