The Consumer Affordability Wall: Where Commodity Inflation Compression Stops

Consumer goods companies pushed commodity inflation costs backward through pricing, but that pass-through hits a hard limit when household budgets break and purchase frequency collapses, forcing cost compression back onto suppliers and manufacturers.

For 2024-2025, consumer-goods companies like PepsiCo and Mondelez held pricing power despite commodity headwinds by absorbing costs into margins initially, then passing them forward to consumers who had limited alternatives. But the tension is now visible: consumer budgets are tightening, volume growth is slowing, and private-label switching is increasing. When consumers stop buying at the elevated prices or trade down to lower-margin formats, the cost compression has nowhere left to go—it travels backward to suppliers and manufacturers with immediate margin impact.

The mechanism

The commodity-inflation transfer began in 2021-2022 when input costs (cocoa, phosphate, sulfur, steel, freight) spiked. Consumer-goods companies had several paths: absorb margins, raise prices, reduce volume, or hedge. Most chose aggressive pricing because they had pricing power in habit-driven categories. Mondelez said 2025 was dynamic and challenging with unprecedented cocoa headwinds, but full-year organic revenue still grew 4.3% because of pricing discipline. PepsiCo raised prices while managing volume pressure through portfolio reshaping and productivity.

But the pass-through is now hitting limits. Mondelez's FY2025 showed the tension: volume/mix fell 3.7% even while organic revenue grew 4.3%, meaning the company won the math on pricing but lost the game on category health. Q2 2026 organic revenue growth decelerated to 2.2%, with volume/mix only marginally positive at 0.7%. Management is now focused on 'improved volumes' and 'volume growth' in 2026—language it did not need in 2025. That shift signals consumers are beginning to switch formats or purchase less frequently at elevated prices.

The leverage operates differently at the input level. Cleveland-Cliffs is experiencing the opposite pressure: customers (automotive, infrastructure) are forcing margin compression because they face consumer and end-user affordability constraints. The company reported $18.6 billion of revenue but $1.4 billion of GAAP net loss in FY2025 partly because fixed-price automotive contracts lagged spot pricing when prices fell. But the deeper issue is that auto demand itself is weak due to consumer affordability pressure on vehicle purchases. That demand weakness flows backward through the supply chain, forcing Cleveland-Cliffs to choose between accepting lower volumes or accepting lower prices.

Mosaic faced the same backward pressure. Q2 2026 operating loss was $36 million despite $2.8 billion of revenue, with reported net loss of $273 million. Mosaic Fertilizantes segment contributed only $60 million of adjusted EBITDA out of $407 million total, showing Brazil weakness and inventory management drag. The company partially idled Bartow (40% of targeted operating rate) because demand for phosphate did not justify production. That idling is not normal capacity management; it is a visible admission that downstream demand is not absorbing prices at the level manufacturers need to stay profitable.

Why now

Consumer budgets are hitting a hard constraint in 2026 after two years of elevated prices and persistent inflation in rent, energy, and childcare. Wage growth has not kept pace with cumulative price increases in many categories. Younger and lower-income households are seeing share loss to private label and discount formats. Frequency is declining (consumers buying less often) and basket size is shrinking (consumers buying smaller packages to reduce per-trip spend). At the same time, input costs have not returned to pre-2021 levels, so manufacturers face a squeeze: they cannot raise prices further without losing more volume, but they also cannot absorb input costs without compression to operating margins.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
In the near term, manufacturers absorb the cost compression first. PepsiCo and Mondelez have productivity programs and cost-reduction initiatives running to offset margin pressure. Cleveland-Cliffs and Mosaic are idling assets and cutting capex. But consumers also pay through reduced assortment, smaller packages, lower quality, or less frequent purchases. Input suppliers (mining, agriculture, chemicals) pay through demand reduction and price pressure. The transfer is not frictionless; it is a chain of margin loss rippling backward through the supply chain as each link tries to pass the burden to the next.
Who profits
Short-term: discount and private-label operators who can serve price-conscious consumers. Dollar General and private-label brands are gaining share as consumers trade down. Long-term: companies that can reduce operating complexity and cost structure faster than demand falls. But there are no real winners in a commodity-whiplash scenario; there are only companies that lose less.

The tension

The core tension is irreversible. Consumer affordability is the hard constraint. Pricing power evaporates when consumers have limited choice but choosing not to buy instead. Once volume loss begins, manufacturers cannot recover it by reversing prices—consumer behavior stickiness means they stay with lower-price alternatives or reduced frequency even if prices fall. For commodity producers, demand destruction from downstream margin compression is permanent within that cycle. For consumer-goods companies, the tradeoff is between accepting margin compression or accepting volume loss, and both are negative for total profit.

The surprising part

The most counter-intuitive signal is that the biggest consumer-goods companies are now prioritizing 'volume growth' and 'improved volumes' in guidance—language that would have been beneath them in 2022-2024 when pricing was the headline. That linguistic shift is evidence of margin exhaustion. PepsiCo emphasizing value and affordability in Q2 2026, Mondelez guiding to flat-to-2% revenue growth after 4.3% in 2025, and Cleveland-Cliffs running plants at 40% capacity are not normal cyclical moves. They are evidence that the commodity-inflation pass-through has reversed direction.

Why it matters

This pattern matters because it shows the limit of corporate pricing power in essential categories. Consumers cannot simply refuse food, beverages, and basic goods indefinitely when prices rise, but they can reduce frequency, trade down, and shift behavior. At the societal level, this creates pressure on household budgets, especially for lower-income households who spend a higher share of income on food and essentials. It also signals to policy that inflation in essentials, even if wholesale inflation recedes, can create lasting consumer behavior shifts that erode category health. For labor and wages, it suggests that without wage growth matching cumulative price increases, consumer volume behavior will continue deteriorating.

Consumer affordability became the irreversible constraint when elevated prices forced purchasing-frequency collapse and private-label switching, reversing commodity inflation costs backward through the supply chain where there is no pricing power to push them forward again.
