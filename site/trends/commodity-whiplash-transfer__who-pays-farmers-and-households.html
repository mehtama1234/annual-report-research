The Final Link: Farmers, Contractors, and Households

The unhedged actors at the end of supply chains—small farmers, contractors, and households—absorb commodity volatility that large producers systematically pass through.

At the end of every commodity pass-through chain sits an actor with no hedging infrastructure, no negotiating power, and no pricing power to pass costs further. The farmer buying nitrogen fertilizer at prices set by CF Industries, the small contractor buying steel at spot rates, the household buying packaged food from Mondelez after cocoa costs are embedded in retail prices—these are the final links, and their inability to absorb or pass costs is not temporary or negotiable but structurally permanent.

The mechanism

Commodity price volatility is not absorbed equally across supply chains. Instead, it flows downstream in a systematic pattern: producers lock in prices through hedging, contracts, and scale; large intermediaries negotiate based on volume and relationships; and the final links—small farmers, small contractors, households—face spot prices with no negotiating power. CF Industries, Nutrien, and Mosaic control the upstream nitrogen, potash, and phosphate prices that determine fertilizer costs. These companies have hedging desks, scale contracts, natural-gas locking mechanisms, and the ability to adjust production and capital spending to manage commodity risk. In 2025, CF Industries reported $1.46 billion in net earnings and $2.89 billion in adjusted EBITDA despite volatile markets; Nutrien reported $2.30 billion in net earnings and returned $848 million to shareholders in just the first half of 2026. These companies capture the spread between commodity inputs and fertilizer outputs. But at the end of the chain sits a farmer in Iowa or Brazil who must buy nitrogen at whatever price CF Industries' production and natural-gas costs dictate. That farmer cannot hedge, cannot negotiate batch contracts, and cannot reduce acreage fast enough to avoid buying at peak prices.

The pattern repeats across commodity chains. Mondelez, the global snacking platform with $38.537 billion in 2025 revenue, faces cocoa cost volatility that it explicitly frames as an unprecedented headwind pressuring profitability. But Mondelez has multiple tools to manage this pressure: it can raise prices on consumers (which it did, generating volume/mix declines of 3.7% in 2025 despite top-line growth), it can reduce cocoa content in products, it can shift sourcing, and it can substitute lower-cost commodities where possible. A Mondelez consumer in Europe who buys Cadbury Dairy Milk or Oreos faces a price increase when cocoa costs rise. But that consumer cannot hedge, cannot negotiate directly with Mondelez, and cannot stockpile because the product is perishable and shelf-stable time is limited. What Mondelez did in 2025—raise prices, cut costs, maintain capital returns of $4.9 billion to shareholders—only worked because consumers kept buying despite price increases. The company succeeded by passing the cost forward to households, not backward to cocoa suppliers. The household paid, and Mondelez still generated earnings and returned capital. The farmer buying Mosaic's phosphate or CF's ammonia faces the inverse problem: they cannot raise the price of corn or soybeans to the market. They face a global commodity price set on exchanges they do not access. When fertilizer costs rise, their margin gets compressed, and they absorb the loss.

The structural permanence of this imbalance is the key insight. A farmer cannot start a hedging desk or negotiate a five-year fertilizer contract at reasonable terms. Small construction contractors cannot negotiate bulk-steel pricing with US Steel or ArcelorMittal. Households cannot negotiate cocoa prices with Mondelez. The reason is scale and information asymmetry. Nutrien operates more than 600,000 customer accounts and over 1,800 retail locations. These give Nutrien direct access to agronomic data, farm economics, credit decisions, and product bundling that individual farmers cannot replicate. When Nutrien sells proprietary biological products, agronomy services, and financial support alongside commodity fertilizer, the customer is locked into a relationship where Nutrien controls the pricing menu. In 2026, Nutrien Q2 reported $1.22 billion in net earnings and emphasized record potash sales volumes and stronger proprietary-product margins. The proprietary products generate higher margins precisely because customers cannot price-shop as easily for customized agronomy as they can for bulk urea. This is not just commodity pricing; it is relationship lock-in where the large integrated player has all the negotiating power and the farmer has none.

Why now

Global commodity volatility since 2022 has exposed the fragility of unhedged supply chains. Fertilizer costs have remained elevated relative to historical norms even after the initial post-Russia-sanctions spike. Cocoa costs remain near multi-year highs. Shipping and logistics disruption make spot purchases necessary even when prices are unfavorable. At the same time, large integrated producers like CF, Nutrien, and Mosaic have proven they can maintain earnings and return capital even in high-cost environments by passing prices forward. The imbalance is now structural rather than cyclical—the large producers are not waiting for prices to fall; they are actively monetizing scarcity and cost inflation.

Who profits
Large integrated commodity producers and branded manufacturers who control pricing, hedging, and customer relationships

The tension

The system is only stable if the final links have some ability to absorb costs or can be continually squeezed without collapsing. For households buying Mondelez, the limit is price elasticity and brand strength—they can absorb 3.7% volume declines if Mondelez raises prices, but eventually demand curves bend. For farmers, the limit is crop economics and debt capacity—they can absorb one year of margin compression, but repeated years force consolidation or exit. The deepening tension is that large producers have every incentive to keep prices high to maximize near-term earnings, while small farmers have declining incentive to stay in business if margins stay compressed. This could eventually snap into agricultural consolidation or commodity supply disruption if farmers exit faster than new entrants replace them.

The surprising part

The most non-obvious twist is that Mondelez's ability to pass costs forward is not inevitable or permanent—it depends on brand strength and consumer loyalty holding even as prices rise. In Q2 2026, Mondelez reported volume/mix growth of 0.7% and raised guidance to expect at least 2% organic growth. That is a shift from the 3.7% volume/mix decline in 2025. This suggests that either consumers are adapting to higher prices or Mondelez is finding ways to absorb some costs internally. But the underlying dynamic—that Mondelez was willing to cut volume by 3.7% rather than cut costs or margins—reveals the priority: maintain profitability and capital returns even if it means fewer sales. The farmer and the household absorb the cost, not the shareholder.

Why it matters

This pattern determines who gets hurt when commodity prices spike and who stays insulated. Large companies and their shareholders get protected through hedging, pricing power, and scale. Small farmers face margin compression and potential bankruptcy during commodity booms because their input costs stay high relative to commodity prices they cannot control. Households face food-price inflation that reflects both genuine commodity scarcity and corporate margin protection. The political consequence is that when food or fertilizer prices spike, small producers get blamed for not producing more, but the real issue is that the supply chain is designed so large intermediaries capture the spread. This creates long-term consolidation pressure and pushes small operators out of business, which then further concentrates power and pricing in large producers' hands.

The unhedged final links in supply chains—farmers, contractors, households—absorb commodity volatility that large producers systematically pass through, creating a permanent wealth transfer upward through every commodity system.
