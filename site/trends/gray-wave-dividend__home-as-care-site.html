The Last Move: When Home Becomes the Hospital

From 2024 through Q1 2026, payers systematically redirected care from institutions into homes: Addus reported record $1.42B revenue in 2025, Aveanna Q1 2026 revenue hit $647.9M (up 15.9% YoY), and Enhabit's 9.5% admissions growth in Q1 2026 came despite 3.1% per-patient-day reimbursement decline. Four operators expanding simultaneously proves this is payer policy, not market share competition.

Aging-in-place is no longer a preference—it is now policy infrastructure. Payers actively reward home-based care because it costs 75-80% less than SNFs, hospitals, and assisted-living facilities, while seniors demand it. This creates a durable, policy-backed expansion in home health, personal care, and hospice revenue across all four major operators, even as unit economics tighten and labor remains constrained. Addus' 78.4% personal-care mix in Q2 2026, Aveanna's 15.9% YoY growth to $647.9M in Q1 2026, BrightSpring's 54% YoY home-health census expansion, and Enhabit's 9.5% admissions growth all signal the same payer-driven demand wave.

The mechanism

The economics are simple and relentless. A skilled-nursing facility bed costs Medicare and Medicaid roughly $300–500 per day. A hospital inpatient stay averages $2,500–3,500 per day. A home-health visit, personal-care hour, or hospice-service day costs a fraction of that. Payers—both government and commercial—have written policies that route patients into home-based care whenever clinically feasible. Aveanna's pediatric private duty work ($647.9M Q1 2026, up 15.9% YoY), Addus's personal-care empire (78.4% of Q2 2026 revenue), Enhabit's skilled home-health branch network (249 locations, 41,786 average daily census), and BrightSpring's integrated pharmacy-and-provider model (46,448 home-health census, up 54% YoY in Q2 2026) all exist because this reimbursement shift happened, not in spite of it.

The second driver is patient and family preference combined with policy enforcement. Seniors want to age at home. Parents of medically complex children want their kids at home, not in institutions. Families experience less disruption, less infection risk, and more autonomy when care is home-based. This preference is no longer treated as nice-to-have. It is now embedded in outcome metrics, quality ratings, and value-based-care contracts. The result is that home-health operators are not fighting demand. They are managing explosive growth in referral volume while navigating simultaneous pressure to hire caregivers and clinicians faster than supply permits. Aveanna's 15.9% YoY growth and BrightSpring's 54% YoY home-health census expansion are not sustainable growth rates under tight labor markets—they are policy-driven demand waves that are outpacing supply.

The third mechanism is consolidation and density. Home-based care is fragmented at the local level but increasingly concentrated at the operator level. Addus acquired HomeCourt in Indiana during Q1 2026 to build recurring local density. Aveanna announced the Family First acquisition post-Q4 2025 to deepen pediatric private duty. Enhabit was merged into Kinderhook's platform to benefit from operational integration. BrightSpring divested Community Living to focus on higher-acuity home-health and pharmacy. Each operator is using acquisition and branch pruning to build density—more locations, more clinicians, more predictable referral flow—in geography where they can compete for managed-care contracts and capture local market share. Fragmented home care was invisible. Consolidated home care is a scaled, recognizable payor partner that can negotiate rates and sign turnkey service agreements.

Why now

Three policy and demographic shifts converged between 2024 and Q1 2026. First, value-based-care arrangements, bundled payments, and managed-Medicaid penetration reached critical mass. These mechanisms created explicit financial incentives to move volume out of institutions. State budgets faced long-term-services-and-supports (LTSS) pressure as Medicaid costs accelerated, making home-based alternatives strategically valuable. Second, the aging population reached critical mass—Medicare enrollment crossed 68 million, Medicaid LTSS spending accelerated, and the 65-plus demographic wave manifested in actual referral volume. The pandemic normalized home-based care as a clinical option (2020-2022), and post-pandemic adoption (2024-2026) proved it was durable, not temporary. Post-2022, home-based care shifted from 'substitute' to 'default.' Third, the four operators all proved they could operate at scale without facility infrastructure. Enhabit's nine consecutive quarters of debt reduction, Aveanna's consistent 15%+ growth, BrightSpring's integrated platform, and Addus's record $1.42B revenue all sent the same signal to payers: home care operators are now reliable enterprise partners. That reliability unlocked payer confidence to accelerate volume flows into home settings.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Medicare and Medicaid are the spine—they cover skilled nursing, home-health visits, personal-care attendants, and hospice for the populations that most need care. Commercial managed-care plans are the accelerator—they have contractual flexibility to incentivize home-based settings and penalize institutional settings through benefit design and reimbursement. Families, out-of-pocket, fill the gaps. Aveanna's private-duty model is often funded through a mix of Medicaid, commercial riders, and family self-pay. Addus's personal-care business leans heavily on state Medicaid and state-level rate decisions (Texas and Illinois rate increases were called out as material drivers in Q1 2026). BrightSpring and Enhabit benefit from Medicare managed-care concentration.
Who profits
The four operators—Addus, Aveanna, Enhabit, and BrightSpring—capture direct recurring revenue. But the profit accrues unevenly. Addus is mostly personal care (78.4% of Q2 2026 revenue), which is labor-intensive and lower-margin. Enhabit and Aveanna sit in the middle: clinically managed, better-reimbursed, but still labor-constrained and exposed to admissions and census volatility. BrightSpring is the most integrated and arguably the highest-margin operator because it combines pharmacy (where margins are better) with home-health and provider delivery. Downstream, regional and local home-care startups, therapy networks, and primary-care clinics benefit from referral flow. The real winners are managed-care organizations, accountable-care organizations, and value-based-care networks that hold per-member costs down by shifting volume to lower-cost settings.

The tension

Labor is the breaking point. Every operator reports hiring and retention pressure. Caregivers, nurses, therapists, and home-health aides are hard to recruit and keep. Wages need to rise to compete with other low-skill healthcare work and non-healthcare options. As volume scales (Aveanna up 15.9%, BrightSpring census up 54%), the per-unit reimbursement rate can flatten or fall even as total revenue rises. Enhabit's Q1 2026 showed the pattern exactly: 9.5% admissions growth but only 0.6% revenue growth—unit revenue per patient day fell 3.1% despite cost per patient day improving 2.2%. The labor cost squeeze is built into the model. Additionally, the payer environment can shift. If Medicare reimbursement declines more sharply, if managed-Medicaid contracts tighten, or if state budgets cut LTSS funding, the expansion stops abruptly. Technology and compliance costs are rising too—electronic visit verification, cybersecurity, clinical documentation, and regulatory infrastructure all burden smaller and less-efficient operators. The last tension is that much of the demand being absorbed is the demand that used to go to family caregivers, often unpaid. Home-based care operators are monetizing what was previously a household burden.

The surprising part

The surprising piece is not that home-based care is growing—that is obvious. It is that the growth is simultaneous and synchronized across all four operators at 15%+ rates despite visible reimbursement headwinds. Addus, Aveanna, Enhabit, and BrightSpring are all expanding home-health census, acquiring local competitors, and posting record adjusted EBITDA at the same time. This is not a zero-sum market share shift. This is absolute demand expansion driven by policy. Enhabit's Q1 2026 perfectly illustrates the pattern: 9.5% admissions growth but only 0.6% revenue growth means unit revenue fell sharply, yet the company continued expanding because payer demand was inelastic. That is not a normal market; it is a policy-driven demand wave.

Why it matters

For an aging adult or a family with a medically complex child, this trend means care happens at home, not in an institution—better quality of life, less disruption, more autonomy. For families stretched by caregiver burden, this trend means paid professional care can absorb work that used to fall on unpaid relatives, though it also means that labor cost rises and may not be covered by insurance. For the healthcare system, this is the mechanism by which aging is managed without overwhelming institutional capacity—it is how we make aging-in-place real, not rhetorical. For payers and public budgets, this trend is cost-control infrastructure—the way aging populations are absorbed without explosive spending growth.

Four operators simultaneously expanding home-based care not because they competed for market share, but because payers systematically redirected an entire generation of care into kitchens and bedrooms to cut costs and meet patient preference.
