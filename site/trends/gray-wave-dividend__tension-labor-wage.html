The Wage Floor That Could Break the Model

Home-care operators monetize aging through workers paid $16-20/hour in isolated conditions—but the workforce shrinking as demand soars. Revenue growth outpaces wage sustainability by 15% annually, creating a math that stops working around 2027-2028.

Home care's economics pivot on a specific arbitrage: aging patients stay home at 40-50% of facility cost, staffed by low-wage aides. Aveanna, Addus, and Brookdale are all growth-stage when that arbitrage existed. But the 2024-2026 period shows demand growing 15-54% year-over-year while caregiver recruitment tightens in every market. Reimbursement rates (set by state budgets and Medicare formulas) rise 2-3% annually. If caregiver wages rise 10-15% annually to clear scarcity—as labor scarcity patterns suggest—the compressed spread forces either margin collapse, price increases (shrinking addressable market), or consolidation-driven labor outsourcing. The model does not fail outright; it becomes a two-tier system: good care for private-pay, access-restricted care for Medicaid populations.

The mechanism

Home care's unit economics depend on a specific spread: patient visit cost (caregiver wage + overhead + routing + compliance) versus reimbursement per visit (Medicare/Medicaid rate + managed-care margin). At Addus, personal care is 78.4% of revenue—the entire company is essentially structured around $16-20/hour aides visiting patients 3-5 times weekly at a Medicaid rate of roughly $18-22 per visit. Brookdale operates 647 communities with daily staffing needs; each occupancy point requires local caregiver density. The spread works only when local labor markets supply aides at wages below $22/hour. Historical wage data shows home-care aide wages grew 2-3% annually 2015-2023. Starting 2024, as senior-housing and home-care operators simultaneously scaled (Aveanna 15.9%, BrightSpring 54% census growth), local markets began tightening. Competing for the same low-wage, part-time workers across retail, food service, and care roles, home-care operators discovered recruiting and retention had become central operating constraints, not afterthoughts.

Reimbursement does not move with labor-market scarcity. Medicare and Medicaid rates are set through federal or state legislative budgets, updated annually or biannually, with typical increases of 2-3% tied to inflation indices. Addus explicitly emphasizes that Texas and Illinois state rate increases are essential to maintaining margins. Aveanna's packet notes reimbursement advocacy and payor strategy as central to value capture. But state legislatures do not react to local labor-market tightness; they respond to budget constraints and political optics. If home-care operators need to raise aide wages from $18 to $21/hour to clear recruitment (a 17% jump), and Medicaid rates rise 2.5% that same year, the operator absorbs the 14.5% spread compression. Brookdale's 2025 net loss of $263.5 million while adjusted EBITDA grew 19.1% and occupancy recovered to 80% in Q1 2026 is the real signal: growth and occupancy are not translating to net profit because labor costs are rising faster than per-resident pricing can follow. The company is growing occupancy but losing money because the labor-cost base is outpacing reimbursement elasticity.

Consolidation appeared to be the solution—rolling up fragmented local operators to gain recruiting density and negotiate scale with payors. Addus expanded into Indiana via the HomeCourt acquisition specifically to build recruiting density. Aveanna acquired Family First Homecare post-Q4 2025 to secure stable workforce access in tight pediatric-care markets. But consolidation has not solved scarcity. It has only shown that a tightening low-wage labor market is a system-level constraint, not a local-operator problem. When BrightSpring's census grows 54% but the available aide pool grows 2-4%, no acquisition strategy closes that gap. The model's arithmetic breaks: growth driven by aging demand collides with labor supply growing an order of magnitude slower. Growth itself becomes the problem.

Why now

The tension emerged in 2024-2026 because three things aligned: First, post-pandemic caregiver exodus (2020-2023 saw 15-20% workforce shrinkage in home care as aides exited to retail and other sectors during COVID-related burnout and exposure fears). Second, aging cohort acceleration—the 75+ population in the US was growing 2-3% annually before 2024; by 2025-2026 that growth accelerated as the early-boomers aged into high-need years. Aveanna's 15.9% revenue growth Q1 2026, BrightSpring's 54% home-health census growth, and Brookdale's occupancy recovery to 80% in Q1 2026 all represent the compressing gap between growing demand and fixed labor supply. Third, regulatory attention to care-worker pay turned acute. Minnesota and other states began raising Medicaid rates for home care in 2024-2025 explicitly to address aide retention crisis. These rate increases (typically 4-6% one-time) are exceptions, not the rule, and they signal policymakers recognizing the problem. By mid-2026, every home-care operator in the archive was listing 'caregiver hiring and retention' as a central operational challenge, not a peripheral staffing issue. The inflection is 2026-2027: if wage pressure continues at current pace (7-10% annually in tight markets) while Medicaid rates rise 2-3% per state legislative cycle, the model's profitability compress into negative territory by 2027-2028, forcing consolidation, service narrowing, or reimbursement policy crisis.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
In the immediate term (2026-2027), publicly traded home-care operators (Aveanna, Addus, BrightSpring, Brookdale) absorb margin compression through slower growth, higher leverage, or operational restructuring—already visible in Brookdale's $263.5 million 2025 net loss despite growth. Regional, smaller home-care providers lacking scale and balance-sheet flexibility will consolidate or exit. Families at the income threshold—those who do not qualify for Medicaid but cannot afford private-pay rates of $250+/day—face narrowing options as providers redirect capacity to higher-margin populations. Those dependent on complex private-duty care (Aveanna's 78% revenue growth driver) face fewer aides willing to take those high-burden roles. In the medium term (2028+), if wages rise without reimbursement following, access to home care narrows sharply. Medicaid recipients and dual-eligible populations lose viable aging-in-place pathways. Families shift burden back to unpaid caregivers (mostly daughters and wives), or patients move into assisted living and nursing facilities, raising total system costs.
Who profits
Winners emerge only from asymmetry. First: early-exit investors who sell before full margin compression (2026-2027). Second: large consolidators (Aveanna, BrightSpring) large enough to negotiate better reimbursement rates with Medicaid (concentration strategy) and to absorb near-term margin pressure through leverage (if capital markets allow). Third: specialized labor-management outsourcers that could emerge—vendors selling recruiting, screening, and retention as an outsourced service to take labor cost and risk off operator balance sheets. Fourth: facility-based operators (skilled nursing, assisted living) if families cannot afford home care and shift to institutions. Finally: any policy intervention that raises Medicare/Medicaid rates sharply (a political choice, not an operator choice)—that transfers cost from operators to government payors.

The tension

The externalized cost is social access and unpaid care burden. Home care was designed to be the lower-cost, patient-preferred alternative to nursing homes—keeping people independent, reducing institutional burden on public systems, and letting families remain caregivers (involved, not sole-responsible). If wages rise faster than reimbursement, that breaks: the model works only for wealthier patients or becomes a two-tier system (good care for private-pay, restricted access for Medicaid). Policymakers resist raising Medicaid rates (budget constraints, optics). Caregivers—mostly women, often immigrants—continue leaving for retail or food service (less emotionally taxing, more stable). The result is hollowing-out: growing unmet care needs, families forced into unpaid informal care (mostly women, the same burden home care was supposed to shift away), and gradual return to institutional care for those who can afford it, leaving public systems even more strained. The hidden tension is that home-care operators are not the real policymakers—they are caught between aging demand and labor scarcity, with reimbursement set by political process, not by economics.

The surprising part

The non-obvious dynamic is that home-care operators are partly victims of their own scale success. By making aging-in-place work economically at scale (Aveanna 15.9% growth, BrightSpring 54% census growth, Brookdale occupancy recovery), they drove adoption so rapidly that labor supply cannot keep pace. The growth itself creates the scarcity problem. Consolidation has also reduced worker bargaining power in some local markets—when one operator dominates a region, aides have fewer employers to choose from—yet it has not solved the underlying scarcity. The operators need slower demand growth than the aging demographic is producing (impossible) or a productivity breakthrough that eliminates per-visit labor time (remote monitoring, tech-enabled care)—both implausible in 3-5 years. So consolidation is not an escape valve; it is a trap. The only exit is policy (higher Medicaid rates) or market (price increases that shrink addressable market), neither of which the operators control.

Why it matters

Millions of older Americans and people with disabilities depend on home-care workers for basic daily functioning—bathing, medication, mobility, meal prep. If access narrows, the alternative is institutional care (costlier, often lower-quality, deeply isolating) or unpaid family caregiving (mostly daughters and wives, recreating the burden shift home care was supposed to solve). Caregivers themselves—mostly women, often immigrants, working in isolated patient homes with minimal benefits, job security, or wage growth—face either perpetual poverty wages or exit to other sectors. A wage squeeze that makes home care inaccessible does not eliminate the need; it shifts cost and burden, not burden away. The question is not whether caregiving happens; it is whether it happens as paid labor in a sustainable model or as unpaid family burden in a reverted system.

Home care works as a business model only if a shrinking low-wage workforce can staff a growing aging population at rates set by government budgets; that equation breaks when labor scarcity raises wages 7-15% annually while reimbursement rises 2-3%.
