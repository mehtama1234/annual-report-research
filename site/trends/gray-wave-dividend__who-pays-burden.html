The Invisible Labor: Who Actually Provides the Care

The home-care model externalizes labor costs onto predominantly female, immigrant caregivers working near-minimum wages, and onto unpaid family caregivers who absorb coordination and supervision without compensation.

The financial success of the gray-wave dividend depends entirely on two groups that do not appear in earnings statements: the home health aides and personal-care workers earning poverty-adjacent wages in isolated home settings, and the unpaid family caregivers who coordinate, supervise, and fill the gaps between professional visits. The companies' filings reveal chronic labor shortages while providing no path to wage competitiveness. The model is sustainable only as long as these workers cannot price their own scarcity.

The mechanism

The gray-wave dividend is architecturally dependent on externalizing labor costs. A home health aide visiting three patients per day generates revenue for Aveanna or Addus of perhaps $150-200 per visit. Yet that aide makes approximately $16-20 per hour, often without benefits, working in isolation without colleagues, and bearing the emotional and physical toll of intimate care work. The economic model works only because these workers cannot command higher wages. If home health aides became unionized or if wage pressure forced companies to pay $25-30 per hour, the entire margin structure of home care would collapse. The companies' filings repeat references to labor challenges—Aveanna's mentions of 'caregiver recruitment, retention' and Addus's references to density and service-line discipline—but these are euphemisms for the inability to pay enough to keep workers.

The second layer of externalizing is the unpaid family caregiver. Brookdale's 58,000 residents in 647 communities require coordination, medication supervision, transportation, and emotional support. Much of that actually happens in the form of adult children visiting daily, managing complex medical care, and absorbing gaps between professional visits. These family caregivers—disproportionately women in their 40s and 50s—are bearing real economic and opportunity costs. They reduce working hours, take sick leave, and forgo career advancement to manage aging parents or spouses. That burden is completely invisible in Brookdale's or Welltower's financial statements. It shows up as value creation for the company. From the family's perspective, it is a transfer of burden from the institution to the household.

Welltower's 2026 financial performance reveals the extractive logic most clearly. Seniors housing operating same-store NOI grew 20.4% in Q4 2025 and 22.1% in Q1 2026. That 20%+ growth in net operating income did not come from innovation or efficiency. It came from occupancy gains and pricing moves—more residents paying higher fees. Those residents are getting older, frailer, and more medically complex. The care required to manage them is not getting cheaper. But the workers providing it are not seeing the benefits of that margin expansion. The care workers are still near minimum wage. The families are still absorbing unpaid coordination. Yet the REIT is collecting 20%+ NOI growth.

Why now

The inflection has arrived because the demographic wave is finally large enough that companies can fill occupancy without competing fiercely for workers. Brookdale's occupancy recovered to 80% in Q1 2026, a marked improvement from prior years. With more residents coming in and more care required, the labor shortage has become systemic. But companies are not responding by raising wages to levels that would attract and retain workers. Instead, they are responding by spreading the care burden onto family caregivers and accepting high turnover among aides. The political moment also matters: policymakers treat aging-in-place and home care as a solution to healthcare costs without acknowledging that the solution depends on hidden subsidies from family labor and from care workers living below their economic reservation price.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
The home health aide in Iowa or Texas working for $18 per hour without paid time off, bearing the physical toll of lifting patients and the emotional toll of intimate care work. The adult daughter who takes 2-3 hours per week to manage her mother's medications, coordinate visits, manage billing, and fill the gaps. The immigrant caregiver working off-the-books or through understaffed franchises. The working-age adult who reduces hours or leaves the workforce entirely to provide family care. These populations are paying the cost in time, health, and foregone opportunity. Their payment is not voluntary and not compensated by the system.
Who profits
Aveanna, Addus, Brookdale, and Welltower capture financial value by keeping labor costs low while charging private pay and insurance at market rates. Welltower captures financial value by owning the real estate and taking a share of the operating economics without bearing the direct care liability. The broader healthcare system profits by shifting care out of expensive institutions into cheaper home settings without paying for the actual labor complexity. Policymakers and health systems benefit politically from appearing to solve the aging problem through market-based home-care expansion, when they are actually shifting burden to households and to a labor class that cannot bargain.

The tension

The tension emerges as two pressures collide. First, the demographic wave is accelerating, and occupancy is finally tight enough that labor becomes a bottleneck. Brookdale and others are already reporting difficulty filling shifts. Second, the political visibility of caregiver burnout and family caregiver burden is rising. If wages do not rise or if the burden on families becomes unsustainable, the home-care model can break—not through market failure, but through a refusal to participate. Workers can shift to retail or hospitality if those sectors offer better wages and conditions. Families can decide that institutional care is actually preferable to the burden of managing care at home. At that point, the entire gray-wave dividend evaporates because it was always dependent on these costs being suppressed.

The surprising part

The most counterintuitive finding is that the companies' financial success is almost entirely divorced from the care workers' economic well-being. Addus revenue is $1.42B but we see no disclosure of average aide wage or turnover rate. Aveanna is growing at 15.9% with private duty services expanding, but there is no mention of how wage or retention trends compare to historical norms. Welltower is achieving 20% NOI growth in seniors housing, but the underlying care labor market is not improving. The companies are decoupling care volume and revenue growth from labor market pressure. That only works if workers have no outside options and if families have no exit. Both of those assumptions are fraying.

Why it matters

This matters to any adult with aging parents and to any worker providing or considering home care as a career. You are either the person being paid $18/hour to manage intimate care tasks in homes with limited support, or you are the adult child managing your mother's medical care without compensation while trying to maintain your own career and family. The financial success of Brookdale and Welltower is being built on your labor scarcity and your inability to refuse. That math is unsustainable.

The gray-wave dividend is built on the economic impossibility of care workers earning livable wages and families absorbing unpaid coordination costs simultaneously.
