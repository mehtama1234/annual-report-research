The Grid's New Landlords

Gas pipelines and regulated transmission operators are extracting structural economic rents from the AI buildout — tolling electrons and molecules that hyperscalers cannot route around — while bearing none of the technology risk that defines every other layer of the AI stack.

Hyperscalers solved the generation problem by signing power purchase agreements with wind and solar developers. They cannot solve the transport problem. In most U.S. jurisdictions, private parties cannot own transmission lines or interstate gas pipelines — the regulated utility or midstream operator owns the only available corridors. That ownership position converts AI power demand into a 40-year annuity: the utility builds a dedicated line, files for a regulated return on that capital with a state commission, and the data center pays. The midstream operator owns the gathering and transmission infrastructure that delivers gas to the combined-cycle plants backing those power contracts, and signs a 10- to 15-year take-or-pay agreement for the molecules. Neither the hyperscaler nor the renewable developer can avoid these companies. What makes the position durable is that the pipes and wires were built for 20th-century industrial loads, so incremental expansion costs a fraction of greenfield construction on a per-unit basis.

The mechanism

The economic structure is a toll road with no alternative route. Data centers require power 24 hours a day at reliability standards that solar and wind portfolios cannot meet without battery storage at a cost that makes the economics of the data center unworkable. Natural gas plants — combined-cycle turbines that ramp in minutes — fill the reliability gap. Midstream operators like Williams and Energy Transfer own the gathering and transmission infrastructure that delivers gas from production basins to those plants. When a hyperscaler signs a 15-year power contract backed by a gas plant, the contract chain reaches the midstream operator who owns the pipes. Williams calls this the 'LNG-plus-power backbone'; its Transco pipeline, Socrates and Power Express projects, and Blackstone power-innovation joint venture are each designed to monetize this position. The operator bears no demand-creation risk and no technology risk. It collects a tariff on throughput.

Transmission utilities occupy the same structural position at a different layer. A data center in Iowa drawing 500 MW needs dedicated transmission and distribution infrastructure that only the local regulated utility can build and own. Alliant Energy contracted 3.4 GW of data-center demand as of Q1 2026. Each gigawatt requires new transmission construction; Alliant files for a regulated return on that capital at the Iowa or Wisconsin commission, and the construction cost flows into rate base. That rate base grows at roughly 6% to 8% per year on Alliant's plan through 2029, which is directly funded by load growth from customers like QTS, Google, and Meta siting near Des Moines. The key asymmetry: the utility cannot refuse the load, but the data center also cannot refuse to pay.

At the gas-distribution layer, Atmos Energy shows a slower but equally structural version of the same dynamic. Atmos is the largest fully regulated natural-gas-only distributor in the country, serving 3.4 million customers across eight states. It does not have a data-center concentration story the way Alliant does. Instead, it has a modernization compounding story: $26B of projected capital through fiscal 2030 recovers through regulated rates on a rate base that doubles from $21.4B to $42B. Communities growing around data-center corridors need upgraded gas service for the on-site backup generation, heating, and industrial support that accompany hyperscaler campuses. Atmos collects those returns without needing to name a single hyperscaler customer.

Why now

The inflection is dateable. In 2023 and early 2024, AI power demand was a forecast item in utility rate cases — a number planners discussed but regulators treated as speculative. By late 2025 and into 2026, it had become signed contracts. Alliant Energy ended 2025 with roughly 3 GW of contracted data-center demand and framed it as a 50% load increase by 2030; by Q1 2026 that had grown to 3.4 GW and a 60% projected increase by 2031. Williams Companies entered 2026 guiding to $8.05B–$8.35B of adjusted EBITDA; it exited Q2 2026 having raised that midpoint by $200M and explicitly cited power-innovation projects and data-center-linked demand as a driver. Energy Transfer entered 2026 guiding to $17.45B–$17.85B of adjusted EBITDA and exited Q2 2026 guiding to $18.8B–$19.1B — two raises in two quarters driven in part by NGL export expansion (NGL exports +25% year over year in Q2 2026) and long-term take-or-pay agreements extending into the 2030s. The shift from forecast to committed capital happened between mid-2025 and mid-2026, and it is now embedded in multiyear capital plans from which these companies cannot easily retreat.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Electricity customers in regulated Midwestern and Sun Belt markets where data-center load growth drives higher rate-base investment that utilities recover from all customers, not only from the data-center operators. Alliant explicitly argues its Iowa base electric rates stay below national averages, but the trajectory is higher capital per customer over time. Communities near new transmission routes, pipeline expansions, and gas-export facilities absorb siting disruption, construction impacts, and operating noise. Low-income households in service territories with heavy data-center concentration face the fastest rate-trajectory risk because the capex is socialized across the customer base regardless of income. Regions without data-center corridors see their relative share of national utility capital investment decline, creating a compounding geographic divergence in energy-system quality.
Who profits
Infrastructure operators with existing geographic footprints near emerging AI campus corridors — Williams Companies, Energy Transfer, Atmos Energy, Alliant Energy, NextEra Energy — whose incremental expansion cost against already-depreciated networks is a fraction of greenfield construction. Williams' Blackstone power-innovation joint venture, Socrates, and Power Express projects generate new revenue streams that layer on top of existing Transco tariffs. Energy Transfer's acquisition of J-W Power through USA Compression adds compression and generation capacity as a higher-margin service on top of commodity gas transport. NextEra's FPL regulatory capital base grew 9.3% year over year to $79.7B in Q2 2026, compounding at above-GDP rates without bearing AI technology risk. These companies hold the only legal option available to hyperscalers needing physical energy delivery.

The tension

The capex programs being committed now assume data-center demand will materialize on the timeline that utility resource plans project. Alliant's $13.4B capital plan through 2029 is sized for 3.4 GW of contracted demand and a 60% peak-load increase by 2031. Williams' $7B-plus of power-innovation capital is sized for gas demand that assumes AI compute keeps scaling and natural gas remains the dispatchable reliability layer. If AI buildout slows, if on-site nuclear or fuel-cell generation becomes cost-competitive before the capex is recovered, or if battery storage costs fall faster than expected, utilities and midstream operators cannot simply exit the investment. The regulatory compact that allows them to earn 6% to 8% returns also prevents them from writing off stranded assets without passing the cost to remaining customers. A 500 MW dedicated transmission line built for a hyperscaler that relocates or shuts down becomes a multi-decade rate-payer liability. State regulatory commissions must approve each capital recovery filing, and public pressure on rate increases or siting opposition can delay or block projects. The regulatory protection and the regulatory constraint are the same mechanism.

The surprising part

The surprising part is not that infrastructure operators benefit from AI — it is that AI growth has given them pricing power they did not have before. For decades, regulated utilities argued for capital investment by pointing to reliability, safety modernization, or mandated renewable integration. Regulators and consumer advocates pushed back. The data-center demand wave has changed that negotiation: a utility that can show 3.4 GW of signed customer contracts asking for 500 MW each has a straightforward case for transmission investment that regulators find hard to deny. Hyperscalers need the sites more than they need to fight the rate case. Williams can propose a power-innovation joint venture with Blackstone that turns gas-network access into a behind-the-meter data-center product, and data centers will negotiate rather than walk away, because there is no alternative gas network to walk to. The AI buildout has, inadvertently, resolved the long-standing regulatory friction that held utility capital programs at modest growth rates. The irony is that the most disrupted technology cycle in a generation is the best thing to happen to 20th-century pipe-and-wire operators in decades.

Why it matters

The energy infrastructure being expanded to serve AI is being funded through utility rate bases and long-term customer contracts rather than through direct technology capital markets. That means the capital allocation decisions of Williams, Energy Transfer, Atmos, Alliant, and NextEra — not the decisions of hyperscalers — determine where AI infrastructure physically locates and how quickly it can be built. A utility that cannot get regulatory approval for a transmission expansion in six months is a binding constraint on AI deployment in that geography. Regions without existing gas or transmission corridors near data-center corridors fall further behind as capital concentrates where the pipes and wires already run. Rate-payers in corridor regions fund the buildout and absorb the stranded-asset risk if demand assumptions prove wrong. The broader consequence is that AI's geographic footprint is being shaped by 20th-century infrastructure maps, and the companies that own those maps are collecting economic rents from a technology cycle whose gains will accrue elsewhere.

The owners of wires and gas pipes have become the silent co-investors in every data center being built — collecting regulated returns and long-term tariffs from hyperscalers who have no alternative route to electrons or molecules.

How We Got Here
Origin

The Reliability Gap That Handed Gas the Keys

Data-center buildout exposed a structural gap: intermittent renewables cannot guarantee continuous megawatts. Gas infrastructure, already built and depreciated, filled that gap immediately. Gas pipeline operators are now extracting structural rents on power reliability.

Four Ways to Collect the Toll
Sub-pattern

From Pipeline to Power Platform: The Gas-Transmission Play

Williams Companies is deliberately converting existing gas-pipeline infrastructure into a power-delivery platform for hyperscalers and industrial customers, transforming a toll-collection system into a multi-layer energy backbone that neither pure-pipeline nor pure-power competitors can replicate.

Sub-pattern

The Molecule Machine: NGL, Fractionation, and Export Corridor Control

Integrated NGL operators control every chokepoint from Permian gathering through Mont Belvieu fractionation to export docks—and long-term contracts extending into the 2030s lock both upstream producers and downstream consumers into their toll structure.

Sub-pattern

The Regional Utility as Data-Center Landlord

Regulated utilities with geographic proximity to AI campus corridors are converting large-load customer demand into decades of predictable capital expansion and rate-base growth, shifting construction and financing risk to ratepayers.

Sub-pattern

Gas Distribution as Regulated Capital Compounding

While electric utilities captured headlines on reliability and load growth, gas-distribution systems proved resilient through regulated capital compounding and rate-base growth. Atmos Energy and ONE Gas exemplify how natural-gas distribution remains economically viable despite energy-transition pressure. Both companies relied on rate relief, system-integrity capex, customer growth in constructive jurisdictions, and weather-normalization mechanisms to deliver EPS growth of 6-8% without commodity upside or volume acceleration. The sub-pattern shows that regulated gas infrastructure is not a sunset asset class; it is a steady capital-deployment machine where rate design and regulatory recovery matter more than gas demand growth.

Who Actually Pays for the AI Power Buildout

Utilities are deploying tens of billions in capex to serve data-center demand signed in 2025-2026, but the bill goes to residential ratepayers and small businesses, not to the hyperscalers benefiting most from the infrastructure.

The tension

The Stranded Asset Trap: What Breaks If AI Demand Disappoints

Infrastructure operators have committed tens of billions in capex betting that AI data-center load will materialize for decades. If hyperscaler growth slows, model efficiency improves, or policy shifts, that committed capex becomes stranded — absorbed by equity holders and ratepayers rather than the customers who signed the contracts.
