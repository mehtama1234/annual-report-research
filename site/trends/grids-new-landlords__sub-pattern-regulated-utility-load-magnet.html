The Regional Utility as Data-Center Landlord

Regulated utilities with geographic proximity to AI campus corridors are converting large-load customer demand into decades of predictable capital expansion and rate-base growth, shifting construction and financing risk to ratepayers.

Alliant Energy's Iowa and Wisconsin footprint illustrates how a mid-sized regulated utility becomes a data-center infrastructure landlord. With 3.4 GW of contracted large-load demand and a projected 60% peak-load increase by 2031, Alliant is building a $13.4B capital plan justified entirely by signed Electric Service Agreements with QTS, Google, and Meta. The regulated compact converts hyperscaler growth into predictable, ratepayer-funded returns.

The mechanism

The regulated utility model creates a unique value capture mechanism: the utility signs a long-term Electric Service Agreement with a hyperscaler, commits to build generation, transmission, and distribution infrastructure to serve that load, and recovers all costs through a combination of base rates, formula rates, and advance-ratemaking mechanisms. The load growth itself becomes the justification for capital expansion, and the capital expansion itself becomes the earnings driver.

Alliant's story makes the mechanism legible. Alliant had essentially no data-center load in 2023. By Q1 2026, it had 3.4 GW committed and was projecting 60% peak-load growth by 2031—a regime shift from the utility's decades-long single-digit growth pattern. Instead of competing on price or negotiating customer-specific rates, Alliant is using its regulatory compact in Iowa and Wisconsin to offer advance-ratemaking and individualized rate structures. This means the company can commit to capex before the risk of customer disconnection exists, and recover that capex cost across the broader ratepayer base. The utility's capital plan of $13.4B for 2026-2029 is entirely justified by these signed agreements; without the data-center load, capex would be roughly half that.

Duke Energy's example shows the same pattern at much larger scale. Duke signed approximately 2.7 GW of additional data-center ESAs in Q1 2026, bringing total secured data-center ESAs to roughly 7.6 GW (up from approximately 5 GW at year-end 2025). That 2.7 GW increase in a single quarter justifies a $103B five-year capital plan and 14 GW of new capacity by 2031. Duke is using regulatory tools (advance ratemaking, formula rates, investment-recovery mechanisms) to convert customer demand into earnings visibility. The key insight is that Duke can raise its earnings-per-share growth target to 5-7% through the decade specifically because the capital plan is justified by signed customer agreements—not by speculative demand forecasting. This is a structural shift: in 2023, Duke had negligible data-center demand; by Q1 2026, it had 7.6 GW committed.

The risk transfer is the hidden mechanism. A hyperscaler signs a long-term PPA or ESA, but actual site construction, zoning approval, and supply-chain execution remain the hyperscaler's problem. The utility's problem is different: it must build transmission, substations, and generation to match the timeline and capacity. If the hyperscaler delays or pulls back, the utility still owns the infrastructure. If construction costs rise (as they have 2023-2026), the utility absorbs the cost through rate recovery mechanisms. The utility's regulated status means it passes through costs to ratepayers—not shareholders. This is the structural change from 2023-2026: hyperscalers' demand jumped from unmeasurable to multi-GW, and utilities' regulatory frameworks moved from cost-containment to cost-pass-through for data-center loads.

Why now

Data-center and AI workload concentration in specific geographic regions (Iowa, the Carolinas, Ohio) became a structural inflection point in 2024-2025. Through 2023, hyperscalers were exploring sites and studying power requirements. By Q4 2024, announced deals and ESA signings accelerated sharply, and by Q1 2026, utilities were disclosing GW-scale commitments with multi-year build timelines. The timing is driven by: (1) hyperscalers committing multi-year capex plans and signing binding power agreements (2024-2025 inflection); (2) grid reliability becoming a competitive differentiator for data-center sites (solved 2024 onward); (3) renewable buildout requiring new transmission anyway, so utilities justified expansion by attributing it to data-center demand (2024 forward); (4) regulatory commissions became receptive to data-center load because it brings economic development and tax revenue (approval momentum 2024-2025). The specific inflection is Q1 2026, when both Alliant and Duke disclosed their hyperscaler agreements and updated capital plans, signaling this is not cyclical but structural. Utilities that control access points to data-center corridors are locking in decades of load growth and rate recovery. This is happening now because the convergence of AI demand, renewable transition, and regulatory permissiveness created a window of opportunity for utilities to expand the rate base at historically fast rates.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Existing and future ratepayers in Alliant's service territory (Iowa and Wisconsin) and Duke's service area (the Carolinas, Indiana, Ohio, Kentucky, and Florida) pay for the infrastructure build through rate increases. Alliant explicitly points to affordability as a constraint, saying it keeps Iowa base electric rates stable—but only through a narrow regulatory framework. If the utility cannot recover costs through rate recovery, ratepayers absorb any shortfall. Alliant's 60% projected load increase by 2031 requires proportional infrastructure scaling; that $13.4B capex shows the magnitude. Small businesses and low-income households in these regions face higher electricity costs as the utility's cost base expands. Regional gas utilities also pay an indirect cost: as power demand grows, natural-gas demand for peaking and baseload generation may compress, stranding gas infrastructure and shifting depreciation burdens.
Who profits
The regulated utility captures predictable returns through rate recovery and earnings growth tied to capital expansion. Alliant's 5-7% EPS growth target is tied directly to successful capex recovery; Duke's same target assumes $103B is fully recovered over five years. Hyperscalers benefit because they offload infrastructure risk to a regulated entity with guaranteed cost recovery. They can build data centers with near-certain power availability at contractually fixed rates (typically 20-30 year ESAs). Large shareholders of Alliant and Duke benefit from years of predictable EPS growth. Contractors and equipment suppliers benefit from the large, long-duration capex programs. Regulatory commissions benefit politically from economic development and tax revenue in their regions. But the entire gain distribution is one-directional: utilities capture returns on deployed capital, hyperscalers lock in low power costs, and ratepayers absorb all risk.

The tension

The first tension is stranded capacity risk. Utilities are building infrastructure for specific hyperscaler commitments signed in 2024-2026. If those customers re-evaluate, delay, or move to other regions, the infrastructure becomes underutilized. The cost of that underutilization gets socialized across all ratepayers. The second tension is that this model only works when regulatory commissions are permissive and affordable-rate constraints are loose. If rate pressures intensify or regulation tightens (particularly on affordability), utilities cannot expand capex at this pace without causing customer backlash or political intervention. A third tension: hyperscalers are shopping their load across multiple utilities (Google signed with Alliant, Duke, and others). A company like Alliant could build the infrastructure, but the hyperscaler could switch to a lower-cost region after signing, leaving Alliant with stranded assets. The final tension is that this model privileges large-load customers (data centers) over residential and small-business ratepayers in cost-allocation frameworks, deepening rate inequality.

The surprising part

The speed of load-growth recognition is remarkable. Alliant had essentially no data-center load in 2023; by Q1 2026, it had 3.4 GW committed and was projecting 60% peak-load growth. That is not a gradual forecast adjustment; it is a regime shift within 24-36 months. The utility has reoriented its entire five-year capital plan around it. The second surprise is how openly utilities are embracing the hyperscaler landlord role. There is no pretense of broad economic development or serving the regional economy. The growth is explicitly tied to specific data-center customers under contracts. Duke disclosed 7.6 GW of data-center ESAs with 5 GW under construction by Q1 2026, showing that the infrastructure build is already underway—this is not theoretical. The third surprise is that this is happening in lower-income Midwest regions (Iowa, parts of the Carolinas) where customers have less political power to resist rate increases. In 2023, these utilities projected 1-3% load growth; in 2026, they are projecting 5-7% through the 2030s. That acceleration is structural, not cyclical, and it is ratepayer-funded.

Why it matters

This model accelerates infrastructure inequality: regions with data-center activity get new generation, transmission, and grid modernization. Regions without it see slower infrastructure investment and relative decline. It also redistributes wealth from ratepayers to hyperscalers by locking in low power costs for large customers while bundling infrastructure costs across all ratepayers. For rural and small-business customers, it means rate increases to fund infrastructure they do not use directly. For employment, the data-center jobs are highly technical and capital-intensive, not labor-intensive. The local benefit is modest relative to the infrastructure and rate burden. Finally, it concentrates power-system risk: if hyperscalers consolidate their workloads or relocate en masse, entire regional power systems become stranded. A utility that commits to $13.4B capex based on 3.4 GW of hyperscaler demand faces existential risk if that demand evaporates after the capex is spent.

Regulated utilities in data-center corridors become landlords whose capital expansion and earnings growth are justified by hyperscaler commitments, with construction and financing risk transferred to ratepayers.
