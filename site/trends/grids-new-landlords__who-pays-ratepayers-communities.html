Who Actually Pays for the AI Power Buildout

Utilities are deploying tens of billions in capex to serve data-center demand signed in 2025-2026, but the bill goes to residential ratepayers and small businesses, not to the hyperscalers benefiting most from the infrastructure.

Data-center demand signed in 2024-2026 triggered visible capex acceleration at regulated utilities. Alliant Energy went from ~1.5 GW of contracted data-center load in 2024 to 3.4 GW by Q1 2026, driving a $13.4B capital plan for 2026-2029. AEP raised its five-year capital plan to $78B and now expects 69 GW of new load by 2030. NextEra Energy Resources added 13.5 GW of renewables and storage in 2025 alone to serve hyperscaler pipelines. Atmos Energy projects rate-base growth from $21.4B to $42B by fiscal 2030. This capex is approved and recovered through regulated rate bases that include all ratepayers—residential customers, small businesses, and the data centers themselves pay different per-megawatt-hour rates, but the infrastructure cost is spread through the regulatory system. Residential customers in Iowa, Ohio, and Florida are now financing grid expansion built primarily for Google, Meta, and Amazon, while the hyperscalers negotiate below-market rates backed by the utility's cost-of-service guarantee.

The mechanism

Regulated utilities recover capex through state-approved rate bases. When Alliant Energy signed new data-center agreements in 2024-2025, adding 1.9 GW to its portfolio, it did not charge those customers directly for the transmission lines and substations needed to serve them. Instead, Alliant filed a capital plan with regulators, arguing that large-load growth required immediate infrastructure investment. Iowa and Wisconsin utility commissions approved the plan, and Alliant added the capex to its rate base. The cost then spread across all ratepayers: residential customers in Cedar Rapids pay the same per-kilowatt-hour rates regardless of whether those electrons serve their home or a data center outside town. Alliant's $13.4B plan for 2026-2029 is funded this way. A household using 10,000 kWh per year now pays a proportional share of the substation upgrades and transmission buildout that primarily serve data centers.

Affordability arguments mask the cross-subsidy. Utilities argue that adding large-load customers reduces per-unit fixed costs, making electricity cheaper for everyone. This is technically true if demand grows faster than costs. But the framing hides the fact that residential rates rise to recover capex that creates value for hyperscalers. AEP explicitly stated in Q2 2026 that it expected $16B of 'customer cost offsets' and $1.4B of 'customer savings' from DOE loans and grants—language that conflates federal subsidy of hyperscaler infrastructure with affordability gains for a household in Columbus. NextEra Energy Resources sold 13.5 GW of new generation and storage projects in 2025; those projects will be built, financed, and recovered through utility rates. The marketing pitch is 'lower per-kilowatt costs through scale.' The reality is that homeowners are financing the power plants themselves.

The regulatory structure guarantees cost recovery for utilities and creates perverse incentives to overinvest. State Public Utilities Commissions approve rates based on a utility's cost-of-service plus a regulated return on rate-base investment. When AEP files to add $78B of capex over five years, regulators face a choice: approve the investment and let rates rise, or deny it and risk grid inadequacy or losing large-load customers to neighboring states. Regulators consistently choose approval because rejecting grid investment has immediate political costs (outages, competitiveness, job losses). The result is that capex gets approved, rate bases grow (Atmos's rate base is growing 96% in four years), and residential rates climb to fund infrastructure they did not request. The utility earns a guaranteed return on the investment. Shareholders profit. Regulators face pressure to approve whatever the utility deems 'needed' for reliability and load growth.

Why now

Three forces converged in 2024-2026. First, data-center and AI-related demand became visible and contractible. Alliant saw contracted load jump from roughly 1.5 GW in 2024 to 3.4 GW by Q1 2026—more than a doubling in 18 months. AEP's signed load agreements went from 28 GW in October 2024 to 56 GW by Q4 2025 to 69 GW by mid-2026. These are not forecasts; they are signed contracts with Google, Meta, and hyperscalers. Second, electricity scarcity and prices became obvious in 2023-2025. ERCOT faced tight supply, PJM issued reliability warnings, and solar/wind supply gaps appeared. Utilities became confident they could file for large capex increases and get them approved because 'reliability' had become a politically salient issue. Third, state governments shifted economic-development priorities to attract data centers and large industrial load. Texas, Ohio, Iowa, and Florida began competing for hyperscaler facilities. Public utility commissions became more receptive to arguments that large-load customers drive affordability and community growth. All three forces—contractible demand, electricity scarcity, and state economic priorities—made utilities willing to deploy tens of billions in capex and confident they could recover it through regulated rates that include residential and small-business ratepayers.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Residential ratepayers and small businesses without negotiating power. A household in Iowa using 10,000 kWh per year pays a share of Alliant's $13.4B capex proportional to its consumption. A small business in Ohio pays its share of AEP's $78B capital plan. Neither signed a data-center demand agreement. Neither negotiated favorable rate terms. Neither receives direct benefit from hyperscaler infrastructure. Their rates rise. Communities near transmission lines and substations absorb construction disruption, environmental risk, and property-value impacts—without compensation or direct access to the power generated.
Who profits
Hyperscalers and utility shareholders. Hyperscalers sign large-load demand agreements at favorable rates because they have scale, credit quality, and the ability to shop for jurisdictions. They negotiate rates often 30-40% below residential per-megawatt-hour prices because the utility needs their long-term revenue certainty and the regulatory credibility that comes from a signed contract. The infrastructure is built for them first. Residential rates adjust upward to fund it. Utility shareholders profit through rate-base growth. AEP's rate base is expected to grow 11% annually from new load additions. Atmos's rate base is growing 96% between fiscal 2025 and fiscal 2030. In a regulated-utility model, rate-base growth translates directly to earnings growth and shareholder returns. The more capex gets approved, the higher the rate base, the higher the guaranteed return.

The tension

The cross-subsidy model depends on political durability and opacity. Residential ratepayers often do not see the connection between rising bills and data-center infrastructure. Regulators benefit from the narrative that new load supports affordability. Utilities benefit from the certainty that capex gets approved. But the model breaks if ratepayers become aware of the transfer and demand cost-allocation rules that require large-load customers to pay directly for dedicated infrastructure. Some states may impose data-center-specific surcharges or 'load-specific capital recovery' rules. This would eliminate the cross-subsidy and make hyperscaler economics less attractive, potentially slowing infrastructure buildout or forcing utilities to negotiate more transparent rate structures. The political risk is real but not yet visible because the per-household bill impact ($5-15 per month additional) is modest and the data-center job narrative is still politically powerful.

The surprising part

The burden-transfer is not hidden or accidental—it is explicit in regulatory filings and investor presentations. AEP's Q2 2026 investor update states directly that new load will reduce per-unit costs for all customers. NextEra's annual materials describe the backlog as infrastructure that 'serves America's next wave of electricity demand.' Utilities are comfortable with this framing because it aligns with state development priorities, passes regulatory scrutiny, and has proven politically durable. What is truly surprising is that residential ratepayers do not organize more strongly to resist, and that regulators do not impose stronger cost-allocation mechanisms. The answer appears to be threefold: the individual household bill impact is modest enough to avoid attention, regulatory political economy favors utilities over ratepayers, and the jobs narrative (data centers create tax base and employment) is politically powerful enough to overcome fairness objections. Put another way, the system works as designed—it transfers cost from politically weak homeowners to politically powerful data-center operators—but does so incrementally enough that the transfer remains largely invisible.

Why it matters

Electricity affordability is being systematically transferred from hyperscalers to price-sensitive households. A family that uses electricity efficiently to lower its bills now finds rates rising to fund infrastructure that benefits a tech company's data center. This widens inequality between those who can negotiate utility-scale rates (large industrials, hyperscalers) and those who pay standard residential tariffs (most households, small businesses). The mechanism—regulated utility cost-of-service—was designed to spread costs fairly and ensure universal service. It is now being weaponized to transfer wealth from ratepayers to capital-intensive corporations backed by deep-pocketed tech companies. When ratepayers eventually understand this, faith in regulated utility models may erode, creating political pressure for utility deregulation or for rate structures that the industry cannot control through lobbying.

Utilities are financing the infrastructure buildout that powers AI data centers through residential electricity rates, while hyperscalers negotiate below-cost service and utility shareholders pocket the rate-base growth.
