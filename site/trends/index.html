Bottom-up trend analysis

What 519 companies are actually telling us

Trends surfaced bottom-up from 519 company annual reports and the latest earnings — open-coded, clustered, and written up in depth. 20 distinct dynamics, 168 deep-dive pages, every figure traced to a filing.

The 2025 economy is not reorganizing around new growth — it is extracting premium pricing from the assets, customers, and infrastructure it already built, and the companies that optimized for extraction instead of innovation are leaving competitors behind.

1. The End of Reinvestment

For three decades, the operating assumption was that profitable companies reinvest surplus revenue into the next cycle: better products, new markets, faster delivery. That cycle is ending. Cash Harvest companies — energy, healthcare, financial services, consumer staples — have concluded that their customer bases are captive enough to support price increases routed directly to shareholders through buybacks instead of reinvestment. The 2025 data confirms they are right. ConocoPhillips returned $9.0 billion to shareholders while implementing 49% automated ore extraction at Nutrien. These are not edge cases — they are the template. The winners are companies that own structures customers cannot leave: utilities, regulated platforms, prescription dependencies. The middle shelf — companies that had to compete on value or innovation — is hollowed out.

This is happening across sectors. Bloomingdale's runs +10.2% comps while Macy's runs +1.6% in the same quarter. HOKA grows 23.6% as Nike Direct contracts 9%. The bifurcation is not preference — it is constraint. The affluent can pay for premium; the middle absorbs the cuts. Gannett's profit paradox — first profit since 2019 on falling revenue — is the honest version of this story. Digital progress is real. Structural collapse is faster.

2. The Three Moats That Replaced Innovation

Companies stopped competing on what they build and started competing on what they own or what they learned to extract. The Vertical Integration Ratchet captures the first: companies that acquired inputs on cheap debt between 2022 and 2024 now run those assets hard to justify the capital — and the winners are not the largest integrators but the ones whose inputs carry structural advantages competitors cannot replicate. The Spread Machine is the second: companies that own nothing worth owning and innovate nothing worth patenting, but compete on the prohibitive cost of building around them. These are cable operators, payment platforms, landlords of essential infrastructure. The third is psychological: The Trust Market, where institutions that built verifiable credibility over decades are converting it into premium pricing power as AI makes unverified alternatives cheap to produce and expensive to trust.

What ties these together is that none of them require the company to outinnovate the next competitor. They require the company to foreclose competition itself. Own the scarce input. Make it ruinously expensive to route around you. Or be the only source customers believe. The Gray Wave Dividend and The Grid's New Landlords follow the same logic: Medicare is paying for the shift of chronic care to living rooms, and the companies that built the logistics to serve that shift are collecting recurring revenue on demographic certainty. Hyperscalers committed capital simultaneously, and now utilities own the wires running power to every data center. Both are moats built on scarcity and regulatory tailwinds, not innovation.

3. When Customers Become Involuntary

The clearest pattern across the 20 trends is the systematic conversion of customer choice into captured behavior. The Addiction Dividend names this directly: tobacco, streaming, pet care, gaming, beverages, mobility. The highest-margin businesses of the 2020s are those that converted consumer choice into automated habit, then disclosed that behavioral capture to investors as the primary moat. The Ritual Economy is the softer version: post-2022 rate compression sorted consumer spending into price-inelastic rituals tied to daily identity or life milestones, and volume-stable essentials, while aspirational categories absorbed the cuts. Chewy, Colgate, Starbucks held price and volume. Discretionary goods collapsed.

This captures every other trend as well. The Presence Premium extracts scarcity: infinite on-demand content made presence — being in the same room at the same time — the only thing left that cannot be reproduced cheaply. Venues own that scarcity. Membership or Nothing formalizes the extraction: the best prices, fastest delivery, and highest quality now sit behind a fee gate, and non-members subsidize members' deals through full-freight pricing. The Screening Revolution is not clinical breakthrough — it is behavior redesign that replaced a procedure patients avoided with a mail-in kit they tolerate, and the companies that made that substitution are growing at rates the rest of healthcare cannot match. The workers' Invisible Tax completes the picture: labor shortages are choices by profitable companies to automate workers rather than pay market-clearing wages, and the proof is visible in shareholder returns and ore extraction automation while no comparable infrastructure absorbs the displaced workers.

4. The Toll and the Colonization

Two trends describe the structural instability beneath this extraction economy. The AI Infrastructure Toll captures the first: hyperscalers are paying premium rates for the physical layer of AI — licensed labor, aggregates, backup power, precision circuit boards — because they committed capital simultaneously into a pool of contractors and materials that cannot scale as fast as a software budget. This is a temporary advantage for suppliers, not a sustainable moat. The second is deeper: The Private Capital Colonization describes how $2-3 trillion annual flows of insurance float, pension capital, and consumer credit have left the regulated banking system for private platforms charging 20x higher fees, facing no deposit insurance requirements, and invisible to stress-test regulators. The Commodity Whiplash Transfer routes risk to the tier with the least ability to push it downstream.

The Outage Anxiety Economy and AI Cultural Disintermediation reveal the psychological underside. Fear of failure is more durable than actual failure frequency — companies learned to sell backup electricity as insurance and are now repricing it as infrastructure. And the companies that own the AI layer are not replacing experts with cheaper labor — they are absorbing the cultural authority of those experts and making it proprietary to an algorithm that improves with every decision, so professionals who held that authority can never price it independently again. These are not innovations. They are captures.

Winners
Companies that own scarce physical inputs (utilities, mining operators, regulated infrastructure), captured behavioral customer bases (streaming, tobacco, pet care, healthcare prescription dependencies), verified credibility platforms (financial identity, government contracting), high-capacity fixed venues (live entertainment, real estate), and integrated supply chains with structural input advantages that competitors cannot replicate without rebuilding their asset base. Also: private capital platforms charging 20x higher fees with no regulatory oversight.
Who pays
The middle shelf — retailers, brands, and service providers that had to compete on value or innovation without owning scarce inputs or capturing behavioral lock-in. Discretionary goods manufacturers. Workers in mining, landscaping, packaging, and routine professional roles targeted by automation while no infrastructure absorbs their displacement. Local journalism. Traditional healthcare delivery models competing with behavior-redesigned screenings. Professionals whose cultural authority is being absorbed into proprietary AI systems. Tier-two suppliers of commodity materials and labor bearing the whiplash from price swings they cannot push downstream. Banks and regulators losing visibility into $2-3 trillion annual flows moving to unmonitored private platforms.
The 2025 economy is not reorganizing around new growth — it is extracting premium pricing from the assets, customers, and infrastructure already built, and the companies that optimized for extraction instead of innovation are leaving competitors behind.

The Cash Harvest

In energy, healthcare, consumer staples, and financial services, companies with captive customer bases are raising prices on people who cannot easily leave, then routing every dollar of excess cash to shareholders instead of workers, communities, or long-cycle assets. The discipline looks like financial virtue. The distribution of its costs is not.

The Spread Machine

The most durable profit engines in the 2026 economy are not innovators but intermediaries — companies that sit between producers and consumers at chokepoints so expensive to replicate that pricing power has nothing to do with service quality and everything to do with the cost of building around them.

The Vertical Integration Ratchet

Industrial platforms bought their supply chains between 2022 and 2024 when debt was cheap and commodity exposure was untenable. Now the leverage is fixed and the moats are structural—but so are the failure modes, and the companies that look strongest are not the biggest integrators but the ones with input advantages their competitors cannot buy.

The Trust Market

The Addiction Dividend

The Presence Premium

Algorithmic content has made on-demand video a commodity — and made being in the same room at the same time genuinely scarce. The companies that control fixed-capacity live experiences are converting that scarcity into deferred-revenue moats and operating margins that infinite-content businesses cannot touch.

The Affordability Ceiling

The Workers' Invisible Tax

The Pet Economy's Mirror

The Grid's New Landlords

Gas pipelines and regulated transmission operators are extracting structural economic rents from the AI buildout — tolling electrons and molecules that hyperscalers cannot route around — while bearing none of the technology risk that defines every other layer of the AI stack.

Membership or Nothing

Across mobility, media, pet food, and financial services, the best prices and fastest delivery now sit behind a fee gate — and the companies that built that gate are using non-member revenue to subsidize member pricing, creating two parallel economies under one brand.

The Commodity Whiplash Transfer

When nitrogen prices spike or cocoa costs surge, integrated giants no longer absorb the pain — they pass it downstream through contracts engineered to shift risk onto the next tier. The farmer, the packager, the small contractor, and the household bear the hit so CF Industries, Glencore, and Freeport-McMoRan do not have to.

The Private Capital Colonization

Alternative asset managers have absorbed trillions in insurance float, pension obligations, and retail savings since 2022 — capturing fee structures 20x higher than regulated banks for identical economic risk, while remaining invisible to the regulatory safety net built to handle the next crisis.
