When Accountability Stops: Local News Collapse Leaves Communities Operating Without Scrutiny

EchoStar's going-concern warning and subscriber collapse (6.998M to 6.391M in 18 months), Gannett's digital-only mix hitting 47.4% of revenue, and iHeartMedia's $209M license-value impairment show that the economics of local accountability journalism have inverted. Digital ads don't monetize, subscriptions don't scale locally, and news companies have stopped investing in the institutional scrutiny that kept government, courts, and schools accountable.

Local news collapsed not from lack of audience but from economics: the business model that funded accountability journalism (print classifieds, broadcast advertising, geographic reach) broke between 2015-2020. That destruction accelerated 2020-2026. Digital advertising pays a fraction of print rates. Subscription models work for national brands (NYT's 13.35M subscribers) but not local news. Neither Gannett, EchoStar, nor iHeartMedia rebuilt business models at scale to fund local newsrooms. Instead, all three contracted. Fewer journalists cover courts, police, schools, and city councils. Communities lose institutional memory and the overhead cost that kept institutions honest—the knowing that a reporter might show up.

The mechanism

Local news economics broke along a specific timeline. Print advertising supported local newsrooms because classified ads, auto dealers, and real estate businesses needed local reach to function. When Craigslist, Zillow, and digital directories captured those advertisers (2008-2015), local newspaper revenue collapsed. Broadcasting economics lasted longer because broadcast reached large geographic areas and political advertising remained tied to local elections. But streaming and cord-cutting accelerated (2015-2020), and then COVID compressed adoption (2020-2022). EchoStar's 607,000 pay-TV subscriber loss from FY 2025 to Q2 2026 is not slowing; it is accelerating. That destruction of eyeballs means broadcast advertising collapses too. iHeartMedia's $209M license impairment (Q3 2025) is the market saying radio broadcast licenses have no value anymore.

The subscription model has not replaced that revenue at local scale. Gannett has 1.5 million digital-only subscriptions, which is substantial. But the company's total revenue fell because the print base was far larger. Gannett's 47.4% digital mix (Q2 2026) looks like progress until you remember: that mix reflects decline in the traditional base, not explosive growth in digital. The company earned $114.4M operating cash flow on $2.3B revenue (5% margin)—sustainable, but not rebuilt at scale. A 5% operating margin cannot fund the newsroom investment Gannett once made. Fewer reporters, fewer beats covered, less accountability journalism produced.

The consequence cascades invisibly. Newsrooms contracted. Beats that once had dedicated reporters (city council, courts, police, schools) are now covered part-time or not at all. Breaking-news coverage in smaller cities disappeared. That is not a side effect; it is the core mechanism. The cost center (newsroom) was cut because matching revenue growth didn't materialize. Without those reporters, accountability mechanisms stopped functioning. City governments approve projects with less due diligence because no one is watching. School districts make decisions without scrutiny. Police departments operate less transparently. Courts process cases without observers. Corruption and mismanagement go uncovered longer because the institutional check—the reporter who might show up—is gone. Citizens don't know these things are happening because no one is reporting them. This cost is externalized from media companies onto communities.

Why now

The inflection was 2020-2024. COVID accelerated digital migration and streaming adoption, which compressed pay-TV and broadcast listenership simultaneously. Advertisers who were 60% digital before became 90% digital. Local classifieds had already moved online years before. The transition that could have been managed over 20 years was forced into 5. Companies did not have time to rebuild business models at scale before revenue disappeared. EchoStar's going-concern language (FY 2025 annual report) is the hard inflection: the company cannot fund operations and debt service from current revenues without asset sales or restructuring. That is not hypothetical; it is a filing statement required by accounting standards. When a public company discloses substantial doubt about going concern, the business model is broken, not in transition.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Communities pay through degraded civic oversight, loss of institutional memory, and reduced accountability for government, courts, schools, and businesses. Residents pay through worse government decisions, more frequent institutional failures, and higher ultimate costs (from waste, corruption, or poor policy). Individual journalists lose jobs or are forced into contract work with no benefits or pension. News sources lose their largest platform and reach. The cost is not paid by media companies; they are simply abandoning the work.
Who profits
Tech platforms (Google, Facebook/Meta, TikTok) capture advertising spend that once went to local media. There is no alternative local beneficiary. National premium direct-subscription media (NYT's 13.35M subscribers) captures audience and ad spend, but that is concentrated in national and international news, not local accountability reporting.

The tension

Local accountability journalism is a public good with no private market mechanism to fund it at the scale it once was. Subscription models work for national/premium brands with differentiated content (NYT, WSJ). Advertising still funds some forms of media (programmatic digital display), but not local newsrooms because CPMs are 1/10th of what print classifieds or broadcast advertising paid. Philanthropic models and nonprofit newsrooms are emerging but do not replace the scale of traditional local news. The economic tension is irreversible under current technology and advertising models—there is no path back to the prior business model.

The surprising part

The most counter-intuitive fact is that audience still exists. Gannett reached 186 million average monthly unique visitors in 2025. That is massive reach. But massive reach does not monetize at the scale required to fund local newsrooms. Digital advertising CPMs are a fraction of what print classified or broadcast advertising once paid. So media companies serve massive audiences at declining per-unit revenue. The scale paradox—huge reach, declining business value—is the defining feature of modern media attempting to operate without subscription scale or platform control.

Why it matters

This pattern is fundamentally about the conditions for democratic accountability and institutional transparency. Without local news, local government and institutions operate with less scrutiny, more waste, and less responsiveness to community feedback. School boards make decisions without observing reporters. Police departments operate without press coverage. Courts have fewer observers. Businesses engage in practices that would have been reported in an earlier era. Waste, corruption, and mismanagement go longer unchecked because the institutional overhead—the knowing that someone might report it—is gone. This is not a media industry problem; it is a civic infrastructure problem that shows up in higher government waste, slower response to problems, and reduced civic participation.

Local news collapsed not because communities stopped needing accountability reporting, but because digital advertising fragmentation made that reporting economically impossible to fund, leaving governance and institutions operating without meaningful public scrutiny.
