Trends · The Local News Collapse

National Scale Works. Local Scale Doesn't.

The New York Times proves people will pay for journalism — and in the same breath proves nothing about whether a city can fund coverage of its own school board.

The subscriber-supported journalism model is not broken; it is simply arithmetic that only closes at global scale. The New York Times turns $2.825 billion in revenue and 12.78 million subscribers into $550 million of free cash flow because it amortizes a fixed newsroom across every English reader on earth. A paper covering one mid-sized city's zoning board has the same cost structure and one one-thousandth of the addressable audience — so the exact model that mints money at the Times cannot, by construction, fund local accountability journalism.

The mechanism

Journalism is a fixed-cost business wearing variable-cost clothing. The reporting, editing, legal review, and production of a story cost the same whether one person reads it or ten million do. The New York Times has engineered its entire strategy around that single truth: build one high-value bundle — News, The Athletic, Cooking, Games, Wirecutter, Audio — and sell it to the largest possible audience. At 12.78 million subscribers and a digital ARPU that climbed from $9.72 to $9.94 over recent quarters, the fixed cost of the newsroom is spread so thin per reader that the marginal subscriber is almost pure margin. That is how a news company posts a 19.5% free-cash-flow margin.

Now hold the cost structure constant and shrink the audience. A paper covering a single city's council meetings, court docket, and school budget carries the same fixed reporting cost per beat — a reporter still has to sit through the three-hour zoning hearing — but its addressable market is capped at the literate, paying adults within that metro. There is no global English audience for the Springfield sewer bond. The denominator collapses while the numerator holds, and the arithmetic that produces $550 million at the Times produces a loss at the local level.

Gannett is the natural experiment. It runs the same subscription playbook — 1.5 million digital-only subscriptions, 186 million average monthly unique visitors, digital now 46% of a $2.302 billion revenue base — and it worked hard enough to post its first positive annual net income since the 2019 merger. Yet its free cash flow was $64.2 million, a 2.8% margin, against a $988.3 million debt load. The same model, applied to fragmented local markets, generates one-seventh of the Times's cash efficiency. The scarcity the Times monetizes — trusted information at global reach — simply does not exist for a company selling 200 local mastheads one small market at a time.

Nexstar shows the only local model that actually prints cash, and it proves the point by inversion. Nexstar earns $4.949 billion in revenue and a 31.5% adjusted-EBITDA margin — but its $2.924 billion of distribution revenue is retransmission fees extracted from cable and streaming carriers, not readers paying for journalism. The civic-information layer is a byproduct that lets Nexstar 'monetize heavily when elections drive demand,' with a $233 million political-advertising swing between quarters. Local broadcast survives on carriage economics and election cycles, not on a community's willingness to pay for its own accountability coverage.

Why now

The New York Times has spent five years being cited as proof that the subscription model 'saved journalism.' In FY2025 it made that case irrefutable at the national tier: $2.825 billion in revenue, 12.78 million subscribers, digital ad revenue growing 20-31% quarter over quarter, and a bundle explicitly designed to build 'valuable daily habits and lifelong direct relationships worth paying for.' But 2025 is also the year the local counter-evidence became undeniable — Gannett squeezed out its first net-positive year since 2019 and still generated only $64.2 million of free cash flow against nearly a billion in debt. The two facts arrived at the same time, and together they retire the idea that the Times is a template anyone local can copy.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Residents of the roughly one in five American counties that are now news deserts or nearly so. They pay in the currency of what goes uncovered: the zoning variance granted with no reporter in the room, the school budget passed without scrutiny, the county contract steered to a commissioner's cousin. The Times subscription proves people will pay for journalism they can get — it says nothing about the journalism no one is left to produce.
Who profits
The New York Times, which converts the collapse of everyone else's model into a wider moat — 'information trust is becoming a monetizable scarcity,' and scarcity accrues to the survivor. Nexstar profits at the local level by monetizing carriage and political ad cycles rather than journalism itself. Both capture the value of trust; neither business model transfers to a paper whose only possible audience is a single city that will never scale.

The tension

The success story is the trap. Every time the Times posts another record — 12.78 million subscribers, $550 million in cash — it reinforces a policy assumption that the market has solved journalism, which removes urgency from the subsidy, foundation, or public-funding mechanisms that local accountability reporting actually requires. National profitability is being read as an all-clear for a local sector that is structurally incapable of reaching it.

The surprising part

The healthiest local-news balance sheet in the group — Nexstar's $1.561 billion in adjusted EBITDA — belongs to a company whose journalism is almost incidental to how it makes money. Its cash comes from $2.924 billion in retransmission fees paid by carriers, not from anyone paying for the news. The one local model that works financially works because it does not depend on selling journalism at all.

Why it matters

If you live anywhere that is not a global media capital, the lesson of the Times's success is that the market will not fund the coverage of your local government — because the math that works for a worldwide audience mechanically fails for a single town. The accountability that keeps your local officials honest is not a business that scales down, and pretending it is leaves your community blind.

The New York Times didn't prove the subscription model saves journalism — it proved the model only closes at planetary scale, which is precisely the scale a school-board beat can never reach.
