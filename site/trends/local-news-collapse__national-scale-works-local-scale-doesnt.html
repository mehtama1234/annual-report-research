Trends · The Local News Collapse

Reader Pay Only Works at Global Scale

The New York Times proves people will pay for journalism when one newsroom's cost is spread across a worldwide audience. A school-board beat can never reach that scale, which is why local reader-pay does not work.

A newsroom is a fixed cost. Reader subscriptions only cover it once that cost is amortized across a large enough paying audience, and the only English-language audience large enough is global. The New York Times has it; a local paper structurally cannot. Gannett runs the identical subscription playbook across fragmented local markets and converts far less of its revenue into cash. Nexstar is the one local model that reliably prints cash, and it does so on distribution fees, not reader subscriptions, which proves the point by inversion.

The mechanism

The New York Times pays for one global newsroom and spreads that fixed cost across a worldwide English-speaking audience. In 2025 it turned 12.78 million total subscribers and $1.951 billion of subscription revenue into $2.825 billion of total revenue and $550.5 million of free cash flow. Management frames the strategy as owning daily habits and the direct billing relationship across news, The Athletic, Cooking, Games, Wirecutter, and Audio.

A local school-board beat serves a market of tens of thousands, not tens of millions, so the same fixed newsroom cost has almost no audience to amortize against. Reader-pay math that closes for a global publisher never closes locally, no matter how good the journalism.

Gannett runs the identical subscription-and-digital playbook across fragmented local markets. In 2025 it reached about 1.5 million digital-only subscriptions and 186 million average unique visitors, but converted $2.302 billion of revenue into only $64.2 million of free cash flow and $1.7 million of net income, its first positive annual net income since the 2019 merger. Against NYT's $550.5 million of free cash flow, the same playbook at local scale yields a fraction of the cash, and Gannett carries $988.3 million of debt principal (Q1 2026) that NYT does not.

Nexstar is the only local model that reliably prints cash, and it does so by inverting the reader-pay premise. Its 2025 cash engine is $2.924 billion of distribution revenue, retransmission and carriage fees paid by cable and streaming distributors, not subscriptions paid by local readers. That distribution leg produced $1.561 billion of adjusted EBITDA in 2025 and stayed almost flat year over year even as advertising fell.

Nexstar's advertising leg shows how fragile the non-distribution local dollar is: Q4 2025 political advertising dropped $233 million to just $21 million once the election year ended, pushing the quarter to a net loss. Local cash rides on carriage contracts and political cycles, not on households paying for the local newsroom.

Why now

The 2025 annual chain lines up all three models in the same window. The New York Times closed 2025 with $1.951 billion of subscription revenue, 12.78 million subscribers, and $550.5 million of free cash flow. Gannett, running the same subscription playbook locally, reached its first positive annual net income since the 2019 merger at just $1.7 million and $64.2 million of free cash flow on nearly the same $2.302 billion revenue base. Nexstar's local model printed $1.561 billion of adjusted EBITDA, but on $2.924 billion of distribution fees, not reader subscriptions, and its Q4 2025 political-ad line fell $233 million to $21 million the moment the election cycle ended. Three newspaper-and-broadcast businesses, one profitable global reader-pay model, one barely-breakeven local reader-pay model, and one cash-rich local model that does not depend on readers at all.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Global readers fund the NYT newsroom directly through 12.78 million subscriptions. Local Gannett readers are asked to fund newsrooms the same way but cannot generate comparable cash, so the shortfall is covered by debt and by legacy print that keeps shrinking. Nexstar's local newsrooms are effectively funded by cable and streaming distributors through carriage fees, with readers paying nothing directly.
Who profits
The New York Times captures the full economics of global reader-pay, converting subscriptions into $550.5 million of free cash flow. Nexstar profits from local relevance without relying on reader-pay, monetizing carriage rights and political-ad cycles into $1.561 billion of adjusted EBITDA. Gannett, stuck running reader-pay at local scale, captures the least, $64.2 million of free cash flow against a $988.3 million debt load.

The tension

Everyone agrees communities need local journalism, and audiences still show up: Gannett draws 186 million unique visitors. But the one business model that pays for journalism, direct reader subscriptions, only closes at global scale, and the one local model that prints cash pays for a lot of things other than the local school-board beat.

The surprising part

The only local-news business that reliably makes money makes it by not selling journalism to readers at all. Nexstar's cash comes from $2.924 billion in distribution fees paid by cable and streaming carriers, so its local newsrooms are subsidized by carriage contracts, and a single political-ad swing of $233 million in one quarter moves the business more than any subscriber decision ever could.

Why it matters

If reader-pay journalism only works at global scale, the local school-board and city-hall coverage that democracy depends on has no self-sustaining business model, since the audience that would fund it is too small to amortize a newsroom and the only profitable local model funds itself on carriage fees and election-year ad cycles instead of local reporting.

People pay for journalism only when one newsroom's cost is spread across a global audience, which is why the Times prints $550 million of free cash flow, Gannett running the same playbook locally scrapes $64 million, and the one local model that reliably makes money, Nexstar, does it on distribution fees rather than readers.
