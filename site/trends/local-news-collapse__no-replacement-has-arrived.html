Trends · The Local News Collapse

The Replacement That Has Not Arrived

Every attempted substitute for the local advertising model has failed to close the gap at scale: digital subscriptions work only for premium national brands, out-of-home advertising captures attention without funding original reporting, and podcast revenue is concentrated in entertainment not civic accountability.

The structural gap between what local journalism costs and what any current business model can fund is widening, not narrowing. Digital audio, podcasting, out-of-home displays, and open-web advertising platforms are all growing profitably—but none of them are funding investigative reporting about city councils, schools, courts, or local businesses. The gap is not being filled; it is being bypassed.

The mechanism

The advertising-supported local news model of the 20th century rested on a simple monopoly: if you wanted to reach your town, you bought ads in the local newspaper or on local radio. That monopoly extracted enough surplus to fund reporters, editors, and the cost of original reporting about local government, courts, and business. The cost structure was real—investigative journalism is expensive—but the revenue floor was almost guaranteed. When digital media fragmented audience attention, that floor collapsed.

What has replaced it is not a new journalism model but a redistribution of the same advertising attention into faster-moving, measurable, and more algorithmic surfaces. Taboola and Outbrain/Teads have grown into $1.9B and $1.3B revenue businesses by sitting between advertisers and the remnants of publisher inventory, including local news sites. But the revenue model is fundamentally different: these platforms take a middle cut (traffic acquisition costs) and pass the rest back to publishers as a commodity price. A local news site that once charged $500 for a print ad now makes cents-per-click through a recommendation widget. The scale is there, but the margin per story is not.

iHeartMedia's podcast segment—$147M in Q1 2026, up 27% year-over-year—shows where audio attention is consolidating: entertainment, personality, music, and national shows. The company's 860+ broadcast stations still exist, but the growth story is measurable digital audio and podcasts, not local news radio. Clear Channel Outdoor's $1.6B annual revenue in out-of-home advertising is a thriving business, but billboards and transit displays do not employ investigative reporters. They are attention capture divorced from information production. The platform and ad-tech companies are profitable precisely because they optimize for traffic flow and attribution, not for the expensive work of original reporting that has no immediate performance metric.

This is the paradox at the heart of the collapse: advertising spending on digital has grown significantly, but it has been redirected toward formats that do not require newsrooms. A news site that generates traffic through SEO-optimized stories and recommendation networks can earn partial credit for that traffic value. But the economic floor under original reporting—the part that requires weeks of investigation, legal risk, and expertise—has no direct revenue counterweight. The gap between the cost of local journalism and the revenue any single model generates is not shrinking. It is being exposed as permanent. Digital substitutes are succeeding as traffic engines and attention brokers while failing as funding mechanisms for civic accountability.

Why now

The gap has always existed in principle, but three structural changes have made it catastrophic. First, the ad-tech and platform layer has matured into industrial scale. Taboola's $1.9B revenue and Outbrain's participation in $1.3B of platform revenue would not have been possible a decade ago. These businesses proved that attention could be monetized without funding original content. Second, local advertising budgets—which once had to concentrate locally because of geography—can now be served programmatically at national scale, making local publishers price-takers rather than price-setters. Third, the measurement and attribution arms race (Taboola's 28.8% adjusted EBITDA margin, Outbrain's focus on full-funnel outcomes) has rewired advertiser expectations around what counts as valuable attention. A local news report about a zoning board is invisible to these measurement systems. An entertainment podcast with precise listener attribution is not. The window has closed because the economic evidence is now undeniable. In 2010, it was still possible to argue that digital models would eventually replace print revenue. By 2026, the direction is clear: the open web, podcasting, out-of-home, and video platforms are all growing profitably without requiring journalists. The companies pursuing these models have no economic incentive to fund expensive local reporting. They profit from attention fragmentation, not from solving it.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Advertisers pay into a fragmented system they do not fully understand or control. Small businesses that need local reach (plumbers, dentists, real estate agents) now bid against national campaigns for the same digital inventory, paying more for less targeted exposure. National consumer brands pay Clear Channel, iHeartMedia, Taboola, and Outbrain because these platforms can still measure and deliver attention at scale, but that scale is achieved by aggregating many small inventory sources, not by funding editorial quality. The cost is passed through as lower publisher payouts. Consumers pay through loss of information. They see more ads across more surfaces—billboards, podcasts, recommendation widgets, connected TV—but those surfaces do not fund the local reporting they actually need to understand their own communities.
Who profits
Platform and ad-tech companies profit systematically. Taboola earned $215.5M in adjusted EBITDA on $1.9B revenue (11.2% margin). Outbrain earned $93.4M in adjusted EBITDA on $1.3B revenue (7.2% margin). iHeartMedia's Digital Audio Group earned $86.8M in adjusted EBITDA on $327M revenue (26.6% margin). Clear Channel Outdoor earned $143.4M in adjusted EBITDA on $438M quarterly revenue. These are not thin-margin businesses. They profit from being the routing layer between fragmented advertising demand and fragmented publisher supply. They scale by distributing the cost of attention capture across thousands of publishers rather than by funding any single newsroom. Entertainment podcasts and national shows profit. National brands with attribution budgets profit. But the local journalism layer is structurally bypassed—it is a cost center in most cases, not a profit center. The take-private transaction of Clear Channel Outdoor by Mubadala at $2.43 per share signals confidence that attention capture and physical ad infrastructure remain valuable even outside public markets. The deal assumes sustained advertiser demand for reach and repetition. Local news is not mentioned because it is not part of that thesis.

The tension

The fundamental tension is that all four substitutes are succeeding at what they were designed to do—monetize attention and attract advertising—while failing at what they never intended to do: fund information about local governance, accountability, and community dynamics. Taboola's $1.9B platform works because advertisers do not care where their ads appear, only that they reach the right person at the right moment. Outbrain's CTV growth (67% year-over-year in Q2 2026) works because connected TV viewers represent measurable, targetable attention. iHeartMedia's podcast growth works because personality and entertainment are highly monetizable. Clear Channel's physical displays work because billboards cannot be skipped or ad-blocked. None of these models creates an economic reason to fund a reporter to spend three months investigating municipal corruption. The externalized cost is invisible until it manifests as actual government malfeasance, fraud, or neglect that goes uncovered precisely because there is no mechanism to pay for reporting on it. That cost has historically been absorbed by the local advertising surplus. Today, that surplus is being extracted by platforms that have no obligation to replace it with anything else.

The surprising part

The most non-obvious fact is that advertising spending has not collapsed—it has merely moved. Taboola reaching 600M daily active users, iHeartMedia's podcast segment growing 27% year-over-year, Outbrain's CTV revenue up 67%, and Clear Channel growing revenue 6-12% in most quarters are all signs of a robust and growing advertising market. The collapse is not about insufficient demand for ads. It is about the disconnect between what is being monetized (entertainment, personality, measurable performance, physical attention) and what is being starved (local civic reporting). The platforms are not replacing journalism with other journalism. They are replacing journalism with attention optimization. The advertising market has plenty of money. It has simply been redirected to formats that do not fund newsrooms. Another surprise: the attempted substitutes are themselves rarely profitable at the publisher level. Taboola and Outbrain survive as platform operators by capturing the middle, not by improving the economics for the local publishers using their services. A local news site using Taboola's recommendation network generates revenue per story measured in cents, not dollars. The companies that are thriving are the ad-tech operators, not the news sites.

Why it matters

Every community is becoming functionally blind to its own institutions. City councils make decisions about zoning, permits, and contracts with no one watching. Schools award contracts without scrutiny. Police departments and courts operate without independent oversight. Businesses engage in fraud or environmental harm with reduced likelihood of exposure. The economic value of these information gaps—the ability to cut corners, hide decisions, avoid scrutiny—flows to the entities being unmonitored. The cost is borne by citizens who now lack the information to make informed decisions about who to trust, who to vote for, where to send their children to school, where to move. Local democracy was never perfect, but it had a feedback mechanism: newspapers. That mechanism is gone. The advertising market is thriving without it, which means there is no profit motive to rebuild it. Any replacement will have to come from outside the advertising system entirely—from subscription revenue, philanthropy, or public funding. None of those models are scaling fast enough to offset the $20B+ of local news advertising that migrated to digital platforms in the past fifteen years. The gap is not temporary. It is now the stable equilibrium.

A $7B+ ecosystem of profitable platforms for attention monetization has proven that advertising can thrive without funding journalism—widening rather than closing the gap between the cost of accountability and the revenue available to pay for it.
