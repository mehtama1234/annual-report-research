Trends · The Local News Collapse

The Replacement That Has Not Arrived

Taboola reached $1.912B in 2025 revenue and $711.5M in ex-TAC gross profit. Outbrain/Teads reached $1.300B with CTV revenue up 67% year-over-year. iHeartMedia's podcast segment grew 27% year-over-year to $147M. Clear Channel Outdoor went private at $2.43/share for $1.604B annual revenue. Every one of these is profitable and growing. None of them funds local journalism about city councils, courts, schools, or municipal corruption.

The structural gap between what local journalism costs and what any current business model can fund has not narrowed—it has widened. Digital audio, podcasting, out-of-home displays, and content-recommendation platforms are all growing profitably without funding investigative reporting about local governance. The gap is not being filled; it is being bypassed by the companies that absorbed the advertising dollars that once funded newsrooms.

The mechanism

The 20th-century local news model rested on a monopoly: if you wanted to reach your town, you bought ads in the local newspaper or on local radio. That monopoly extracted enough surplus to fund reporters, editors, and investigative work about local government, courts, and business. When digital media fragmented audience attention, that floor collapsed. The advertising market did not disappear—it migrated to platforms optimized for measurable, algorithmic attention routing.

Taboola and Outbrain/Teads became $1.9B and $1.3B revenue businesses by sitting between advertisers and fragmented publisher inventory, including local news sites. The business model is fundamentally extractive: these platforms take a middle cut (traffic acquisition costs averaging 50%+ of gross revenue) and pass the rest to publishers as commodity price. A local news site that once charged $500 for a print ad now makes cents-per-click through a recommendation widget. The scale is there (600M daily active users at Taboola), but the margin per story is not. Publishers using these platforms cannot fund newsrooms.

iHeartMedia's podcast growth ($147M Q1 2026, up 27% YoY) shows where audio attention consolidates: entertainment, personality, national shows, not local news radio. The company operates 860+ broadcast stations, but the growth lever is measurable digital audio and podcasts. Clear Channel Outdoor's $1.604B in out-of-home advertising revenue is thriving, but billboards and transit displays do not employ reporters. They are attention capture divorced from information production. These companies are profitable precisely because they optimize for traffic flow and attribution, not for expensive original reporting.

The paradox is that advertising spending on digital has grown, but it has been redirected away from formats that require newsrooms. A news site that generates traffic through SEO-optimized stories and recommendation networks can earn partial credit for that traffic. But the economic floor under original reporting—weeks of investigation, legal risk, expertise—has no direct revenue counterweight. The gap between the cost of local journalism and what any model generates is not shrinking; it is permanent.

Why now

Three structural changes converged to make the gap catastrophic. First: the ad-tech layer matured from 2015-2025. Taboola and Outbrain went from tens of millions to $1.9B and $1.3B revenue, proving that attention could be monetized at scale without funding original content. Second: local advertising budgets, which once had to concentrate locally because of geography, can now be served programmatically at national scale. Regional publishers became price-takers. Third: measurement platforms (Taboola's 28.8% adjusted EBITDA margin, Outbrain's focus on full-funnel outcomes) rewired advertiser expectations. A local news report about a zoning board is invisible to these measurement systems. An entertainment podcast with precise listener attribution is not. By 2025-2026, the direction was clear: open-web, podcasting, out-of-home, and video platforms were all growing profitably without funding journalists. The companies pursuing these models had no economic incentive to rebuild what they displaced.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Advertisers pay into a fragmented system optimized for clicks, not for journalism. Small businesses (plumbers, dentists, real estate agents) that need local reach now bid against national campaigns for the same digital inventory, paying more for less targeted exposure. Consumers pay through loss of information—they see more ads across more surfaces but those surfaces do not fund reporting about their own communities. Communities pay through loss of oversight.
Who profits
Platform and ad-tech companies systematically. Taboola earned $215.5M in adjusted EBITDA on $1.912B revenue (11.2% margin). Outbrain earned substantial EBITDA on $1.300B revenue. iHeartMedia's Digital Audio Group earned $86.8M adjusted EBITDA on $327M revenue (26.6% margin). Clear Channel Outdoor went private at a valuation that assumes sustained advertiser demand. These firms profit from being the routing layer between fragmented advertising demand and fragmented publisher supply, scaling by distributing costs across thousands of publishers rather than funding newsrooms. Entertainment podcasts and national shows profit. National brands with attribution budgets profit. The local journalism layer is bypassed.

The tension

All four substitutes are succeeding at what they were designed to do—monetize attention and attract advertising—while failing at what they never intended: fund information about local governance and accountability. Taboola's $1.9B platform works because advertisers do not care where their ads appear, only that they reach the right person. Outbrain's CTV growth (67% YoY) works because connected TV viewers represent measurable attention. iHeartMedia's podcast growth works because personality and entertainment are monetizable. Clear Channel's physical displays work because billboards cannot be skipped. None of these models creates an economic reason to fund a reporter investigating municipal corruption. That cost is invisible until malfeasance goes uncovered because there is no mechanism to pay for reporting on it.

The surprising part

The most non-obvious fact is that advertising has not collapsed—it has merely migrated. Taboola reaches 600M+ daily active users, iHeartMedia's podcasts grow 27% YoY, Outbrain's CTV revenue grew 67%, and Clear Channel grew revenue in most quarters. The advertising market has plenty of money. It has been redirected to formats that do not fund newsrooms. The second surprise is that attempted substitutes are themselves rarely profitable at the publisher level. A local news site using Taboola's recommendation network generates revenue measured in cents per story, not dollars. The companies thriving are the platform operators taking the middle cut, not the publishers. The third surprise is the linguistic evasion: Taboola and Outbrain are not 'content distribution' platforms that absorbed local newspaper advertising—they are described as 'recommendation engines' and 'contextual monetization solutions,' which makes the displacement invisible in policy conversations.

Why it matters

Every community is becoming functionally blind to its own institutions. City councils make decisions about zoning and contracts with no one watching. Schools award contracts without scrutiny. Police and courts operate without independent oversight. The economic value of these information gaps—the ability to cut corners, hide decisions, avoid accountability—flows to those being unmonitored. The cost is borne by citizens who lack information to make informed decisions about governance, schools, and where to live. Local democracy required a feedback mechanism: newspapers. That mechanism is gone. The platforms that absorbed the advertising revenue have no profit motive to replace it.

A $4B+ ecosystem of profitable platforms for attention monetization has proven that advertising can thrive without funding journalism, widening rather than closing the gap between the cost of accountability and the revenue available to pay for it.
