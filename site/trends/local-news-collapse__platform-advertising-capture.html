Who Captured the Money: Platform Advertising at Scale

Meta and Google absorbed the advertising dollars that once funded local newsrooms through scale, targeting precision, and measurement infrastructure that local newspapers cannot match structurally.

Local journalism is not dying because the story value disappeared. It's dying because the advertising revenue that funded it migrated to Meta and Google whose scale, targeting precision, and measurement infrastructure make local newspaper CPMs structurally uncompetitive. FY2025 Meta generated $200.966B in revenue, Q2 2026 Meta reached 3.6B daily active people, and Alphabet's full-year 2025 revenue exceeded $400B for the first time. The value didn't disappear—it migrated to platforms that have no obligation to fund local civic reporting.

The mechanism

For most of the 20th century, local newspapers owned local advertising because they were the only efficient way to reach local audiences at scale. Digital platforms destroyed that moat through three mechanisms. First: Meta and Google reach larger, more targeted audiences more cheaply. Meta's 3.6B daily active people (Q2 2026) and algorithmic targeting mean an advertiser can reach their exact demographic for pennies compared to newspaper cost per impression. Second: measurement became automatic and real-time. Advertisers see exactly how many clicks, impressions, conversions, and revenue their ad generated—something newspapers never offered. Third: self-service tools eliminated the need for ad salesmen, further cutting cost. A local business spending $500/month on newspaper ads could spend $50/month on Meta and reach a better-targeted audience.

The scale advantage is not competitive. Meta's Q2 2026 showed the company reached 3.6B daily active people, delivered 14% growth in ad impressions year-over-year, achieved 12% growth in average price per ad, and generated $60.8B in quarterly revenue with AI-optimized ad delivery. Alphabet's FY2025 operating income exceeded $129B on $402.8B revenue—both numbers that dwarf any regional newspaper's economics. A local newspaper has maybe 50,000 readers, mostly in their community. Meta has orders of magnitude more reach with AI recommendation engines showing ads to the most likely buyers. The newspaper cannot compete on reach, targeting precision, or measurement.

The broader advertising stack reinforced the capture. The Trade Desk and Criteo emerged to automate and optimize ad buying across fragmented digital channels, making it possible for advertisers to coordinate campaigns across hundreds of publishers without negotiating direct relationships. That further eroded the local newspaper's value proposition. The newspaper once owned the local advertising channel. Now it is just one fractional inventory slot competing against billions of impressions from Meta and Google.

Meta's explicit infrastructure spending proves the commitment is permanent. FY2025 capital expenditures reached $72.22B for data centers and AI infrastructure that will make future ad targeting even more precise and efficient. Alphabet raised Q2 2026 capex guidance to $195-$205B for 2026 and said capex should increase significantly in 2027. These are investments in advertising infrastructure that local newspapers could never replicate at any scale.

Why now

The inflection is Q2 2026 and FY2025 filings showing advertising capture is now a realized operating reality, not a future risk. Meta's Q2 2026 marked the clearest proof: 3.6B daily active people, 14% impression growth, 12% average price-per-ad growth, all driving $60.8B quarterly revenue. Alphabet's full-year 2025 exceeded $400B for the first time, with YouTube alone topping $60B of annual revenue. Both companies are now spending record capex on AI infrastructure specifically to improve ad targeting and measurement. The inflection is precise: between Q4 2025 and Q2 2026, the platforms moved from competitive advantage to structural dominance in advertising, with investment levels that lock in moat-building for the next 5+ years.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Local communities lose civic oversight when newspapers lose advertising revenue and cut reporters. Fewer reporters means government, courts, and local businesses face less scrutiny. Corruption goes undetected longer. School boards make decisions without coverage. The human cost is invisible until something goes very wrong. Smaller publishers without scale cannot build equivalent ad infrastructure and cannot compete.
Who profits
Meta and Google captured the advertising dollars without obligation to fund journalism. FY2025 Meta net income reached $60.458B; Q2 2026 Meta net income reached $15.848B. Alphabet FY2025 operating income was $129.0B. Both are deploying record capex to deepen the moat. The Trade Desk and similar infrastructure platforms profit by taking a cut of budgets flowing through their systems. Technology companies profit by selling tools that businesses use to run their own ads instead of hiring local ad salespeople.

The tension

The externalized cost is the hollowing of local civic infrastructure. The platforms' algorithms treat all content equally—a quality investigative report is worth the same as a misleading rumor when they're competing for attention. As local news dies, communities have fewer independent information sources, making them more vulnerable to misinformation and more dependent on social-media narratives.

The surprising part

This is not a business failure by newspaper companies. The value didn't disappear because journalism got worse. It disappeared because the structure of advertising itself changed. If a newspaper owner could have run Meta's algorithm in 2010, they would have made it work. But they couldn't. The technological and capital requirements to build a Meta-scale platform were beyond what a regional newspaper could afford. The captured value is structural, not skill-based.

Why it matters

Local democratic participation depends on local information. When local newspapers collapse, communities lose the institution that covered school boards, city councils, and local corruption. Without that coverage, political participation drops, civic knowledge declines, and communities become more fragmented. The platforms gain advertising revenue and users, but society loses the shared information infrastructure that held local democracy together.

Meta and Google didn't kill local news by running better newspapers—they killed it by offering advertisers a better place to spend money with zero obligation to fund journalism.
