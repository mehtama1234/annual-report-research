Trends · The Local News Collapse

Print advertising collapses faster than digital revenue offsets it; news organizations bet survival on subscriptions and AI licensing

As print advertising collapses faster than digital revenue can offset it, news organizations bet survival on subscriptions, SMB marketing services, and AI licensing—even as audience attention fragments.

Print decay is unstoppable (~50% of revenue in 2024, falling); digital growth (46% of Gannett FY2025) is real but insufficient to offset print decline. Profitability requires three new levers simultaneously: subscription scale (1.5M digital-only), SMB services (LocaliQ $106.3M Q2 2026), and data licensing to AI companies. No single lever solves the problem.

The mechanism

Print advertising collapse accelerated in FY2025. Gannett's FY2025 data shows digital was only 46% of revenue, meaning print was still ~54%—but print is declining -5% to -8% annually while digital grows +3% to +5%. At this trajectory, print falls below 40% by 2027 and below 30% by 2029. This is not stabilization; it is managed decline. The core problem: print advertising margin was 70%+, digital advertising margin is 30-40%, so even if digital revenue equals print revenue, absolute profit halves. Gannett FY2025 total revenue was $2.302B; if digital had been 60% (still growing), print would have been 40% (still declining)—the company would still be losing absolute profit.

Digital subscription scale is the primary survival strategy, but 1.5M digital-only subscribers at Gannett is a low base. Assuming $100–150 per subscriber annually, this generates $150–225M revenue—meaningful, but insufficient to offset $1.2B of declining print ad revenue. The math: 1.5M subs × $150 = $225M; print decline rate is $1.2B × 7% annually = $84M/year decline. Subscriptions offset ~3 years of decline, then the gap widens again. Gannett's Q1 2026 same-store digital grew 5.2% while total declined -4%, showing the crossover point (digital growth > total growth) was reached, but on a smaller base.

Parallel revenue streams emerge as mathematical necessity: LocaliQ (Gannett's SMB marketing arm) generated $106.3M in Q2 2026, representing ~4.6% of company revenue but growing faster than core publishing. iHeartMedia podcasts ($174M Q4 2025, +24%) show audio content licensing is scalable. Taboola FY2025 revenue was $1.912B with $42.3M net income—pure ad-tech intermediation without content ownership. These parallel streams (subscriptions, SMB services, content licensing, ad-tech) are not supplementary; they are structural requirements. No legacy publisher survives on content alone anymore.

Why now

Inflection point occurred in 2024–2025 when print fell below 50% of revenue and digital's growth rate exceeded total revenue growth rate for the first time. Gannett FY2025 was the moment: (1) Digital was 46% of revenue, proving digital has scaled; (2) 1.5M digital-only subscribers exceeded print subscribers, a psychological and operational threshold; (3) LocaliQ scaled to quarterly revenue of $106.3M, showing non-content revenue is viable; (4) Taboola's FY2025 profitability ($42.3M net income on $1.912B revenue, 2.2% margin but positive) proved ad-tech intermediation works without owning content. This is not a one-year fluctuation; it is a structural shift. Publishers now operate as portfolio companies (content + subscriptions + SMB services + ad-tech + licensing), not content companies with a marketing arm.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Local news consumers who lose newsroom scale and reporting depth. Gannett and competitors consolidated newsrooms post-2020; fewer reporters per market means less investigative capacity. SMB services (LocaliQ) cannibalize reader attention for advertiser attention. Additionally, SMBs pay for LocaliQ services, raising their marketing costs (passed through to prices). Simultaneously, premium news subscribers (Gannett digital-only) pay rising fees to offset declining ad revenue, fracturing the audience into free-tier (ad-supported) and paid-tier (subscriber-supported) segments. Low-income readers are pushed to free (ad-heavy, outdated) tiers.
Who profits
Gannett and publicly traded local publishers who can scale SMB services and ad-tech (LocaliQ profit margin not disclosed separately, but scale to $106.3M suggests 40-50% margins). iHeartMedia through podcast licensing and digital audio (+14% Q4 2025). Taboola through ad-tech intermediation ($42.3M FY2025 profit). Large platforms (Google, Meta) capture incrementally more SMB advertising budgets that might have gone to local news. News organizations with strong subscriber bases (NYT, WSJ) benefit; fragmented local publishers struggle.

The tension

Regulatory risk from content licensing: EU Digital Services Act and similar regulations may force platforms to compensate publishers for training data. If regulators mandate AI-licensing fees, large publishers capture upside; small publishers get negotiated minimums. Additionally, subscription fatigue: consumers are reaching the limit of how many subscriptions they will maintain. Q1 2026 showed Gannett digital growth at 5.2% despite declining total revenue—subscriber growth is slowing. LocaliQ and SMB services create a different conflict: news organizations compete directly with Google Local Services and Meta Small Business, and they will lose on scale and reach.

The surprising part

Gannett and legacy publishers remain profitable at much smaller scale than in 2015. This is not because they found growth; it is because they cut costs (newsroom consolidation, real estate sales) faster than revenue declined. FY2025 revenue of $2.302B looks healthy until you compare to 2012 revenue ($15B+). The company shrunk 85% in absolute terms and is now profitable only through portfolio diversification (subscriptions, SMB services, ad-tech), not content. If content were still the profit driver, Gannett would be insolvent.

Why it matters

Local news collapse creates information deserts and reduces accountability for local government, real estate, and corporate misdeeds. Communities without local news see crime reporting disappear, school board corruption go unchecked, and tax abuse undetected. The shift to digital-only and subscriber-only models means low-income residents lose free access to civic information. The shift to SMB services means publishers increasingly serve advertisers, not readers.

Print advertising collapse outpaced digital growth; publishers now survive on portfolio layering (subscriptions + SMB services + content licensing) rather than news subscriptions alone.
