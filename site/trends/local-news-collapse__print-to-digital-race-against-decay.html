Trends · The Local News Collapse

The Race Against Decay: Print to Digital

As print advertising collapses faster than digital revenue can offset it, news organizations and media companies are betting their survival on subscriptions, SMB marketing services, and AI licensing—even as audience attention fragments across more platforms than ever.

Legacy media operators face a brutal mathematics: print revenue declines 5-8% annually even as digital subscription and targeted advertising services grow in the single digits. The race is whether companies can convert shrinking audiences into higher-quality, recurring revenue streams—subscriptions, marketing services for small businesses, and AI content licensing—fast enough to outrun the decay of the old bundle. Local and national news publishers are trying to survive not by denying decline, but by rebuilding themselves as hybrid subscription, ad-tech, and professional services platforms.

The mechanism

The decay mechanism is structural, not cyclical. Print audiences cannot be recovered; they are migrating to digital, social, and algorithmic discovery. What publishers lose in print circulation revenue—down 5% to 8% year-over-year at scale—cannot be directly replaced by display advertising or subscription revenue at the same volume. A local newspaper that once made $10 from print circulation and classified ads must now earn $3 from digital subscription, $2 from targeted display ads, $2 from SMB marketing services, and $3 from licensing and syndication to replace that bundle. The math forces diversification.

Digital subscription economics are real but slow. Gannett reached 1.5 million digital-only subscribers by end of 2025, and those subscribers generate recurring revenue that scales without incremental print production cost. However, subscription revenue grows in single digits annually, not high single digits. Each new subscriber acquisition requires investment in marketing, onboarding, and content development that eats into the value chain. Radio broadcaster iHeartMedia shows the same pattern: podcast revenue grew 24-27% year-over-year in late 2025 and early 2026, but even at high growth rates, it does not yet offset the contraction in legacy broadcast spot revenue, which declined 3-5% in the same periods.

The SMB marketing services layer emerged as the most visible diversification escape hatch. Gannett's LocaliQ platform generated $106.3 million in Q2 2026 revenue and is now the company's highest-growth business line. LocaliQ sells local search, social, and web services to small and medium-sized businesses—hair salons, dentists, plumbers, auto dealers—who need to be found and messaged but lack the resources to build their own marketing infrastructure. This is not content; it is a software-as-a-service play built on top of the local news brand's distribution and data footprint. It sidesteps the subscriptions-versus-advertising debate entirely by selling operational tools to advertisers. That model works because SMBs cannot afford expensive agencies, they trust local media brands, and the lifetime value of an SMB customer (especially if bundled across multiple services) can exceed what a single display ad impression is worth.

Fragmentation and ad-tech intermediaries are capturing the value chain. Taboola's 2025 turnaround—reaching profitability with $42.3M net income after prior-year losses—came not from owning content or audiences directly, but from sitting between advertisers and publisher surfaces as a demand-routing and measurement layer. Similarly, Teads/Outbrain is rebuilding itself around connected TV and omnichannel branding after the merger, capturing value not as a publisher or agency, but as infrastructure that helps advertisers navigate a fragmented open internet. These companies are winning not because print is thriving, but because they are becoming essential as intermediaries in a world where no single platform (Google, Facebook, Amazon, Meta) owns everything. The race is moving from publishers competing on content quality to media companies competing on their ability to integrate subscriptions, targeted advertising, SMB services, and measurement into one workflow that neither publishers nor advertisers could build alone.

Why now

The structural decline of print has finally made the old business model untenable for all but the largest premium brands like The New York Times. Mass-market newspapers and local media reached the inflection point around 2024-2025 where print revenue fell below 50% of total revenue, and the mathematics of replacement became visible. Simultaneously, digital advertising became more measurable, more performance-based, and more favorable to intermediaries than to content owners. Publishers faced a choice: become subscription platforms with niche audiences (the NYT model), become SMB marketing services (the Gannett/LocaliQ model), or consolidate around ad-tech infrastructure (the Teads/Outbrain model). All three strategies are unfolding in parallel, and all three show that the old "trusted content plus mass advertising" economics no longer funds journalism or broadcasting at scale outside premium verticals.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
End consumers pay through digital subscriptions (Gannett's 1.5M subscribers paying between $10-20/month), but that revenue base is still too small to fund most news operations. Small and medium-sized business owners pay through LocaliQ and similar marketing-services platforms—a plumber in Milwaukee pays $200/month for local search management and is far more valuable than the same plumber's occasional newspaper ad. Media-buying teams at larger companies and agencies pay through programmatic platforms like Taboola and Teads, which automate and measure their spending. Advertisers of all sizes are paying, but not for content reach anymore. They are paying for targeting, measurement, attribution, and operational integration. That is a fundamentally different customer value proposition.
Who profits
The companies that profit most are not publishers themselves, but the infrastructure and services layers sitting on top of fragmented audiences. Taboola returned to profitability in 2025 while most traditional publishers remain structurally pressured. Teads/Outbrain is capturing value through consolidation and margin recovery despite revenue pressures. Meanwhile, Gannett is profitable but must run the entire stack—content, subscriptions, SMB services, and ad tech—to stay afloat. The winners are companies that can commoditize publishing (by aggregating many small publishers into one monetization platform) and turn attention fragmentation into a coordination problem they can solve. The losers are mid-sized publishers that lack the scale for subscriptions, lack the advertiser relationships for premium ad sales, and lack the capital to invest in SMB services or ad-tech infrastructure.

The tension

The fundamental tension is that digital monetization has never and likely will never replace the economics of mass-market print advertising plus circulation. At peak, a newspaper made 60-70% of revenue from advertising and 30-40% from circulation. Digital advertising and subscriptions together today amount to 40-50% of total revenue for companies like Gannett, and that proportion is growing, but the total size is shrinking. A newspaper with $1 billion in revenue in 2010 (70% from print, 30% from digital) cannot become a $600 million business in 2025 (40% from print, 60% from digital) and still employ the same newsroom or broadcast network. Something has to give. For newspapers, it is newsroom size and local news depth. For broadcasters like iHeartMedia, it is licensed content costs and market coverage. The audience attention is still there—Gannett's 186 million average monthly unique visitors show that—but attention no longer pays the same way it used to, and that mismatch has no technological solution.

The surprising part

The most surprising finding is that profitability is possible without solving the core problem. Taboola is profitable ($42.3M net income in 2025) not because anyone cracked how to make digital content monetization work at scale, but because it is not trying to monetize content at all. It is monetizing distribution, routing, and measurement. Similarly, Gannett is profitable ($1.7M in 2025, up from multi-year losses) not because newspaper subscriptions suddenly boomed, but because the company learned to run three different businesses on the same brand and data assets. LocaliQ treats the news brand as a trust asset to sell business services to SMBs. Digital subscriptions treat the content as a premium product for engaged readers. And advertising treats the audience as inventory to be routed and targeted. None of these individually replace old-media economics, but together they create a diverse enough revenue base to be profitable. The surprise is that traditional media survived the digital disruption not by winning the disruption (as tech companies did), but by fragmenting into three or four different business models inside one corporate entity.

Why it matters

Local news matters to communities; when local media collapses, so does the information backbone that holds civic accountability together. Gannett, iHeartMedia, and their peers are the operators of that system for most Americans outside the largest metros. Their survival is not a business question alone—it is a question about whether communities will have reliable, locally-rooted information infrastructure or whether all news will flow through algorithmic platforms owned by a handful of tech companies. The second outcome is possible and increasingly likely in many markets. The companies profiled here (Gannett, iHeartMedia) are fighting to remain relevant by diversifying, and they are partly succeeding financially, but that financial success is coming through SMB services and targeted advertising, not through the restoration of local news as a sustained public good. Readers should care because if these companies disappear, the audience attention they hold does not disappear—it flows to social platforms, search engines, and streaming services that have no particular incentive to fund local reporting or community-focused journalism.

Legacy media companies are surviving not by making digital subscriptions or advertising work, but by fragmenting into subscription platforms, SMB marketing services, and ad-tech infrastructure operators that monetize attention through increasingly specialized, high-touch revenue streams.
