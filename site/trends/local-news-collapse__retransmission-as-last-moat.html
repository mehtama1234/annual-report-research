The Retransmission Fee as the Last Moat

Nexstar's distribution revenue ($2.924B) now exceeds advertising revenue ($1.959B). Local broadcast survives on cable bundle contracts that extract fees from MVPD operators—a temporary tax that ends when MVPD households shrink enough to break the math.

Local broadcast television found a revenue floor that print journalism never had: retransmission and distribution fees paid by cable and satellite operators to carry local affiliate channels. As advertising has collapsed and MVPD households have shrunk, these fees have become the primary economic foundation of local TV newsrooms. Nexstar's FY2025 split—$2.924B distribution revenue versus $1.959B advertising—proves the business has transformed from broadcaster to fee collector. This is not a sustainable moat—it is a temporary protection that works only as long as cable bundles remain relevant and regulators allow the fee extraction to continue. The deeper insight: local journalism is surviving not because communities value it but because regulatory and contractual arrangements mandate that cable companies buy it.

The mechanism

Retransmission fees emerged as an accidental moat in the 2000s when cable and satellite operators realized they could not drop local TV stations without facing regulatory and consumer backlash. Local stations owned must-have content: breaking news, weather, emergency alerts, high-school sports, and civic information that major networks cannot efficiently replicate across hundreds of markets. Cable operators needed to bundle local channels alongside ESPN, news networks, and entertainment to justify subscription pricing. The regulatory structure—the Compulsory License under the Copyright Act and must-carry rules—gave broadcasters leverage to demand payment. Slowly, this evolved into a formal retransmission market where operators pay per subscriber to carry signals. Nexstar's FY2025 split shows that today's local broadcaster is primarily a distribution collector, not an advertising medium. The retransmission fee has become the business model.

The model works because of structural coupling: MVPD operators (Comcast, Charter, EchoStar/DISH) cannot affordably drop local channels without losing subscribers to competitors who keep them, yet the cost of carrying the channels has become material enough to fight over in renewal negotiations. Nexstar's disclosure that distribution agreements covering more than 60% of its subscriber base were renewed with strong terms in Q4 2025 signals that even in a period of pay-TV decay, broadcasters retain pricing power. The fees survive not because viewers demand high-quality local news—local news viewership has collapsed alongside MVPD adoption—but because the bundle and regulatory structure create artificial demand. Cable operators essentially pay a tax that they pass through to consumers, and that tax has become the only revenue stream keeping hundreds of local newsrooms operational.

The fragility is absolute. Nexstar's Q2 2026 distribution revenue of $1.116B remained stable even though EchoStar (a key payer) was hemorrhaging subscribers—from 6.998M in Q4 2025 to 6.391M in Q2 2026—and Comcast lost 167,000 residential broadband customers in Q2 2026. But the math is deteriorating. Every year, MVPD households shrink by 3-5% as consumers drop cable entirely. The per-subscriber fee can rise, but the subscriber base it applies to shrinks. Nexstar's strategic response—building NewsNation, securing The CW through sports rights, and diversifying into digital and multicast networks—shows management knows the moat is temporary. By 2028-2030, retransmission fee economics may look materially different as MVPD operators complete their conversion away from video-centric bundles toward broadband-plus-wireless models that have lower video-content dependence.

Why now

The inflection is visible in the timing of data arrivals. Nexstar's FY2025 results (announced Q1 2026) showed distribution revenue essentially flat ($2.924B versus $2.897B in 2024), while the company was already projecting continued subscriber losses at its MVPD customers. Simultaneously, EchoStar announced Q2 2026 that it was deconsolidating substantially all of its Pay-TV segment, signaling the MVPD operator was exiting the business model that has funded retransmission fees. Comcast's Q2 2026 earnings showed total customer relationships fell to 47.7M from prior quarters, with management explicitly framing video-bundle economics as unsustainable. The inflection is that stabilization in distribution revenue (flat FY2025) disguises an accelerating structural problem: per-subscriber fees can increase, but the subscriber base shrinks faster. Nexstar's comment on strong renewal terms proves the company is winning price increases even as MVPD decline accelerates—the definition of a moat at its peak before collapse.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Cable and satellite subscribers pay, invisibly. Comcast, Charter, and EchoStar remit billions annually in retransmission fees to broadcasters, and they pass that cost through to customers as part of video-bundle pricing. A cable subscriber in 2026 pays roughly $30-50 more per month than they would if retransmission fees did not exist. That burden hits hardest on lower-income households that rely on cable for video and internet. Voters and taxpayers also pay indirectly: as local journalism becomes dependent on retransmission fees rather than genuine community support, newsrooms optimize for content that maximizes carriage (dramatic news, political coverage, sensationalism) rather than for community service (zoning board meetings, school-board accountability, local business analysis). The journalistic product degrades because the revenue model is decoupled from actual news value.
Who profits
Nexstar, Fox, and other broadcasters profit directly. Retransmission fees account for roughly half of Nexstar's revenue and are the single largest growth driver of profits in the modern local TV business. MVPD operators (Comcast, Charter, EchoStar) do not profit—they lose margin and subscriber relationships—but they tolerate it because they need the channels to defend their bundles. Regulators profit by avoiding a crisis: they can pretend local journalism is still being funded while knowing that the real economics are disconnected from genuine community value creation.

The tension

Retransmission fees work when MVPD households are large enough that per-subscriber fees (roughly $1.50-2.50 per subscriber per month) generate meaningful totals. But MVPD decline is accelerating: EchoStar fell from 6.998M to 6.391M subscribers in six months (Q4 2025 to Q2 2026). At that rate of decline, the household base may shrink from today's ~60M to <40M by 2035. At that point, retransmission fees alone cannot support a newsroom. Nexstar's strategy of diversifying into NewsNation, The CW, and digital is management's acknowledgment that they cannot rely on traditional retrans. But those new ventures require investment and do not generate the same margin. The second tension is regulatory: if Congress acts to cap retransmission fees or eliminate must-carry rules, the moat dissolves overnight. Broadcasters have fought hard to prevent this, but the political argument is weakening. The third tension is cultural: retransmission fees mean local journalism survives through forced subsidies, not genuine community support.

The surprising part

Local broadcast television is not collapsing at all—it is simply becoming a cable extraction mechanism. Nexstar's Q2 2026 distribution revenue ($1.116B) exceeded advertising ($862M), showing the business already transitioned away from being a broadcaster toward being a regulatory toll collector. The company is not fighting to keep journalists or build newsroom quality; it is fighting to renew retransmission agreements with Comcast and Charter. EchoStar's Q2 2026 deconsolidation of substantially all its Pay-TV segment also admits that the traditional cable-TV model is broken and that pure broadcasting economics no longer work. What survives is not journalism—it is the regulatory mandate to extract fees from cable bundles on behalf of a shrinking audience that no longer watches broadcast TV anyway.

Why it matters

The public loses on every front. Cable subscribers pay higher bills to subsidize local TV that most households no longer watch. Communities lose accountability journalism as newsrooms shrink and consolidate (Nexstar now operates more than 200 stations across the country with skeleton crews). Voters become less informed about local government, schools, and business because the local journalism that once provided accountability no longer exists. The regulatory fiction that retransmission fees are protecting local journalism actually enables the opposite: it allows broadcasters to extract value from the bundle while cutting journalists, and it allows cable operators to blame program costs for price increases while actually funding distant corporate media companies. When fees eventually collapse due to MVPD decline, communities will discover they have lost their information infrastructure with no replacement in sight.

Local broadcast television survives retransmission fees, not journalism, and those fees are a temporary tax that ends when MVPD households shrink enough to break the math.
