Membership or Nothing

Across mobility, media, pet food, and financial services, the best prices and fastest delivery now sit behind a fee gate — and the companies that built that gate are using non-member revenue to subsidize member pricing, creating two parallel economies under one brand.

The membership fee is not the revenue. It is the toll that lets the platform convert a one-time buyer into a recurring relationship, then extract value across every surface that relationship touches. Uber One members drive roughly half of Uber's gross bookings from 24% of its monthly users. Amazon Prime members get same-day delivery that Uber-level pricing mechanics make possible, while the $19.8B advertising business and $46.8B third-party seller fees run on top of the same captive audience. The mechanism is tiered economic architecture, not habit formation: non-member transactions effectively cross-subsidize the member deal, and the platform compounds returns by stacking more monetization surfaces on the captured relationship.

The mechanism

The membership model runs on a single economic lever: capture the customer relationship before the transaction happens. Once captured, price the membership to generate frequency. Uber One at $14.99/month or $149/year covers both rides and food delivery. A member who has already paid for the month treats the next ride as marginal cost, not full price. The purchase threshold drops. Frequency rises. From 50M Uber One members driving roughly half of gross bookings, it is clear that less than a quarter of the monthly user base is generating half the platform's volume — membership concentration at work.

The platform then uses that frequency and the behavioral data it generates to stack additional monetization on the same relationship. Amazon Prime at $139/year funds delivery speed that increases purchase frequency, which improves merchant demand, which grows the $46.8B third-party seller services line and the $19.8B advertising business. Chewy Autoship converts a single pet-food order into a recurring delivery, generating $595 in net sales per active customer against a 30.1% gross margin. SiriusXM locks in monthly billing at 1.4% monthly churn — a retention rate closer to a utility bill than an entertainment subscription — then funds exclusive content and Pandora's 41.1M active monthly users on the same infrastructure.

The non-member customer is structural to this economics. Uber non-members pay the full per-ride rate; Uber One members pay a fixed monthly fee and then a lower per-trip commission, allowing Uber to absorb more cost per member trip and offset it through non-member full-freight pricing. Amazon Prime members get same-day delivery; non-members pay list prices and wait. The New York Times digital subscribers pay $9.94 ARPU per month with access to Games, Cooking, and The Athletic; non-subscribers see the paywall. American Express Platinum cardholders at $695 per year receive travel credits and Uber Cash that partially offset the fee; non-cardholders pay full merchant prices without the benefit stack. The tiers are not product variants. They are economic architecture designed to distribute the best deal to those who can afford the upfront gate.

This is distinct from addiction or ritual. The mechanism is financial, not behavioral. The membership fee creates a sunk cost that raises frequency, the platform uses that frequency to justify subsidized member pricing, and the subsidy is funded by charging non-members full freight. American Express names this structure directly: the company calls it the Membership Model and has sustained 30 consecutive quarters of double-digit net card fee growth by tightening the link between fee, benefit, and cardholder spending volume.

Why now

Membership economics have been building since 2005 when Amazon launched Prime with two-day shipping. But the 2025-2026 data marks a different threshold: simultaneous maturation signals across every major consumer sector at the same time, not emergence in one corner. The inflection has three legs. First, scale: Uber One reached 50M members driving half of gross bookings by Q1 2026 — a number management first disclosed in early 2026 as evidence that the membership tier had become structural rather than optional. Chewy's Autoship hit 84% of net sales in Q3 2025, meaning the overwhelming majority of its $12.60B full-year business is now recurring rather than transactional. Second, pricing power: American Express posted its 30th consecutive quarter of double-digit net card fee revenue growth in Q4 2025, a streak that began in 2018 when the company repositioned premium cards around experiential benefits and held the fee line through two economic cycles including a post-2022 rate environment that tightened most consumer spending. Third, subscriber durability: SiriusXM's self-pay churn reached a record-low 1.4% in Q2 2026 on 33M paid subscribers — lower churn than most cable TV providers in their prime, achieved in a saturated audio market with free-streaming alternatives. The pattern is not that membership is growing. It is that membership has become the baseline operating architecture. The companies that cracked it before 2020 are now pulling away from every competitor that did not. The inflection is about structural permanence, not adoption rate.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Occasional or lower-income consumers who cannot justify or afford membership costs and end up paying higher per-unit prices for the same services — the non-Uber One rider paying full surge pricing, the non-Prime shopper waiting five days for delivery, the casual Chewy customer without Autoship who pays the non-discounted pet food price. Workers and contractors whose income depends on transaction frequency that now concentrates in member ecosystems: Uber drivers see more ride requests from Uber One members; Chewy's vendor base competes for Autoship placement. Small merchants and third-party sellers on platforms like Amazon who face margin pressure because the platform uses advertising revenue from member traffic to compete against them and uses delivery speed advantages that only Prime membership density makes economically viable.
Who profits
Platform owners who convert transaction uncertainty into recurring revenue and use that revenue to subsidize member pricing while charging non-members full freight. Members themselves: a consumer paying $149/year for Uber One and using the platform three times a week receives discounts that accumulate well past the annual fee. American Express Platinum holders at $695/year receive a benefit stack — travel credits, Uber Cash, lounge access — that approaches or exceeds the fee value for frequent travelers. Institutional shareholders who receive compounding equity economics: predictable monthly billing, rising customer lifetime value as churn reaches utility-level lows (1.4% at SiriusXM), and cross-selling surfaces stacked on the same captive base. Amazon shareholders in particular: the $19.8B advertising business in Q2 2026 runs primarily on the Prime member audience, meaning the subscription relationship is indirectly funding the highest-margin revenue line in the business.

The tension

Membership saturation. When everyone who would join has joined, growth requires either price increases, which trigger churn, or service degradation, which breaks the trust that justified the membership in the first place. The current data shows early saturation signals embedded in otherwise strong numbers. SiriusXM's self-pay net additions turned negative by 111,000 in Q1 2026 even as churn reached record lows — a sign that the company is defending the subscriber base rather than growing it. At 33M paid subscribers in a market with strong free-streaming alternatives, the ceiling is visible. American Express faces the same ceiling from the opposite direction: it can keep raising card fees and refreshing benefits for the existing premium base, but the universe of consumers who will pay $695/year for a Platinum card is not infinite, and 65% of new acquisitions coming from millennial and Gen Z is both a validation of the model and a bet that those cohorts will maintain premium spending as they age. The membership model also breaks when the benefit gap between member and non-member pricing narrows enough that the membership stops justifying its cost. Starbucks Rewards spent two years — from roughly 2022 to 2024 — recovering from a period when loyalty program changes reduced perceived value and transaction frequency declined. The fix required pulling the benefit differential wider again. Every membership program faces the same maintenance cost: the gap must stay wide enough to justify the gate, and widening the gap for members means tightening it for non-members, which is only sustainable as long as non-members keep transacting.

The surprising part

The membership fee is often priced below the value of the benefits it unlocks — which means the membership itself is partially a loss leader, and the real profit comes from the behavioral lock-in it creates. Uber One at $149/year is subsidized by Uber's ability to charge non-members full freight and by advertising monetization that runs on member behavioral data. American Express Platinum at $695/year delivers a benefit stack — $200 hotel credit, $200 airline credit, $200 Uber Cash, lounge access, and more — that for a frequent traveler approaches or exceeds $695 in cash value. The card is partially subsidized by the $1.67T billed business it generates and the interchange economics on that spending, not by the fee alone. The counterintuitive architecture is that platforms price the membership to win the relationship at a near-break-even or below-cost rate, then compound returns across every surface the captured relationship unlocks. Amazon knows the $139 Prime fee is not the revenue. The $19.8B advertising business in a single quarter is the revenue — and it runs almost entirely on the Prime member audience that paid $139 to get in.

Why it matters

When the best prices and fastest delivery are gated behind membership fees, households that cannot afford or access the tier pay more for less — a regressive redistribution embedded in the infrastructure of everyday commerce. A non-Uber One member paying full surge pricing is subsidizing Uber One members' discounted rides. A non-Prime shopper paying list price for household essentials is subsidizing Prime members' same-day delivery speed and Prime Video streaming. A non-subscriber hitting the New York Times paywall is cut off from journalism that has been publicly funded in part by the subscriber base who could afford the $9.94/month digital rate. The scale is what makes this structural rather than incidental. Chewy runs 84% of its $12.60B business through Autoship, meaning the occasional buyer who orders outside the subscription is a marginal user in a system architected for subscribers. Amazon processed over 1 billion same-day or overnight deliveries in the first half of 2026 — a speed standard that only exists because Prime membership density makes the fulfillment economics work, and that speed is unavailable to the non-member at any equivalent price. The pressure compounds over time. As membership penetration rises, non-member transactions become a smaller share of platform volume, reducing the platform's incentive to keep non-member pricing competitive. The result is two different economies coexisting under one brand, and access to the better one is a function of upfront ability to pay a recurring fee that most platforms now treat as the entry price rather than an optional upgrade.

The best prices, fastest delivery, and highest-quality service now sit behind a fee gate — and the platforms charging that gate use non-member full-freight pricing to subsidize the members' better deal, leaving two parallel economies running under one brand.

Origin

The Architecture of Capture: How Membership Became the Product

Membership is no longer a layer on top of transaction—it is the infrastructure underneath, reshaping retail, media, finance, and mobility into relationship engines where the card, the app, the subscription, and the data loop feed one another.

Sub-Patterns Inside the Trend
Sub-pattern

The Fee-Card Flywheel: When the Card IS the Membership

Premium credit cards have become the most durable membership layer in consumer finance—American Express ($72.2B revenue, 30 straight quarters of double-digit net card fee growth, 70%+ of new cards on fee-paying products) and Marriott (75% of U.S. room nights from Bonvoy members, co-branded card fee revenue up $60M Q1 2026) show how card economics and loyalty have fused into a single recurring-revenue engine.

Sub-pattern

Autoship as Destiny: Replenishment Subscriptions That Never Get Cancelled

The most durable form of membership is the replenishment subscription where the customer made one decision to buy and then stops making the decision each time. Chewy, Sunrun, and SiriusXM show that once a consumer enters an auto-replenishment cycle, churn approaches zero.

Sub-pattern

The Multi-Category Wallet: One Membership, Every Transaction

Platforms that have cracked membership are weaponizing a single paid relationship to colonize multiple spend categories simultaneously—eliminating acquisition friction for new services.

Sub-pattern

Bundle Wars: How Media Turned Subscriptions Into Fortresses

Media companies are building bundles so comprehensive that cancellation becomes cognitively and economically irrational, locking in recurring revenue while making churn mathematically improbable.

Who Wins, Who Pays, What Breaks
Who profits

The Compounding Machine: Who Captures the Economics of Predictability

Recurring revenue doesn't just stabilize earnings—it reorders who wins. Incumbents with membership models deploy predictable cash as a capital weapon, undercutting transactional competitors and subsidizing expansion. The economic gap widens faster than growth rates alone suggest.

Who pays

Who Pays the Non-Member Tax

Membership-conditional businesses extract a hidden tax from non-members by offering better prices, access, or convenience to members only. Non-members pay higher prices, face worse availability, or accept exclusion while members subsidize their experience.

The tension

The Saturation Trap: What Happens When Everyone Who Would Join Has Joined

When a membership platform exhausts its addressable pool, the only levers left are price increases (which trigger churn) or service degradation (which breaks the trust that justified membership)—and every company faces this same endgame simultaneously.
