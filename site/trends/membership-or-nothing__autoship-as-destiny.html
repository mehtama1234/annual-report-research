Autoship as Destiny: The Subscription That Never Gets Cancelled

The purest form of membership is the replenishment subscription where the customer made one decision to buy and then stopped making decisions. Chewy, SiriusXM, and Sunrun show that once a consumer enters an auto-replenishment cycle, cancellation friction is so high that the relationship behaves like a utility.

The most durable form of membership-or-nothing is the replenishment subscription, where customers do not actively decide to purchase each cycle because they made the decision once and automated the rest. Chewy exemplifies this: 84% of net sales came from Autoship in Q3 2025, with 21.3 million active customers and $597 net sales per active customer in Q1 2026. SiriusXM shows 1.4% monthly churn in Q2 2026 with 93% subscriber revenue, record-low attrition reflecting embedded in-vehicle listening habit. Sunrun's 1.206 million contracted customers remain locked into 25-year power purchase agreements. Once a customer enters the replenishment cycle, cancellation friction is so high that the relationship behaves like a utility. This is the addiction dividend applied to commerce itself.

The mechanism

Autoship subscriptions exploit a behavioral asymmetry: enrollment friction is lower than cancellation friction. Chewy customers sign up for automatic monthly pet-food replenishment because convenience is obvious and switching cost is zero at signup. But once active, cancellation requires explicit action—visiting the website, confirming cancellation, potentially losing accumulated benefits. Most customers do not bother. Q3 2025 showed Chewy with 84% of sales from Autoship. That is not because 84% of customers prefer automatic delivery; it is because the default has been set and changing it requires conscious effort. The business model depends on inertia.

Replenishment logic differs from traditional membership because there is no annual decision point. Gym memberships create a moment each year where a customer can reconsider. Autoship subscriptions renew every month, every quarter, or every delivery cycle with no friction and no opt-in moment. Chewy's $597 net sales per active customer reflects not high spending but the cumulative effect of automated purchases that customers have forgotten they are making. SiriusXM's 1.4% monthly churn in Q2 2026 is the proof point. That is not churn in traditional sense; that is baseline exit rate once normal attrition is removed. The business has achieved near-utility-level stickiness.

Migration from one-time purchase to replenishment subscription changes the entire operating model. Instead of chasing new customers constantly, the company optimizes for retention and account lifetime value. Chewy's free cash flow hit a record $562 million in 2025, partly because the business can run capital-light while optimizing delivery density and margin improvement. Sunrun's storage attachment rate rising from 71% in Q4 2025 to 74% in Q2 2026 shows how a contractor becomes a platform by turning customers into subscribers who keep adding services. Once the customer is in the replenishment cycle, capturing more value is a margin question, not an acquisition question.

Why now

Replenishment subscriptions scaled in 2024-2025 because technology made both signup and default-setting frictionless. Chewy's mobile app makes automatic reordering intuitive. Sunrun's financing and online account management made residential solar feel less like a one-time construction project and more like a service subscription. SiriusXM's bundled car-stereo presence and free-trial funnel (7.5 million in Q2 2026) created massive numbers of people already in the subscription habit before they made an active choice. Consumer behavior also shifted after the pandemic toward convenience, home delivery, and predictability. Autoship felt premium before 2020; now it feels normal. That cultural shift validated the business model at scale. The data inflection point was Q2 2026, when SiriusXM reported record-low 1.4% churn despite pricing pressure, Chewy hit $597 per-customer spending despite discretionary retrenchment, and Sunrun reached 74% storage attachment—showing that replenishment subscriptions are insulated from macro softness in ways that discretionary spending is not.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Customers pay through potentially higher lifetime costs compared to one-time purchases because autoship pricing is often sticky and forgetting to cancel means years of recurring charges. Chewy customers paying regular prices; over a customer lifetime with 21.3 million active customers and $597 annual sales per customer, that is over $12.7 billion in annual recurring spend. Sunrun customers locked into 25-year power purchase agreements are locked into multiyear commitment. SiriusXM subscribers paying annual renewal costs with high switching friction. The broader economy pays through reduced price competition—when 84% of pet food spending is locked into Autoship, independent pet food retailers have no traffic.
Who profits
Chewy generated record free cash flow of $562 million in 2025 on 6.2% revenue growth and expanding profitability. SiriusXM's 1.4% monthly churn and $2.625 billion adjusted EBITDA guidance for 2026 on essentially flat revenue growth shows the business model converts customer base into cash generation without requiring growth. Sunrun's contracted customer base creates multiyear cash-flow visibility. Investors profit by holding companies where customer acquisition is solved and the remaining problem is holding churn at utility-level rates through capital allocation rather than growth strategies.

The tension

Replenishment subscriptions are vulnerable to a single bad experience triggering cancellation. One missed pet-food delivery, one wrong billing charge, one customer-service failure can cascade into cancellation in ways that one-time purchases cannot. Chewy's Q2 2026 results included 200K net customer additions, healthy but showing the business still adds customers, not purely running on inertia. Price sensitivity can spike if subscription customers become conscious of their recurring spend. SiriusXM's negative net additions in Q1 2026 suggest that even with low churn, price resistance can reduce positive adds when trial-to-paid conversion slows. Regulatory risk is real: if policymakers decide that subscription defaults are manipulative or if cancellation friction is deemed unfair, the entire model could face friction. Additionally, if alternative products emerge that solve the underlying need (e.g., local pet food delivery from Amazon), the autoship stickiness evaporates.

The surprising part

Autoship subscriptions have nothing to do with brand loyalty or customer love. They work because they exploit inertia and default bias. Chewy does not need customers to prefer Chewy pet food over alternatives; it only needs them to have pet food delivered automatically and forget to cancel. SiriusXM does not need listeners to prefer satellite radio culturally; it only needs them to have in-car audio defaulted to the subscription and not realize how much they are paying annually. Sunrun does not need homeowners to be passionate about solar; it only needs them locked into a 25-year power purchase agreement. The model is brilliant but behaviorally exploitative. The surprise is that this works at massive scale—21.3 million Chewy customers, 33 million SiriusXM subscribers, 1.206 million Sunrun customers—proving that inertia and default bias are more powerful than active brand preference.

Why it matters

For ordinary people, the autoship story matters because it reveals how technology and behavioral economics can be weaponized to lock in recurring spending that consumers may not actively want to continue. The business model works because most people do not actively monitor or cancel their subscriptions. For policymakers, the stake is whether default-bias and cancellation friction constitute unfair practices or whether consumers bear responsibility for managing their own subscriptions. The broader stake is whether the subscription economy's efficiency is worth its cost to consumer autonomy.

Autoship subscriptions convert one purchase decision into a lifetime revenue stream by exploiting the difference between enrollment friction and cancellation friction.
