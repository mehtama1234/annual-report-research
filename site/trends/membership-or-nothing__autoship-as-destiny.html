Autoship as Destiny: Replenishment Subscriptions That Never Get Cancelled

The most durable form of membership is the replenishment subscription where the customer made one decision to buy and then stops making the decision each time. Chewy, Sunrun, and SiriusXM show that once a consumer enters an auto-replenishment cycle, churn approaches zero.

The purest form of membership-or-nothing is the replenishment subscription, where customers do not actively decide to purchase each cycle because they made the decision once and automated the rest. Chewy exemplifies this: 84% of net sales came from Autoship in Q3 2025, with 21.3M active customers and $595 net sales per active customer by year-end 2025. Sirius XM (93% subscriber revenue, record-low 1.4% monthly churn in Q2 2026) and Sunrun (1.206M contracted customers, 74% storage attachment) show the same pattern. Once a customer enters the replenishment cycle, cancellation friction is so high that the relationship behaves like a utility. This is the addiction dividend applied to commerce itself.

The mechanism

Autoship subscriptions work because they exploit a behavioral asymmetry: the friction to enroll is lower than the friction to cancel. Chewy customers sign up for automatic monthly pet-food replenishment because the convenience is obvious and the switching cost is zero at signup. But once the subscription is active, cancellation requires explicit action—visiting the website, confirming the cancellation, potentially losing accumulated benefits. Most customers do not bother. Q3 2025 showed Chewy with 84% of sales from Autoship. That is not because 84% of customers prefer automatic delivery; it is because the default has been set and changing it requires conscious effort. The business model depends on inertia.

The replenishment logic is different from traditional membership because there is no annual decision point. Gym memberships create a moment each year where a customer can reconsider. Autoship subscriptions do not. They renew every month, every quarter, or every delivery cycle with no friction and no opt-in moment. Chewy's $595 net sales per active customer reflects not high spending but the cumulative effect of automated, recurring purchases that customers have forgotten they are making. SiriusXM's 1.4% monthly churn (record-low in Q2 2026) is the proof point. That is not churn in a traditional sense; that is the baseline exit rate once normal attrition is removed. The business has achieved near-utility-level stickiness.

The migration from one-time purchase to replenishment subscription changes the entire operating model. Instead of chasing new customers constantly, the company optimizes for retention and account lifetime value. Chewy's free cash flow hit a record $562M in 2025, partly because the business can run capital-light while optimizing delivery density and gross margin improvement. Sunrun's storage attachment rate rising from 71% (Q4 2025) to 74% (Q2 2026) shows how a contractor becomes a platform by turning customers into subscribers who keep adding services. Once the customer is in the replenishment cycle, capturing more value is a margin question, not an acquisition question.

Why now

Replenishment subscriptions scaled in 2024-2025 because technology made both signup and default-setting frictionless. Chewy's mobile app makes automatic reordering intuitive. Sunrun's financing and online account management made residential solar feel less like a one-time construction project and more like a service subscription. SiriusXM's bundled car-stereo presence and free-trial funnel (7.5M in Q2 2026) created massive numbers of people already in the subscription habit. Additionally, consumer behavior shifted after the pandemic toward convenience, home delivery, and predictability. Autoship felt premium before the pandemic; now it feels normal. That cultural shift validated the business model at scale.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Customers in replenishment subscriptions pay through potentially higher total lifetime costs compared to one-time purchases, because autoship pricing is often sticky and forgetting to cancel means years of recurring charges. Chewy customers are paying regular prices; over a customer lifetime with 21.3M active customers and $595 annual sales per customer, that is over $12B in annual recurring spend. Sunrun customers are locked into 25-year power-purchase agreements, meaning they are paying through long-term contractual commitment. SiriusXM subscribers are paying annual renewal costs with high switching friction.
Who profits
The companies capturing the replenishment value are extraordinarily profitable. Chewy generated record free cash flow of $562M in 2025 on 6.2% revenue growth and expanding profitability. SiriusXM's 1.4% monthly churn and $2.625B of adjusted EBITDA (2026 guidance) on flat revenue growth shows that the business model converts customer base into cash generation without requiring growth. Sunrun's contracted customer base ($3.677B in contracted net earning assets) creates multiyear cash-flow visibility. Investors profit by holding companies where the hard problem (customer acquisition) is solved and the remaining problem (holding churn at utility-level rates) is a capital allocation question rather than a growth question.

The tension

The deepest tension is that replenishment subscriptions are vulnerable to a single bad experience that prompts cancellation. One missed pet-food delivery, one wrong billing charge, one customer-service failure can cascade into cancellation in a way that one-time purchases cannot. Chewy's Q2 2026 results included 200K net customer additions, which is healthy but shows the business is still adding customers, not purely running off inertia. Additionally, price sensitivity can spike if subscription customers become conscious of their recurring spend. SiriusXM's negative net additions in Q1 2026 suggest that even with low churn, price resistance can reduce positive adds when trial-to-paid conversion slows. Finally, the regulatory risk is real: if policymakers decide that subscription defaults are manipulative, or if cancellation friction is deemed unfair, the entire model could face friction.

The surprising part

The non-obvious insight is that autoship subscriptions have nothing to do with brand loyalty or customer love. They work because they exploit inertia and default bias. Chewy does not need customers to prefer Chewy pet food over alternatives; it only needs them to have pet food delivered automatically and forget to cancel. SiriusXM does not need listeners to prefer satellite radio for cultural reasons; it only needs them to have in-car audio defaulted to the subscription and not realize how much they are paying annually. Sunrun does not need homeowners to be passionate about solar; it only needs them to be locked into a 25-year power purchase agreement. The model is brilliant but behaviorally exploitative.

Why it matters

For ordinary people, the autoship story matters because it reveals how technology and behavioral economics can be weaponized to lock in recurring spending that consumers may not actively want to continue. The business model works because most people do not actively monitor or cancel their subscriptions. For policymakers, the stake is whether default-bias and cancellation friction constitute unfair practices, or whether consumers bear responsibility for managing their own subscriptions.

Autoship subscriptions convert one purchase decision into a lifetime revenue stream by exploiting the difference between enrollment friction and cancellation friction.
