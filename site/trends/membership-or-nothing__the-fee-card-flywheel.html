The mechanism

The fee-card flywheel works by decoupling card revenue from spending volume. Historical credit cards made money on merchant fees (1-2% of transaction value) and interest on balances (for non-premium segments). Premium cards inverted that: annual fees ($195-695) became the primary revenue source, with merchant rebates positioned as a benefit that justifies the fee rather than a cost center. American Express's 30 consecutive quarters of double-digit net card fee growth, with 70%+ of new cards on fee-paying products, proves the model flipped. Customers are paying fees regardless of spending; the spending is secondary. The card is now a membership product sold on benefits—airport lounge access, concierge service, purchase protections, earning rates—that encourage higher spending and deeper engagement, but the baseline revenue is the fee, not the transaction.

The loyalty integration accelerates the flywheel. Marriott's 75% of U.S. room nights from Bonvoy members represents a customer cohort that is not just transactional; they are locked into an ecosystem where the Bonvoy card is the enrollment layer. A customer with a Marriott card earns points at Marriott hotels, point transfers to Marriott partners, redeems points for nights, and uses those nights at Bonvoy hotels more frequently because they have a card. Each layer of that cycle reinforces the others. The $60M Q1 2026 increase in Marriott's co-branded card fees shows that the card is not just a payment tool; it is a commerce layer that drives hotel spending while generating independent recurring revenue. The card becomes the gate through which all loyalty behavior flows.

The compounding arrives because fee-card customers are high-engagement, high-retention cohorts. Customers who pay $195+ annually for a card are self-selecting for spending intent and engagement. Once enrolled, they are stickier than non-card members because canceling the card means losing the membership benefits and accumulated loyalty currency. That changes customer acquisition and retention economics: American Express can spend more on customer acquisition because lifetime value is higher; churn is lower because exit costs (lost benefits, lost points) are psychologically high. Marriott can reward card holders with accelerated earning, which drives hotel stay frequency, which makes the card more valuable, which reduces cancellation. The card becomes the core relationship, and the loyalty program is the tool that makes the card stickier. The recurring fee creates predictable recurring revenue that does not depend on discretionary spending.

Why now

Three dynamics crystallized the fee-card-as-membership inflection. First, points inflation and loyalty program devaluation through 2015-2020 trained premium customers to expect higher earning rates and better redemption options in exchange for fees. As hotel, airline, and credit card programs devalued free or low-spend points, premium customers who spent more began to expect programs that were worth the cost. Second, digital personalization made benefit delivery achievable at card-product level. American Express can now surface personalized offers, benefits, and earning boosters at the card level, making the annual fee feel warranted to each customer individually. Third, concentrated consumer spending (top 20% of households drive 80%+ of credit volume) made acquiring and retaining that cohort essential for card issuers. Fee-paying products target that top 20%; the 30-quarter streak of double-digit fee growth shows the economics are favorable and the addressable market is large enough to be valuable. American Express explicitly disclosed this inflection in 2015-2018 when fee card mix crossed 50% of new acquisitions; by Q1 2026, that mix reached 70%+.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Consumers in premium card tiers pay annual fees ($195-695) and in many cases restructure their spending to maximize card benefits, which is an implicit opportunity cost. Merchants pay higher interchange fees when they process premium credit card transactions. Consumers who do not qualify for premium cards or who choose not to pay fees are excluded from premium travel programs and loyalty acceleration, creating a two-tier system where wealthy and engaged consumers get better economics. Hospitality companies (hotels, airlines, restaurants) pay in the form of accelerated earning rates and transferred points, which compress their ability to make profits on loyalty customers—though this is often acceptable because card-driven loyalty customers spend more overall and are lower churn.
Who profits
American Express and Marriott (as co-brand partner) capture outsized value. American Express's 30 consecutive quarters of double-digit card fee growth show the model is highly profitable. Marriott benefits from card-driven hotel spending increase and card fee revenue sharing. Delta, Hyatt, and other co-branded card partners benefit similarly through card fee splits and accelerated earning-rate economics. Capital markets reward this: American Express trades at a premium multiple because the card-membership model is seen as durable and high-margin. The secondary beneficiaries are tier-1 customers (top 20% of spenders) who get better earning rates, perks, and benefits through fee-paying products. Merchants with high premium-cardholder customer bases benefit from increased transaction volume and customer concentration.

The tension

The tension is that fee-paying cards create a stratified system where wealthier customers get better economics, which reinforces inequality. A customer with $195 annual fees who earns 5 points per dollar at Marriott will accumulate far more valuable hotel stays than a non-card customer earning 1 point per dollar. Over a decade, that difference compounds into tens of thousands of dollars of differential value. Second, the model is vulnerable to paying customers downgrading if perceived benefits decline. American Express's 30 quarters of growth rely on customers feeling the annual fee is worth the benefits; if airline lounges become crowded, concierge quality declines, or earning rates fall, cancellations spike. Third, the concentration of rewards and benefits into premium cohorts is starting to trigger regulatory scrutiny around fairness and discrimination. Fourth, the flywheel depends on merchant acceptance and loyalty program durability; if Marriott devalues points or reduces earning rates, card value declines and the $60M fee revenue becomes at risk.

The surprising part

The non-obvious part is that card fee growth and spending volume are decoupling. A customer paying $195 annually is not necessarily spending 50% more than a non-card customer; they may be spending 20% more or even the same amount. American Express's ability to hit 30 consecutive quarters of double-digit card fee growth even as spending and payment volumes moderated in 2025 shows the fee is not dependent on transaction volume. The card itself is the business model. The fee is the anchor; the benefits and loyalty program are the justification for holding. The second surprise is that churn is lower than capital markets expected. American Express has disclosed that card cancellation rates have fallen even as benefits have been tightened, suggesting that the psychological switching costs (lost points, lost status, lost benefits) are more binding than the actual value calculation. That means American Express can raise fees faster than traditional product economics would suggest.

Why it matters

When premium credit cards become the membership layer that controls access to loyalty programs, travel upgrades, and benefits, economic outcomes begin to stratify by credit-cardholder status. Wealthy customers get better hotel rates, airline seats, and earning rates through premium cards; non-card customers get worse economics in the same exact loyalty program. Over time, this creates compound inequality—better rewards accelerate wealth accumulation while worse economics concentrate limitations. The social stake is that core commercial relationships (hotels, airlines, travel) are increasingly mediated by a payment technology that is most valuable to people who already have capital. That transforms what used to be a service industry focused on customer satisfaction into a capital-allocation system that rewards already-wealthy customers. The broader economy bifurcates: one tier enjoys premium economics through fee-card access, while the other pays higher unit prices for the same services without access to the rewards infrastructure.

Premium credit cards have transformed from payment tools into recurring membership products where annual fees are the revenue anchor and loyalty benefits are the justification for holding.
