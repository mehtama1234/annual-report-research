Trends · Membership or Nothing

Who Pays the Non-Member Tax

Membership-conditional businesses extract a hidden tax from non-members by offering better prices, access, or convenience to members only. Non-members pay higher prices, face worse availability, or accept exclusion while members subsidize their experience.

Bath & Body Works' 40M active loyalty members get targeted pricing and first access to new products; Planet Fitness' 20.8M members pay affordable recurring dues while non-members cannot participate at any price; Starbucks' app users get faster access and payment incentives while cash customers face longer lines. The business model works by segmenting customers into members (who get optimization) and non-members (who pay hidden tax). The tax can be direct (higher prices for non-members), access-based (non-members excluded entirely), or convenience-based (non-members face slower service). All three are present simultaneously in mature membership systems.

The mechanism

The non-member tax operates through three reinforcing mechanisms. First, price discrimination: membership-conditional platforms offer members lower effective prices through discounts, loyalty rewards, or bulk economics. Bath & Body Works' 40M active members receive targeted pricing and early access to sales; non-members pay regular prices and access inventory after members purchase. Planet Fitness' $15/month dues are artificially low because they depend on the volume density of member utilization; a non-member cannot access any single visit pricing because there is none—the service is membership-only. Second, access control: some membership platforms make participation conditional on membership itself. Planet Fitness fundamentally excludes non-members; the service does not exist without membership. Starbucks membership (through the app and digital rewards) creates a faster lane for app users; non-app customers face longer lines and receive no rewards or personalized pricing. Third, psychological lock-in: members are habituated through frequent interaction, points accumulation, and convenience increments. Bath & Body Works' 40M loyalty members become habitual shoppers because loyalty points make small purchases feel like wins. Planet Fitness members show up because the monthly fee feels sunk and the "Judgement Free Zone" removes friction. The non-member, by contrast, always faces full prices and barrier friction.

The tax emerges because these mechanisms create a stratified customer base. Members receive optimization (faster service, lower prices, personalized offerings), while non-members subsidize the economics through higher prices or exclusion. Bath & Body Works' softening sales (down 0.2% FY2025) despite 40M active members shows the dynamic: the loyalty-member base is large and engaged, but the broader customer pool is shrinking because non-members cannot get the same deals. Planet Fitness' model is even more explicit: the $15/month price is only for new members, and the "Judgement Free Zone" messaging targets a specific psychological comfort for people who feel excluded from premium fitness culture. Non-members simply do not buy the service; they are not a segment to optimize.

The mechanics become visible in pricing changes. When Bath & Body Works needs revenue growth but faces member discounting, it must either raise core prices (hurting non-member value perception) or expand products aimed at non-members (lower-margin, candles vs. lotions). When Planet Fitness planned a national Black Card price increase in Q1 2026, membership churn threatened because the value proposition shifted; management paused the increase and instead began a broader pricing review. Both moves signal that the membership model is sensitive to non-member perception: if non-members perceive pricing as unfair or exclusionary, word-of-mouth and cultural legitimacy decline, which limits sustainable membership pricing power. The tax can only be large if society perceives membership as fair—i.e., that members get real value for their fee. Once the tax becomes visible, resentment builds and willingness to pay membership fees erodes.

Why now

Membership-conditional pricing has always existed, but the scale and integration have intensified since 2020. Costco, Starbucks, Target's Cartwheel/Circle, and Planet Fitness have all doubled down on membership lock-in as a core strategy. Bath & Body Works' loyalty program shifted from occasional-shopper incentive to core-customer subsidy system. As inflation squeezed margins everywhere, companies discovered that membership pricing discipline allowed them to extract more from core customers without appearing to raise headline prices. The "non-member tax" is not new, but it is now systemic, visible, and driving stratification. It appears now because membership platforms have matured enough that they no longer need universal customer appeal; they can optimize for members and simply exclude non-members.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Non-members pay directly through higher prices on discretionary goods and services. Casual customers of Bath & Body Works pay more because core loyalty members get early access and discounts. Non-Starbucks-app users pay full price and face slower service. Non-Planet Fitness members cannot access fitness at any price; they are excluded entirely. The broader tax falls on people without the time, knowledge, or social capital to navigate membership-conditional systems: elderly shoppers, low-income customers, first-time visitors, and immigrants who are not yet integrated into local commercial systems.
Who profits
Membership platforms capture higher margins on core customers by segmenting price-sensitive members from willingness-to-pay customers. Bath & Body Works' margin profile improves because loyalty members get deals that drive volume, while non-members pay full price, netting higher average price. Planet Fitness profits from predictable recurring dues and high member density, which allows franchisees to run leaner unit economics. App-integrated platforms (Starbucks) capture payment data and timing, which improves forecasting, menu engineering, and labor scheduling. Financial beneficiaries include platform operators and franchisees with capital invested in membership infrastructure.

The tension

The tension appears when membership platforms grow large enough that non-member exclusion becomes politically or culturally visible. If 30% of fitness users are Planet Fitness members and everyone knows non-members cannot access the service at any price, equity concerns arise. If loyalty members get 20%+ better prices than non-members, resentment builds. The other tension is competitive: as membership platforms consolidate market share, non-members lose optionality entirely. In a fragmented market, non-members can shop competitors; in a consolidated one, non-members face platform power with no exit. Bath & Body Works' sales stagnation despite 40M members shows this risk: the loyalty program is optimized for members and excludes growth from non-members, leaving the company trapped between loyalty economics and top-line stagnation.

The surprising part

The surprising element is how explicitly profitable and defensible the non-member tax has become. Thirty years ago, loyalty programs were marginal—discount options for occasional shoppers. Now they are core strategy: Bath & Body Works' 40M members drive most profitable volume; Planet Fitness could not exist without membership dues; Starbucks' app is the primary interaction layer. Membership-exclusion is no longer apologetic; it is optimized. The surprise is that this has not driven more competitive entry or customer rebellion. Non-members mostly accept membership fees as normal rather than as a tax. That acceptance is the structural risk: when non-members normalize exclusion, membership platforms can raise fees without limit.

Why it matters

An economy stratified by membership is one where access to goods and services becomes conditional on enrollment and loyalty. That is efficient for merchants but exclusionary for consumers. It is particularly harmful for people who are not digitally native, elderly, or low-income—groups that are least likely to navigate multiple loyalty platforms and most likely to face higher prices as non-members. The cultural consequence is the normalization of contingent access: fitness only if you pay monthly; discounts only if you sign up; fair prices only if you join. That transforms capitalism from "here is what we sell, and here is the price" to "join our membership and we will think about treating you fairly." That is a fundamental shift in the seller-buyer relationship.
