Trends · Membership or Nothing

Who Pays the Non-Member Tax

Every membership platform runs on money that comes from somewhere. The predictable dues that let Planet Fitness raise its new-member Classic Card price 50% and still add 1.1 million members in 2025, or let American Express post a 30th straight quarter of double-digit card-fee growth, are also the money that funds cheaper prices, richer perks, and heavier acquisition for the people who signed up. The non-member and the marginal member pay the higher per-unit price that keeps the machine fed.

Recurring membership does not just lock in the member; it reprices everyone outside the fence. The bet is that predictable dues and fees let a platform lower the price a member sees, raise acquisition spend, and widen the gap over casual buyers who cannot replicate the behavior. Planet Fitness carried a 50% price increase for new Classic Card members through its first full year and still grew net membership by 1.1 million in 2025, then returned $200M to shareholders in a single quarter out of that dues stream. American Express acquired 12.5 million new cards in 2025 with 70%+ on fee-paying products, its net card fee posting a 30th consecutive quarter of double-digit growth. The fee base is what pays for the member's rewards and the issuer's growth. The non-member funds a model built around someone else and gets the list price.

The mechanism

Start with what recurring dues actually buy the platform: certainty. Planet Fitness collected from roughly 20.8 million members at year-end 2025 whether or not those members walked into a club. That predictability is why the company could push through a 50% increase in the new-member Classic Card price, hold it through the entire year, and still add 1.1 million net members and grow same-club sales 6.7%. Predictable cash lets a platform decouple two things that are joined at the hip for a casual buyer: the price you pay and the value you take. A Black Card member paying $24.99 a month gets guest privileges, travel access, and amenities that would be priced far higher a la carte. The dues base underwrites that gap. The person who is not a member, or who would pay per-visit, faces the undiscounted price that keeps the model whole.

The fee then becomes the growth engine, and the growth is aimed at more members, not at the outsider. American Express acquired 12.5 million new cards in 2025 with more than 70% on fee-paying products, and its net card fee revenue posted a 30th straight quarter of double-digit growth. That fee stream funds the lounges, credits, and partner benefits that make the card worth holding, and it funds the acquisition machine that goes after the next high-spending cohort. The economics run in a loop that excludes the non-member by design: fees pay for rewards, rewards raise retention and spend, higher spend and lower churn justify heavier acquisition, and the merchant and the cash-paying customer sit outside the loop absorbing the interchange and the list price that make it all pencil out.

The cross-subsidy hardens when membership behavior compounds into something a casual user cannot replicate. Chewy runs roughly 84% of net sales through Autoship at about $595 per active customer; that is a household that has handed over its replenishment cadence, and the platform prices and forecasts against it. SiriusXM holds churn at a record-low 1.4% across about 33 million subscribers, with 93% of the SiriusXM segment coming from subscription revenue, which lets it treat the base as an annuity and spend the trial funnel to refill it. The counterexample is the discipline in the thesis: Bath & Body Works has 40 million active loyalty members and still watched FY2025 net sales slip 0.2% to $7.291B and operating income fall 11% to $1.126B. A membership file is not a membership flywheel. The tax on the non-member only exists where the recurring relationship is deep enough to reprice the inside; where it is shallow, the platform is just a retailer with a list.

Why now

The clean inflection is Planet Fitness's 2025 fiscal year: the company itself described 2025 as the first full year after the 50% price increase for new Classic Card members, and it still grew net membership by 1.1 million, grew same-club sales 6.7%, and grew revenue 12.1% to about $1.3 billion. That is the moment the mechanism became visible rather than theoretical, a membership platform raised the entry price by half, held it a full year, and the base did not break, proving that predictable dues let a platform reprice around its members and absorb the rejection of the marginal buyer. It landed alongside American Express crossing a 30th consecutive quarter of double-digit net card fee growth in Q4 2025 with 70%+ of new cards fee-paying, confirming the same fee-first logic in a completely different category. The two together, in the same 2025 window, mark the point where recurring-fee repricing stopped being a tech-platform story and became the operating default across fitness, credit, retail, and audio.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
The marginal and non-member pays first, in the price. Planet Fitness could raise the new-member Classic Card price 50% precisely because the existing base was locked in, so the person joining now, or the person choosing a per-visit gym instead, carries the higher entry cost while insiders sit on grandfathered dues and Black Card amenities. Merchants pay in interchange: every premium American Express transaction carries a higher fee that funds cardholder rewards, and that cost is spread into shelf prices paid by everyone, including customers who will never hold the card. The casual or one-time buyer pays by getting the undiscounted unit, no Autoship pricing at Chewy, no bundle economics, no accumulated status, while the recurring customer's cadence lets the platform forecast, discount, and lock them in. And shareholders are paid out of the same stream that could have gone to lower prices: Planet Fitness returned $200.0M via 4.0 million shares repurchased in a single quarter, Q2 2026, drawn from dues collected across the base.
Who profits
The platforms that own the recurring relationship, and the members and owners inside the fence. Planet Fitness converts 20.8 million members' dues into pricing power, a 6.7% same-club sales gain, and buyback capacity while keeping $544.4M of cash on hand. American Express captures the fee: 12.5 million new cards in 2025, 70%+ fee-paying, a 30th straight double-digit net-card-fee quarter, and a premium equity multiple that rewards the annuity. Chewy monetizes ~84% Autoship at ~$595 per active customer as a forecastable, high-retention book. SiriusXM turns 1.4% churn across ~33 million subscribers into an annuity that funds content and trials. Inside each, the committed member profits too, cheaper effective per-unit pricing, richer perks, accumulated status, and lower switching cost, all funded by the fee base and the outsider's list price. Bath & Body Works marks the boundary: 40 million members but falling sales and an 11% operating-income drop show the profit accrues to depth of the recurring relationship, not to the size of the roster.

The tension

The tension is that the non-member tax only holds while the fence holds, and the fence is behavioral. Planet Fitness proved a 50% entry-price increase could stick for a full year, but by early 2026 it flagged that the year started slower than expected on net member growth and paused a planned national Black Card price increase pending review, a direct signal that repricing power has a ceiling and that the marginal member can still walk. The model also assumes the inside stays cheap enough and rich enough to justify the fee; American Express's 30-quarter streak depends on lounges, credits, and status feeling worth the annual fee, and any erosion of that value converts locked-in members back into price-sensitive shoppers. Meanwhile the outsider is not infinitely taxable, if list prices and interchange-loaded shelf prices climb too far above the effective member price, the non-member exits the category or the merchant pushes back, and the subsidy that funds acquisition dries up at the source.

The surprising part

The surprising part is that the strongest evidence of the tax is a price increase that did not cost the base, and the clearest counterexample is a giant loyalty program that could not levy the tax at all. Planet Fitness raised its new-member entry price by 50%, held it through all of 2025, and still added 1.1 million net members, so the very move that should have driven customers away instead demonstrated how little pricing power the platform had surrendered to them. Set that against Bath & Body Works: 40 million active loyalty members, larger than Planet Fitness's entire membership base, and yet FY2025 net sales still fell 0.2% and operating income fell 11%. The non-obvious lesson is that the non-member tax is not a function of how many members you have logged. It is a function of whether the recurring relationship is deep enough to reprice around, dues you owe whether you show up, a fee you renew, an Autoship cadence you forget to cancel. A loyalty card you swipe for points is not deep enough, and the platform holding it collects no tax on anyone.

Why it matters

When recurring membership sets the effective price of a service, the same gym, card, or store quietly runs two price systems: a subsidized one for the committed insider and a list one for everyone else. Planet Fitness's ability to raise the new-member entry price 50% while grandfathering the base means the newcomer and the person who cannot commit to dues pay more for the identical treadmill; American Express's interchange spreads the cost of cardholder rewards into shelf prices paid by cash customers who never see a lounge. The stake is that access to the lower price increasingly requires the ability and willingness to prepay, to hand over a recurring cadence, to hold a fee-paying card, which sorts people by capital and by behavior, not by need. Over time a service economy that once competed on satisfying whoever walked in reorganizes around whoever will commit to a recurring bill, and the person who pays as they go, often the person with the least slack, pays the most per unit to subsidize the person who pays the least.

Predictable membership dues let platforms cut the price a member sees, spend harder to win the next member, and hand the undiscounted list price and the interchange-loaded shelf to the non-member who funds a model built around someone else.
