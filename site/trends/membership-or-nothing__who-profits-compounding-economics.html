The Compounding Machine: Who Captures the Economics of Predictability

Recurring revenue does not just steady earnings. It hands incumbents a capital weapon: American Express has grown card-fee revenue for 30 straight quarters, Chewy's Autoship is 84% of sales, and Uber One members drive half of $58B in quarterly bookings. Predictable cash lets them subsidize growth that transactional rivals cannot finance, and non-members quietly pay for it.

Membership economics compound faster than transactional revenue because predictability unlocks three capital advantages at once: acquisition spend against a known lifetime value, the ability to price the member experience below transaction value, and the balance-sheet room to fund moats (loyalty tech, logistics, content) a transaction-based rival cannot match. American Express acquired 12.5 million new cards in 2025 with 70%+ on fee-paying products; Chewy runs 84% Autoship mix at $595 net sales per active customer; Uber One's 50M members drive roughly half of gross bookings across two surfaces. The companies that own the recurring relationship do not just grow faster; they become structurally hard to displace.

The mechanism

Recurring revenue is a capital-allocation multiplier. Once a company locks in predictable customer cash flow, it can borrow against that stream, invest in competitive infrastructure at below-market returns, and undercut non-member rivals without immediate pressure to break even. American Express is the cleanest case: 30 consecutive quarters of double-digit card-fee growth, 12.5 million new cards acquired in 2025 with more than 70% on fee-paying products, and $1.67 trillion in billed business. That recurring fee stream funds a global travel, dining, and merchant-partnership ecosystem, plus AI-driven commerce positioning, that a transaction-focused competitor cannot afford, and it lifts EPS to $15.38 while keeping credit performance best-in-class.

The second advantage flips acquisition economics toward the member business. Chewy shows it with unusual clarity: Autoship customers were about 84% of net sales in Q3 2025, and that predictability let the company raise gross margin 50 basis points to 30.1% in Q1 2026 while still adding nearly 200,000 net active customers, at $595 net sales per active customer. The margin expansion on a recurring base pays for the next acquisition wave. Non-member pet retailers cannot match that spend because their revenue is episodic, so each customer is a one-off bet. Chewy's 21.3M active customers and $562M of FY2025 free cash flow are proof that recurring purchase behavior can fund competitive growth that transaction models cannot sustain.

The third advantage appears at platform scale: cross-subsidy and ecosystem lock-in that is unbeatable at scope. Uber One's roughly 50M members drive about half of gross bookings across Mobility and Delivery despite being a subset of 208M monthly users, so Uber can subsidize member prices to pull volume that makes the platform more valuable to drivers and merchants, which strengthens the network for everyone. Q2 2026 shows the result: $58.0B in gross bookings, $2.4B GAAP net income, and $2.8B free cash flow, with adjusted EBITDA up 33%. Amazon works the same way: Q2 2026 subscription services were $13.7B, but the real value is that Prime members shop more categories and higher frequency, which feeds $19.8B in advertising and $46.8B in third-party seller services. The recurring relationship does not just monetize through the fee; it unlocks secondary and tertiary value streams that accrue only to the member ecosystem.

Why now

The inflection is not the emergence of subscriptions; it is their maturation into structural dominance across every consumer sector at once, visible in the FY2025 and 2026 data. AmEx's card-fee revenue reached its 30th consecutive quarter of double-digit growth; Uber One passed 50M members driving half of gross bookings; Chewy's Autoship reached 84% of net sales; Sirius XM's churn hit a record-low 1.4%. What changed is that the capital advantage flipped: in a post-2022 environment of higher labor, logistics, and content costs, predictable cash lets incumbents amortize those costs across a known customer lifetime while a transaction-dependent rival must raise capital, hit quarterly targets, and justify spend against immediate revenue. Sirius XM's 1.4% churn means it can invest in podcast content knowing the cash will recur, while an ad-supported radio player cannot make that bet without hitting earnings immediately. Membership has become the gating layer for the whole ecosystem (Uber for movement, Amazon for shopping, Chewy for pet care, Sirius XM for audio), so transactional competitors are not losing on price; they are losing because they cannot fund the tech, content, logistics, and personalization that membership capital enables. The separation is widening now because scale is compounding faster than marginal competition can catch it.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Individual consumers pay the fee, but the real cost runs deeper. Amazon Prime members pay their annual fee and also bear higher prices on third-party items (seller monetization is $46.8B a quarter), see targeted advertising, and normalize a same-day-delivery habit that reshapes total wallet allocation. Chewy Autoship customers get convenience and recurring discounts, but at 84% of revenue the non-Autoship shopper is increasingly marginal. Uber One members pay a fixed fee that lets Uber subsidize member ride and delivery prices, which means occasional non-members pay more per trip and order than members do. The arithmetic is close to zero-sum between members and non-members: members get ecosystem investment and lower effective pricing, non-members pay full freight and are shut out of the secondary value (ad targeting, cross-category discounts, service attachment). This is consumer math, not brand storytelling.
Who profits
Three constituencies capture the value, in order of structural advantage. First, the incumbent platform keeps the residual after acquisition and infrastructure: Amazon's $77.7B FY2025 net income comes only partly from the $13.7B quarterly subscription line and mostly from advertising, seller services, and logistics velocity that membership enables; Uber's $2.4B Q2 2026 net income is the compounding return on Uber One driving volume across both rideshare and Eats. Second, capital providers win because recurring revenue reduces tail risk and creates refinancing options; a company with $2.8B of quarterly free cash flow and SaaS-like predictability (Uber, Chewy, Sirius XM) can carry more leverage and return capital more aggressively than a transaction-dependent rival. Third, employees and service providers in these ecosystems benefit from scale and job creation, but only while the member base keeps growing. Non-members, non-partner merchants, and non-ecosystem players do not participate in the compounding.

The tension

The machine has two breaking points. First, membership requires constant perceived value at the margin: AmEx has 30 quarters of fee growth because premium consumers judge card benefits and merchant networks worth more each year, and the moment that perceived value flattens (travel perks saturate, dining partners commoditize), fee growth stops. Chewy's 50-basis-point margin gain assumes Autoship stays sticky and lifetime value keeps rising; Sirius XM's 1.4% churn is fortress-like now but defended by content exclusivity and in-car distribution that a new audio platform could erode. Second, membership economics are financed by members themselves, and the higher prices non-members pay plus the ecosystem investment funded by recurring fees eventually invite a simpler, lower-cost attacker. Subscriptions are already splintering (Spotify versus Sirius, DashPass versus a broader membership, streaming services traded in and out), so the compounding holds only while the member base stays large and the value gap versus non-members stays wide. Break either pressure and the engine loses its fuel.

The surprising part

The overlooked truth is that membership compounding is not mainly about higher margin on the fee itself. AmEx card fees are only part of the story; the real gain is that fee-paying members support higher-volume transaction activity across the network. Chewy's Q1 2026 margin expansion is counterintuitive because Autoship customers are higher-volume but lower average item price, so the 50-basis-point gain comes from reduced marketing spend and fulfillment density, not from charging more per unit. Uber One members do not pay dramatically more per ride; they pay a fixed annual fee and then trigger more frequent use. The compounding is volume-and-efficiency-based, not price-based, which means membership often relies on appearing to discount members while extracting more through frequency, duration, and lock-in. Amazon's real value from Prime is not the subscription line; it is that members shop more categories, see ads, and let $46.8B of third-party seller services and $19.8B of advertising fund the margin.

Why it matters

Membership is reshaping everyday economic access. Households that can afford the annual fees (Prime, Uber One, Chewy Autoship, Sirius XM) get lower effective pricing, faster service, and a better experience; households that cannot or will not pay often get worse service, higher prices, and no access to the secondary value (ad targeting, merchant partnerships, service attachment). The winners are not neutral, because they actively depend on a widening gap between member and non-member experience, creating a consumer stratification where good pricing and fast service are membership-gated rather than competition-driven. For workers and independent operators, membership consolidation locks customers into platforms that own pricing power and loyalty, making it harder to compete or negotiate terms. And the concentration of recurring revenue into a handful of mega-platforms concentrates capital-allocation power, so a few firms decide where investment flows across delivery, pet care, ride-sharing, and audio. The machine is efficient, but it is not neutral.

Membership converts a variable customer cost into a capital asset, letting incumbents subsidize growth that transactional rivals cannot finance, while non-members unknowingly pay for it through higher effective prices and exclusion from the ecosystem's secondary value.
