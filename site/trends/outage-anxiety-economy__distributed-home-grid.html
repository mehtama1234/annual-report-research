The Home as a Power Plant: Sunrun and the Residential Resilience Model

Rooftop solar has become resilience insurance—households buy battery backup not for environmental reasons but to protect themselves from grid unreliability and rising utility bills.

Sunrun's storage attachment rate reached a record 74% in Q2 2026, up from 71% in Q4 2025. The company now operates 4.647 GWh of networked storage capacity and frames itself as a distributed power plant operator rather than a solar installer. This reveals that household solar adoption has shifted from environmentalism to anxiety-driven resilience purchasing. Consumers are buying outage insurance and utility-rate protection, not panels.

The mechanism

The storage attachment rate is the clearest signal that customer motivation has shifted. When solar was first sold, batteries were optional add-ons—people bought panels for long-term savings, and batteries were for backup. In Q4 2025, 71% of Sunrun customers added storage; in Q2 2026, that figure jumped to 74%. This means that households are not choosing between solar and batteries. They are choosing solar specifically because it enables batteries. The driver is not cost savings alone—it is grid anxiety. Utility outages, extreme weather, and rising electricity prices have made backup power a necessity rather than a luxury.

The customer acquisition speed and retention pattern reflect resilience demand. Sunrun added 20,979 new customers in Q2 2026 despite reducing full-year guidance and acknowledging 'reduced affiliate-channel volumes' and 'delayed direct-sales ramp.' This suggests that core residential demand for the product (solar plus battery backup) remained strong even as growth metrics softened. Customers are willing to wait for installation, pay for financing complexity, and commit to long-term service agreements because they perceive outage risk as real and immediate.

Sunrun's pivot to calling itself a 'distributed power plant operator' reveals the infrastructure read. The company is no longer selling products; it is selling networked capacity. 4.647 GWh of networked storage capacity is not just a collection of household batteries. It is a controllable power source that can be dispatched to the grid during peak demand or outages, potentially offsetting utility generation needs and supporting grid stability. Sunrun manages these batteries as a fleet, not as individual customer assets. This transforms the customer relationship from 'you own a battery' to 'you own a battery that the grid can use.'

Why now

Three factors made this inflection visible in 2025-2026. First, grid reliability actually declined in several regions (California rolling blackouts, Texas summer alerts, Northeast winter stress). Second, customers experienced visible outages during extreme weather events in 2024-2025. Third, electricity prices rose materially, making utility-rate risk more tangible. For a household paying $150-200 per month, an outage or a 20% rate increase is no longer abstract—it is money. Sunrun's storage attachment rate jumped because customers now see the battery as insurance against both interruption and cost.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Households finance the battery systems through Sunrun contracts, typically over 20-25 years. The upfront cost is absorbed into monthly service fees rather than lump-sum purchases. This shifts the payment burden to the consumer but makes it affordable. However, the consumer is also paying an implicit tax: they are now responsible for managing their own power resilience rather than relying on a public utility to do so. This privatizes a public-infrastructure function—resilience becomes something wealthy neighborhoods get (via Sunrun adoption) while lower-income areas remain dependent on the utility grid.
Who profits
Sunrun captures recurring revenue through subscriber contracts, with $3.677B in contracted net earning assets as of Q2 2026. The company also benefits from grid-services opportunities—if regulators or utilities will pay Sunrun for the ability to dispatch networked household batteries during emergencies, that creates a second revenue stream. Utility companies could also benefit if Sunrun's distributed battery capacity reduces peak-demand stress and defers the need for utility-owned generation. However, utilities might also perceive Sunrun as a threat to their load growth and monopoly control of the power supply.

The tension

The model depends on continued grid unreliability and rising electricity prices. If utilities invest in resilience and prices stabilize, demand for Sunrun's products softens. Conversely, if outages become more frequent and rates keep rising, Sunrun could become essential infrastructure—but that level of stress might trigger regulatory backlash or utility pushback against distributed networks that compete with utility generation. There is also a wealth-bifurcation risk: Sunrun creates a two-tier power system where affluent households with solar-plus-battery systems achieve autonomy, while lower-income households remain grid-dependent and exposed to outages.

The surprising part

The growth rate remained resilient despite Sunrun lowering guidance and acknowledging execution challenges. This suggests that customer demand for battery-backed solar has become almost inelastic with respect to company performance. Households are not buying Sunrun because of perfect execution or marketing—they are buying it because they are anxious about the grid. This demand driver is independent of what Sunrun does well or poorly, which makes it more durable but also more vulnerable to a shift in customer perception if grid reliability actually improves.

Why it matters

The shift from public infrastructure (a reliable utility grid) to privatized resilience (household batteries) redistributes both risk and benefit along wealth lines. Wealthy neighborhoods become resilient; lower-income areas become more dependent on an aging utility system. This fragments infrastructure investment and public-health protection, turning power security into a consumer product rather than a public right.

Households are transforming from solar environmentalists into grid-anxiety consumers buying backup power for insurance and utility-cost protection.
