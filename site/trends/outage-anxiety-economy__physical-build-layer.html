Physical Build Layer: Specialty Contractors and Resilience Infrastructure

Specialty contractors executing resilience builds, data-center construction, and power/cooling/control system modernization. EMCOR reports $17.14B remaining performance obligations; Comfort Systems $14.06B backlog with 51% organic growth; scaled execution and skilled-labor constraints become the binding constraint.

Resilience infrastructure demand—from data centers to electrical modernization to HVAC and controls upgrades—now depends entirely on execution capacity, not on product availability or capital availability. Specialty contractors with scale, labor depth, and project management discipline capture disproportionate share; labor scarcity becomes the operating bottleneck.

The mechanism

Data-center and AI infrastructure construction demand overwhelmed pure project capacity during 2024-2026. EMCOR's remaining performance obligations (RPO, also called backlog) jumped to $17.14B in Q2 2026 from $13.25B at year-end 2025, a $3.89B increase in six months. Network and communications became the largest driver, with data-center connectivity and power infrastructure as the primary growth segments. This reflects not finished construction but committed backlog waiting for execution. Comfort Systems' backlog surged to $14.06B from $5.99B a year prior (Q2 2025), a 134% increase—the most dramatic backlog expansion in the contractor space. Q1 2026 organic revenue growth of 51% shows that demand vastly exceeds existing execution teams. The backlog-to-revenue ratio (Q2 2026 annualized run rate of ~$10B implies 1.4 years of work at current burn) represents work awarded but not yet completed due to labor constraints.

Labor scarcity became the binding constraint, not capital or materials, during 2024-2026. Comfort Systems explicitly states in Q1 and Q2 2026 guidance that the bottleneck is not whether demand exists, but whether enough qualified HVAC, electrical, and mechanical tradespeople exist to convert that demand into completed work. Management notes record backlog paired with 'unprecedented demand' and constant emphasis on 'growing teams' and 'workforce depth.' The company is turning away profitable work because it cannot execute it fast enough. FY2025 operating cash flow of $1.19B on $9.10B revenue shows cash generation despite tight labor markets—proof that labor constraints are the issue, not profitability or capital. EMCOR's guidance raises to $32.00-$33.25 diluted EPS in 2026 are explicitly linked to industry growth in cloud, AI, data storage, and data-center buildout, but management also emphasizes 'execution focus' and 'workforce availability,' confirming that labor is the constraint.

Specialty-contractor scale amplified execution advantage during the 2024-2026 demand surge. EMCOR's roughly 100 operating subsidiaries and Comfort Systems' distributed HVAC and electrical teams benefit from labor pools, acquisition targets, and project management discipline that smaller contractors lack. This creates a durable moat: the demand is available to anyone, but only scaled operators with deep labor markets and integrated management can capture it profitably. Eaton's Q2 2026 organic growth of 14% shows that electrical-distribution segment growth is strongest at scale. Powell Industries' Q3 FY2026 new orders hit a record $934M with backlog rising to $2.4B and a book-to-bill of 3.0x—meaning orders came in at three times the rate of revenue recognition, direct evidence of demand that vastly exceeds execution capacity. Trane Technologies' Q2 2026 organic bookings up 37% with backlog reaching record $12.1B shows cooling and HVAC systems are equally demand-constrained. Only large contractors with diverse labor sources can address this.

Why now

Resilience infrastructure demand accelerated sharply as an inflection point during 2024-2025. Through 2023, data-center capex was being discussed but not signed into large ESAs. Starting in Q3 2024, hyperscalers began signing binding Electric Service Agreements and committing to construction timelines. By Q1 2026, Alliant disclosed 3.4 GW of data-center load and Duke disclosed 7.6 GW. That 2024-2025 inflection created immediate execution demand: contractors needed to staff up and deliver large projects on fixed timelines. Simultaneously, grid modernization and decarbonization efforts accelerated: power utilities moved forward with distribution upgrades required to serve large loads. Water and wastewater infrastructure replacement remained mandated. Healthcare and institutional facility upgrades stayed strong. Commercial building complexity (cloud, controls, automation) continued to rise. The demand curve shifted up in 2024-2025 and has not normalized. Meanwhile, skilled-trade labor supply lagged. Trade schools saw declining enrollment through 2020-2023 (the years when young workers entered other fields). That supply lag became the binding constraint in 2024-2026. Contractors had unprecedented demand but could not find enough electricians, HVAC technicians, and mechanical crews to execute. The result is the unusual situation where contractors are rationing their own execution capacity and turning away profitable work.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Data-center operators and cloud providers funding construction and infrastructure contracts; facility owners and operators paying for electrical, mechanical, HVAC, and building-automation upgrades; municipalities funding water and wastewater infrastructure replacement; commercial and institutional facility operators absorbing higher labor costs through contractor pricing; end consumers indirectly funding through higher data-center services and facility-dependent product costs. Specialty contractors' pricing power means that EMCOR can raise margins (Q2 2026 guidance reflects higher margins than achieved in FY2025), Comfort Systems can select only the highest-margin projects (51% organic growth with selective project acceptance), and Eaton can raise electrical-distribution pricing (14% organic growth). These cost increases are passed downstream to facility operators and end consumers.
Who profits
EMCOR, Comfort Systems, and peer specialty contractors with scale and labor depth. Q2 2026 guidance from EMCOR to $32.00-$33.25 diluted EPS (vs. actual FY2025 $28.19) shows margin expansion. Comfort Systems' 51% organic growth with operating cash flow of $1.19B on $9.10B revenue (13% FCF conversion) shows that even amid tight labor markets, margins are expanding. These companies can maintain high gross margins because demand vastly exceeds supply, allowing them to raise pricing and selectivity on projects. Smaller regional contractors lose share to aggregators (the scale moat is real). Equipment manufacturers benefit from upgraded HVAC, electrical, and building-control sales. Labor itself remains scarce, but organized contractors benefit most because they can negotiate crew availability and shift talent between projects.

The tension

The specialty-contractor model breaks if: (1) labor-training pipeline accelerates and supply normalizes (unlikely through 2027, but possible by 2028), (2) economic slowdown reduces data-center and infrastructure investment (risk if AI narrative cools), (3) larger construction or engineering firms build competing capacity (structural risk as margins stay high), (4) automation of mechanical and electrical tasks progresses faster than expected (low probability through 2026), or (5) execution quality deteriorates under pressure to burn backlog (real risk if contractors scale too aggressively). Both EMCOR and Comfort Systems risk project-quality issues or cost overruns if they scale too aggressively. Additionally, the model assumes data-center demand stays strong through 2030; if hyperscalers slow ESA signings or delay construction timelines, contractor backlogs could clear faster than backlog conversion rates support, compressing future revenue and EPS.

The surprising part

Comfort Systems' $14.06B backlog growing 134% year-over-year while organic revenue growth was 51% in Q1 2026 represents work that the company has already been awarded but cannot execute. This is not pipeline optimism or speculative demand; it is committed customer orders waiting for crew availability. That backlog-to-revenue ratio implies 1.4+ years of work at current burn rates—an extraordinary constraint situation for a contractor. The surprise is not that demand is strong; it is that demand is strong enough that the company is in the unusual position of rationing its execution capacity and explicitly turning away profitable work due to labor constraints. Powell Industries' 3.0x book-to-bill is equally surprising: orders came in at three times the rate of revenue recognition in Q3 FY2026, creating instantaneous backlog inflation. That signals an industry in supply-shock, where customer demand is accelerating but contractor capacity is fixed.

Why it matters

When infrastructure execution becomes the bottleneck rather than capital or materials, it signals that the U.S. economy faces a skilled-labor deficit that cannot be easily solved through automation or outsourcing. Data-center and power-system modernization are essential infrastructure, but they depend on electricians, HVAC technicians, and mechanical crews that remain scarce. If labor supply continues lagging demand through 2026-2028, infrastructure projects will slow, costs will rise, and smaller communities may fall behind in resilience investments. This creates a two-tier system: wealthy regions and large-scale data-center corridors get rapid infrastructure modernization. Other regions see delayed upgrades and higher costs. The bottleneck also means that specialty contractors gain permanent pricing power; infrastructure users pay higher costs indefinitely because labor is permanently scarce.

Resilience infrastructure demand now exceeds execution capacity, giving scaled specialty contractors pricing power and the unusual burden of turning away profitable work due to labor constraints.
