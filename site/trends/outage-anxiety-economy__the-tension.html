The Fragility Inside the Resilience Economy

Generac Q2 2026: tariff refunds ($71M pretax) inflated margins to 24.8%, while underlying margin sits ~17-18%. Sunrun cut 2026 guidance ($4.6B-$4.9B ASV down from $4.8B-$5.2B) despite 74% storage attachment—financing, not demand, is the constraint. Comfort Systems' backlog hit $14.06B (doubling from $8.12B a year prior) but organic growth capped at 51%—labor is the binding execution limit. The resilience premium depends on tariffs staying put, capital markets staying open, and skilled labor staying available. All three are cracking simultaneously.

The outage-anxiety economy rests on three temporary props that are now showing strain at once. First, tariff protection: Generac's $71M Q2 2026 pretax tariff refund boosted gross margin to 28% and adjusted EBITDA margin to 24.8%, but management signaled this is non-recurring. Strip it out and underlying margin reverts to approximately 17-18%—little better than industrial equipment commodity levels. Tariffs are policy, not economics; when Congress lets relief expire or renegotiates rates, that $71M evaporates and Generac's cost structure becomes visible. Second, capital-market access: Sunrun lowered FY2026 ASV guidance to $4.6B-$4.9B from $4.8B-$5.2B despite achieving a record 74% storage attachment in Q2 2026. The paradox reveals the real bottleneck: residential solar-and-battery infrastructure requires continuous upfront capital (tax equity, non-recourse project debt, safe-harbor timing) that only exists when institutional investors are risk-on. If capital-market conditions tighten, tax-equity sponsors retreat, or non-recourse debt becomes harder to place, Sunrun cannot convert demand into booked revenue. Customers want batteries; the financing window determines whether Sunrun can afford to install them. Third, labor scarcity: Comfort Systems' Q2 2026 backlog jumped to $14.06B from $8.12B a year earlier, yet the company grew revenue only 51% year-over-year in Q1 2026 despite burning through massive backlog. The gap between backlog and growth is pure labor constraints. Management explicitly warns about 'commitments that exceed labor resources.' EMCOR faces the same reality: Q2 2026 remaining performance obligations hit $17.14B (up from $11.91B a year prior), yet both companies are carefully managing burn rates to protect quality and not attempt to accelerate execution beyond workforce capacity. When any one of these three constraints breaks—tariff expiration, capital crunch, or wage inflation destroying contractor unit economics—the entire system can re-compress. When all three crack simultaneously, the resilience premium disappears faster than the anxiety that created it.

The mechanism

The resilience economy built momentum on three assumptions that all depended on stability. Tariff relief made Generac's residential and C&I equipment more price-competitive versus imports; it was meant to protect domestic manufacturing. But tariffs are not permanent policy. They expire, they get renegotiated, they swing with administration changes. Generac's Q2 2026 results prove this fragility cleanly. Without the $71M pretax tariff refund, Q2 EBITDA would have been approximately $220M (18.8% margin)—essentially flat with prior-year run-rates and showing no operating leverage. Management explicitly told investors this benefit is one-time and will not repeat. That signals they know the tariff shield is temporary. The moment Congress lets relief expire or carve back rates, Generac's gross margin compresses and its guidance needs reset. The company now depends on data-center sales (with a $1.6B backlog already visible) to offset the eventual tariff cliff, but those sales are lumpy and dependent on hyperscale qualification cycles.

Capital-market access is the second pillar showing strain. Sunrun's case is stark: Q2 2026 storage attachment hit 74%, the highest on record, while the company cut its full-year guidance by 200-300 basis points in midpoint. Revenue guidance fell from $4.8B-$5.2B to $4.6B-$4.9B, and cash-generation guidance fell from $250M-$450M to $200M-$375M. This cannot be demand destruction—record storage attachment proves demand strength. It is financing and channel execution. Sunrun's model depends on moving customer contracts into tax-equity partnerships and securitized non-recourse debt pools. When institutional investors pull back (whether due to rising rates, credit concerns, or a shift in risk appetite), Sunrun cannot move inventory into capital structures fast enough. The affiliate-channel pressure and delayed direct-sales ramp that management cited both point to the same constraint: unable to fund customer acquisitions because the capital is not there, regardless of whether customers want to buy. If this tightens further—if tax-equity sponsors slow deployment, if non-recourse debt becomes expensive or unavailable, if safe-harbor timing windows close—Sunrun will face a ceiling that has nothing to do with customer demand and everything to do with financial plumbing.

Labor scarcity is the third and perhaps most intractable constraint. Comfort Systems and EMCOR sit at the front lines of this reality. Comfort Systems' backlog grew from $5.99B (Q2 2025) to $8.12B (Q2 2026) to $14.06B (Q2 2026)—a 135% increase in one year—yet organic revenue growth capped at 51% in Q1 2026. The company cannot even burn backlog as fast as it grows. This is not a demand problem; it is a labor-availability problem. The company cannot hire, train, and deploy electricians, HVAC technicians, and project managers fast enough to execute everything it has signed. EMCOR's Q2 2026 remaining performance obligations hit $17.14B, up 44% from $11.91B a year prior. Both companies are raising guidance (EMCOR raised FY2026 EPS guidance again to $32.00-$33.25 in Q2), which means they are executing, but they are executing into capacity-constrained project mix and selecting for higher-margin work rather than trying to maximize volume. This is the signature of labor scarcity: when execution capacity is the bottleneck, companies choose margin over growth. Neither contractor is running HVAC schools, apprenticeship pipelines, or mass hiring drives that would permanently solve the skilled-labor shortage. Why? Because labor wages have already risen substantially, and further wage acceleration would destroy the unit economics of field work. Eaton's $1.5B capacity-investment announcement signals the same reality: the company is aware that demand now exceeds manufacturing capacity and supply-chain capability, so it has to build its way out.

Why now

The timing is acute because all three constraints are simultaneously showing stress, and the feedback loops are negative. Tariff relief measures were temporary emergency policy enacted in 2023-2024; they are already under review for renegotiation or expiration. Congress has already debated extending or scaling back tariff policy multiple times in 2026, and uncertainty itself suppresses Generac's ability to plan capex and communicate pricing to customers. Sunrun's guidance cut happened in Q2 2026 (late June), meaning capital-market pressure is actively visible right now. Tax-equity sponsors have pulled back on deployment; project-finance timing has shifted. That is not forward guidance based on models—that is current-quarter fact. Comfort Systems and EMCOR's backlogs show the labor constraint is active now. Both companies are at the moment of truth: they can book more work (and they are), but they cannot execute more work without hiring at wages that are becoming uneconomical. If residential HVAC or electrical installation cannot be staffed at margins that support growth, then the backlog becomes a liability, not an asset. Finally, the cyclical risk is elevated. If U.S. economic growth slows in late 2026 or 2027, residential investment in solar, batteries, and backup power becomes discretionary and gets cut. Commercial and data-center work holds up longer, but even that depends on sustained AI capex and hyperscaler capex cycles. If hyperscaler spending slows (as it often does after initial build-out phases), Generac's data-center backlog becomes less convertible, and Eaton, EMCOR, and Comfort Systems' project pipelines thin. The resilience economy works only when underlying anxiety stays high, capital markets stay risk-on, policy stays friendly, and labor stays available at current wage levels. Right now, four factors are showing pressure, and the market has not fully priced that risk.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
End consumers and commercial customers pay for the fragility. Homeowners financing Sunrun systems or Generac backup generators are taking on debt and contracts precisely when the underlying economics are becoming unstable. If Sunrun cannot secure capital to fund leases, customers may face service interruptions, delayed installation, or eventual loss of backup-power availability if the company's financial position deteriorates. If Generac faces tariff cliffs and margin compression, the company will cut R&D, manufacturing investment, or quality to protect profitability, and customers will absorb that through reduced reliability or slower innovation. Commercial and industrial customers also pay: hospitals and data-center operators relying on EMCOR and Comfort Systems for on-time project delivery will face stretched timelines if labor becomes scarce. When installation takes 12 months instead of 6, that delays revenue recognition for the customer's own services or products. The public sector pays indirectly: if resilience infrastructure (backup power, distributed storage, grid-edge batteries) does not get built because of labor and financing constraints, grid reliability lags and communities remain vulnerable to extended outages. Eaton's $1.5B capacity investment is a signal that equipment manufacturers are already rationing supply—they are choosing which customers and segments to serve rather than attempting to meet all demand.
Who profits
Near-term profits flow to equipment manufacturers (Generac, Eaton) and capital-structure intermediaries (tax-equity sponsors, debt holders, Sunrun investors in a healthy capital market). Contractors (Comfort Systems, EMCOR) profit through volume and margin when they can execute, but they are hitting capacity ceilings that limit upside. The moment constraints break, profit streams compress. If tariffs expire, Generac's gross margin falls 200-400 basis points and the company must prove it can cut costs or raise prices (hard against import competition). If capital markets tighten, Sunrun's tax-equity deployment slows, its cash generation deteriorates, and its ability to fund customer acquisitions evaporates—the business model enters a death spiral without working capital. If labor costs spike beyond what field-service economics can sustain, Comfort Systems and EMCOR face a choice: grow more slowly (disappointing the market and eating fixed costs), accept lower margins (disappointing investors), or sacrifice project quality and scope (damaging reputation and eroding future demand). The real structural winner would be a company with pricing power, vertical integration, and labor control—Eaton is closest to this profile, given its manufacturing depth and ability to raise prices in select segments. But even Eaton is announcing $1.5B of capacity investment, meaning it is not immune to capacity constraint risk. Long-term, if the resilience economy compresses, capital no longer flows to these businesses, valuations reset lower, and the physical-infrastructure build-out cycle stalls.

The tension

The fundamental tension is that the resilience economy depends on three fragile, interdependent props: tariff policy (not permanent, subject to renegotiation), capital-market risk appetite (cyclical, can tighten quickly), and labor availability at current cost levels (increasingly scarce, wage pressure rising). Any one breaking creates adjustment; all three breaking simultaneously creates a system reset. The paradoxes are sharp. Sunrun can sell a record number of batteries (74% attachment) while cutting full-year guidance, because financing beats demand. Comfort Systems can double its backlog in one year while capping organic revenue growth at 51%, because labor beats demand. Generac can report record Q2 EBITDA margins (24.8%) while management signals they are one-time distortions, because tariff policy beats operations. The second tension is psychological. The entire resilience economy is built on consumer and business anxiety about outages, climate volatility, and grid reliability. That anxiety drives willingness to pay for insurance in the form of batteries, backup generators, and solar systems. But anxiety is not forever. If outages decrease (due to grid investment, better weather, or economic slowdown reducing electricity demand), that psychological driver evaporates. Consumers who bought Generac backup power will see it unused for years; businesses that invested in resilience will question whether it was necessary. Demand can reverse faster than supply adjusts. The final tension is that the supply chain itself is being stress-tested. Generac is expanding data-center capacity (with a $1.6B backlog already on the books), Eaton is committing $1.5B to capacity, Comfort Systems and EMCOR are both operating at backlog highs, yet none of them are trying to maximize delivery speed. They are rationing supply and choosing margin over volume, which means they believe the constraint is real and cannot be solved by hiring or capital investment at current cost levels. If companies with this level of installed scale and financial capacity cannot expand fast enough to meet demand, the constraint is structural, not cyclical.

The surprising part

The sharpest surprise is that Sunrun achieved a record 74% storage attachment rate in Q2 2026 while the company cut full-year revenue and cash-generation guidance. This is the direct inverse of the resilience-economy narrative. The story says rising anxiety and climate volatility drive unstoppable demand for batteries and backup power, and that installed-base growth creates pricing power and cash generation. The reality: demand is real and strong (record attachment), but the monetization model is broken. Customers want batteries, but Sunrun cannot afford to install them because the capital is not there and affiliate-channel partners are pulling back. This reveals that the resilience premium is purchased not by end-consumer demand but by capital-market appetite. If institutional investors retreat, the entire value chain freezes, regardless of how many homeowners want batteries on their roof. Similarly, Generac's tariff refund is the hidden story inside Q2 results. The company reported 24.8% EBITDA margins and 11% revenue growth, exactly the kind of operating leverage narrative that should support a resilience-infrastructure thesis. But the refund is a $71M one-time policy benefit that masks an underlying margin structure of ~17-18%. This is the inverse of operating leverage—it is policy leverage hiding operational flatness. Without tariff protection, Generac's Q2 would have shown mid-teens margins and modest pricing power, a much less exciting story. The market has priced in permanent high margins at Generac when the company is telling investors the margins are temporary. That gap will close sharply when tariff relief expires.

Why it matters

If the resilience economy fragments due to tariff expiration, capital-market tightening, or labor-cost spirals, communities lose the ability to harden their power infrastructure against grid failures. This is not merely consumer comfort; it is public health and safety. Hospitals depend on backup generation to maintain emergency services during outages. Water systems depend on backup power to maintain pumping and treatment. Communications networks depend on powered infrastructure to maintain emergency dispatch. If Generac cannot deploy backup power at scale and affordability due to tariff cliffs, if Comfort Systems and EMCOR cannot build out resilience systems because of labor scarcity, if Sunrun cannot finance distributed storage because capital retreated, then communities remain vulnerable to extended outages even when technology and capital exist. The labor dimension is particularly acute: if North American skilled-trades capacity is already exhausted (as Comfort Systems' backlog suggests), then entire regions may remain structurally vulnerable to outages for years because human execution is the bottleneck. The policy dimension also matters: if tariff policy becomes essential to the economics of resilience equipment, then tariff policy becomes de facto climate and grid-reliability policy, and policy uncertainty makes large-scale private investment impossible. Finally, there is an equity concern: households and businesses with capital access (ability to finance batteries, backup power, solar) will harden themselves against outages and grid stress. Those without capital access (renters, low-income households, small businesses) will have no resilience option and will bear the full brunt of grid failures. The resilience economy, under capital and labor constraint, becomes a story of widening inequality around outage risk.

The resilience economy survives only as long as tariff policy holds, capital remains available to finance distributed solar-and-storage at scale, and skilled labor does not become so expensive that field-service unit economics break—and all three supports are actively showing stress in Q2 2026.
