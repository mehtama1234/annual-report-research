Who Pays for Certainty: Reliability as a Bifurcated Product

Reliability has inverted from a utility promise to a product you buy. Generac's $1.6B data-center backlog and Sunrun's 74% battery attachment rate show hyperscalers and affluent homeowners paying for certainty, while the grid itself deteriorates for everyone else. The market is splitting between those who can afford to opt out and those who absorb managed outages.

Keeping the lights on is no longer a utility's uniform promise but a bifurcated product: certainty for those who can afford it, managed outages for everyone else. Whoever controls backup generation, batteries, and data-center resilience gets to price fear at will. Generac, Sunrun, and data-center operators are capturing this. Utilities like Alliant and NextEra are locking in regulated returns on reliability infrastructure. Contractors like EMCOR and Comfort Systems are booking record work. The anxiety around losing power is rising faster than the actual outage rate, and the market is monetizing the gap between fear and reality.

The mechanism

The trigger is not that the grid fails more often on average—it mostly doesn't—but that the consequence of failure grew steeper. A blackout in 2010 meant a spoiled fridge and lost evening. A blackout in 2026 means home office stops, electric car cannot charge, CPAP machine stops, telemedicine fails. The cost curve inverted even where outage frequency stayed flat, and people pay to flatten the consequence.

That individual fear scales into industrial architecture. For a hyperscaler, an outage is a design failure, so backup power moved from fallback to mandatory. Generac booked a $1.6B data-center backlog in 2026, securing supply agreements with global hyperscalers. That same grid stress fed the other end: Sunrun turned solar-plus-battery into a distributed grid asset, hitting 74% storage attachment and 4.647 GWh of networked capacity. Utilities like Alliant raced to contract 3.4 GW of data-center load, projecting a 60% jump in peak demand by 2031. The contractors who build the physical resilience—EMCOR with $17.14B backlog, Comfort Systems with $14.06B—booked records because data-center buildout is really a reliability infrastructure buildout in concrete and copper.

The repricing mechanism is simple: reliability used to be bundled into your utility bill as an implicit promise. Now it is unbundled and sold as a discrete premium—a generator, a battery system, a hyperscaler backup contract—and the premium accrues to the vendor, not the grid. Digital Realty's renewal rate jumped to 25.4% cash in Q2 2026 (from 6.1% in Q4 2025) in a single quarter because customers suddenly perceived power capacity as a competitive moat worth repricing for.

Why now

Three pressures converged. Extreme weather—wildfires, winter storms—kept knocking out supply and dramatizing every outage. AI-driven load growth is straining grids faster than they can be upgraded; utilities like Alliant face a 60% peak-load increase by 2031 entirely from data centers. Culturally, every lost hour is now framed as a productivity-and-safety emergency because so much runs on always-on power. The clearest signal: Generac's Q2 2026 bifurcation (commercial up 29%, residential down 2%) with a $1.6B data-center backlog. When industrial-side demand booms on reliability anxiety while consumer demand pulls back, reliability has been formally repriced—from a household commodity into a strategic industrial asset that separates the protected from the managed-outage tier.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Residential buyers pay steep upfront premiums for standby generators or solar-plus-battery systems—thousands of dollars to insure against fear. The household that cannot afford it pays differently: it stays fully grid-dependent and eats the outages the affluent neighbor rode out on batteries. Ordinary utility customers absorb the system-wide cost of a stressed grid without capturing the reliability premium flowing to generator vendors. Low-income neighborhoods get managed outages while affluent neighborhoods buy resilience.
Who profits
Backup-generation makers (Generac) and residential storage operators (Sunrun) capture the household premium. Utilities with data-center siting advantages (Alliant, NextEra) lock in decades of regulated returns on buildout. Contractors (EMCOR $17.14B backlog, Comfort Systems $14.06B) book record work. Data-center operators (Digital Realty) see power as competitive moat and repriced renewal rates from 6.1% to 25.4% in one quarter. The entire value chain extracts premium for certainty.

The tension

The distributed-resilience model runs on enormous materials and labor inputs and distributes cost brutally unevenly—solar-backed comfort for affluent homeowners, managed outages for low-income neighborhoods. There is also a margin illusion: Generac's $71M tariff refund flattered Q2 2026 EBITDA guidance. When that expires, the real cost structure faces hyperscaler pricing discipline that does not pay retail-fear prices. Grid stress makes residential backup urgent but also makes industrial backup mandatory, creating two markets with inverted pricing power.

The surprising part

The trend is not powered by outages getting worse—on average they are not. It is powered by the consequences of outages feeling catastrophic, so the market grows on anxiety, not failure statistics. Generac's $71M tariff refund boosted guidance even as residential demand fell 2%. The company is making real money on hyperscaler contracts while using residential fear as the retail-priced half of a business whose actual growth is industrial. Digital Realty's renewal rate jumped to 25.4% cash (from 6.1%) in a single quarter—not because outages happened, but because customers suddenly perceived power scarcity as a competitive risk worth paying premium rates to escape.

Why it matters

A society where power reliability is purchased rather than provided equally is a society stratifying by resilience. The rich get solar and batteries, everyone else gets managed outages. The map of who still has power after the next storm will look like the map of who has money. Communities lose institutional resilience when they depend on private backup systems instead of public infrastructure. The grid itself becomes underinvested because the wealthy opt out, creating a vicious cycle where grid reliability deteriorates, fear rises, and those who can afford it exit further.

Reliability has inverted from a utility promise into a bifurcated product: those who can afford certainty buy it, everyone else gets managed outages, and the wealth gap becomes a resilience gap.
