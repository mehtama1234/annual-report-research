Where the Reliability Premium Lands: Value Capture Across the Stack

The reliability premium is captured at multiple layers simultaneously: OEMs through contract pricing, contractors through scarcity and backlogs, utilities through rate-base expansion, and residential operators through lifetime value subscriptions.

Backup-generation OEMs like Generac and Cummins are capturing premium pricing through hyperscale contracts; specialty contractors like EMCOR are capturing execution scarcity through record backlogs; utilities like Alliant are capturing power-demand premiums through data-center rate structures; and distributed-power operators like Sunrun are capturing recurring revenue through storage subscriptions. The pricing power is not concentrated in one layer; it is systemic across the stack.

The mechanism

Generac and Cummins are capturing the premium at the OEM level. Generac signed a global supply agreement with a hyperscale operator, expanded data-center backlog to $1.6B, and grew C&I sales 29% in Q2 2026. Cummins saw Power Systems revenue rise 19% in Q2 2026 driven by data-center power demand and signed a natural-gas generator-set agreement with Circe Energy for an HPC data center in Texas. Both companies are also raising full-year guidance on the strength of this segment. The pricing power here is real: when a data center operator must guarantee nine-nines uptime, it cannot shop on price alone, and backup-generation OEMs know it.

EMCOR and specialty contractors capture the execution premium. EMCOR reported record remaining performance obligations of $17.14B in Q2 2026, up from $13.25B at year-end 2025. Quarterly revenue hit a record $5.15B. Data-center construction is the biggest contributor to this backlog growth, but it spans network, communications, water, wastewater, institutional, and healthcare. The contractor premium comes from capacity scarcity (there are only so many firms that can execute large-scale electrical and mechanical data-center builds) and project complexity (modern data centers require sophisticated power delivery, cooling, controls, and redundancy systems). When EMCOR has a $17B backlog, it has pricing power.

Alliant Energy captures the utility premium through rate structures. The company expects load to grow 60% by 2031 based on 3 GW of contracted data-center demand as of Q1 2026. Management is using individual-customer rates, advance ratemaking, and Iowa regulatory structures to convert that load growth into earnings expansion without traditional price regulation constraints. This is different from commodity utilities; data-center load is negotiated bilaterally, not subject to blanket rate caps. For Alliant, that means the reliability premium translates directly to earnings accretion.

Sunrun captures the residential reliability premium through subscriptions. The company reported a storage-attachment rate of 74% in Q2 2026, with an installed base of 1.206M customers and $3.677B of contracted net earning assets. Each household paying for battery-backed solar is paying for resilience insurance through a recurring monthly subscription. Sunrun is monetizing household-level outage anxiety through long-term contracts that give the company visibility and recurring revenue multiple years out. The premium here is psychological (peace of mind) and economic (backup power when the grid fails).

Why now

The reliability premium is systemic because grid stress, extreme weather, and AI-driven power demand are all converging simultaneously. Generac's Q2 2026 commentary explicitly tied data-center demand acceleration to AI adoption. Cummins noted data-center power demand was stronger than expected. EMCOR's backlog is driven by network, computing, and data-center infrastructure buildout. Alliant has 3 GW of contracted data-center load. Sunrun's storage attachment is at 74%, up from 71% a year earlier. Every company in the stack is saying the same thing: reliability is no longer a feature request; it is a core requirement. That demand is still ramping — it has not yet peaked — which is why pricing power remains high across the entire stack.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
End consumers and hyperscalers ultimately foot the bill. Data-center operators pay premium prices for backup generation, specialty-contractor services, and grid-edge storage because the cost of downtime is higher than the cost of redundancy. Those costs get passed through to cloud customers, AI service users, and enterprise IT budgets. Households pay for Sunrun storage subscriptions and higher utility rates from Alliant. Businesses pay more for EMCOR's capacity-constrained contractors and Generac's premium-priced backup systems. The distribution of that burden is uneven: large tech companies can absorb cost inflation better than small businesses; wealthy homeowners can afford Sunrun storage; regulated utilities can pass costs to ratepayers.
Who profits
The entire stack profits, which is unusual. Generac captures OEM premiums; EMCOR captures contractor scarcity premiums; Alliant captures utility-regulation premiums; Sunrun captures subscription premiums. This is not a zero-sum game because the underlying demand (grid reliability, outage-prevention infrastructure, continuous uptime) is genuinely increasing. The winners are those with capacity constraints (Generac and Cummins have full-order books; EMCOR has record backlogs), those with execution scarcity (specialty contractors can't be easily replicated), and those with regulatory moats (Alliant's Iowa regulatory structure protects its ability to raise rates for data-center load). The loser is anyone betting that grid reliability improves so much that resilience infrastructure demand softens.

The tension

The fragility sits in several places. First, if grid investment and renewable capacity deployment accelerate faster than expected, grid reliability could improve enough to reduce the perceived need for backup systems. Second, if Generac and Cummins' data-center backlogs cannot be fulfilled because of supply-chain or manufacturing constraints, the premium pricing will evaporate when customers face delays. Third, if regulatory pushback emerges around data-center power consumption (water usage, carbon intensity, local grid impacts), policy could force constraints that reduce data-center buildout and dampen the demand that justifies the entire stack. Fourth, if AI demand slows or model efficiency improvements reduce power intensity, the data-center build cycle could compress faster than infrastructure operators can redeploy capital.

The surprising part

The non-obvious pattern is that the pricing power is most durable for those furthest from the direct hyperscaler relationship. Generac and Cummins have hyperscale contracts and can negotiate pricing, but they are still commoditized relative to their customer's scale. EMCOR captures more durable scarcity premiums because there are only so many contractors who can execute at that scale and complexity. Alliant captures the most durable premium because it has regulatory protection and long-term rate structures that lock in data-center economics regardless of competitive pressure. Sunrun captures recurring revenue that stays stable even if one-time install demand fluctuates. The lesson: in a reliability-premium economy, the winner is whoever has the deepest scarcity, the longest contracts, and the hardest regulatory moat.

Why it matters

This is a regressive distribution of resilience. Wealthy households and large companies can afford premium backup power, subscription storage, and location siting that secures priority access to grid capacity. Small businesses and lower-income households cannot. If reliability becomes something you buy rather than something the grid provides universally, inequality in outage risk and electrical continuity will accelerate. The societal layer is also the fragility: if public perception shifts toward viewing data-center power consumption as the cause of broader grid stress (rather than just a demand pressure), political pressure could mount to constrain that consumption through policy, which would undermine the entire infrastructure stack that has been built to serve it.

The reliability premium flows across the entire stack from OEM to contractor to utility to subscriber, creating pricing power at multiple layers as long as grid stress and hyperscale demand growth persist.
