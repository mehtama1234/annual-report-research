Four Tollbooths on One Wire: Who Captures the Reliability Premium

Backup-power OEMs, specialty contractors, regulated utilities, and residential storage operators are all pricing the same fear at once - and the pricing power gets more durable the further you sit from the hyperscaler negotiating table.

The premium for guaranteed electricity is not captured in one place; it is captured four times over on the same demand wave. OEMs like Generac and Cummins price it through hyperscale supply contracts, specialty contractors like EMCOR price it through record backlog and skilled-labor scarcity, regulated utilities like Alliant price it through data-center-specific rate structures, and residential operators like Sunrun price it through storage subscriptions. The bet is that the layers with the hardest moats - a utility's regulatory framework, a subscriber's multi-year contract - out-earn the OEMs whose hyperscale pricing is always up for renegotiation.

The mechanism

OEM layer (Generac, Cummins): Generac signed a global supply agreement with a leading hyperscale operator, secured a second hyperscale agreement on 2026-06-24, and expanded its data-center backlog to about $1.6B. Its C&I external net sales rose 29% to $556M in Q2 2026 while residential fell 2%, and adjusted EBITDA reached 24.8% of net sales. Cummins posted record Q2 2026 revenue of $9.5B (up 9%) with Power Systems up 19% on data-center power demand across the US, China, and Asia Pacific, and signed a natural-gas generator-set and microgrid agreement with Circe Energy for an HPC data center in Texas. But this layer's margin optics need care: about $71M of pretax tariff refunds materially boosted Generac's Q2 2026 gross margin and full-year guidance, and higher incentive comp weighed on Cummins' EBITDA percentage. OEM pricing is real but negotiable - a hyperscaler with scale can always re-open the terms.

Contractor layer (EMCOR): EMCOR reported record remaining performance obligations of $17.14B in Q2 2026, up from $13.25B at year-end 2025 and $11.91B a year earlier, on record quarterly revenue of $5.15B. Management raised full-year 2026 diluted EPS guidance to $32.00-$33.25. The contractor premium is scarcity of skilled field labor and execution capability: there are only so many firms that can wire and cool a large data center, and EMCOR explicitly names network and communications work as its biggest RPO driver. Backlog this deep converts the reliability wave into multi-quarter earnings visibility rather than one-time equipment sales.

Utility layer (Alliant): total contracted large-customer demand reached 3.4 GW in Q1 2026 after a new 370 MW Iowa energy-service agreement, which management says drives roughly a 60% increase in projected demand by 2031 from 2025 levels, funded through a $13.4B 2026-2029 capital plan. Crucially, Alliant converts this through individual-customer rates and advance ratemaking - data-center load is priced bilaterally, not under blanket rate caps - and Iowa base-rate stability is expected to hold through the decade. This is the hardest moat in the stack: the earnings are locked into a regulated framework a competitor cannot simply undercut.

Residential layer (Sunrun): Sunrun reported a record 74% storage attachment rate in Q2 2026, about 1.206M customers, about 4.647 GWh of networked storage capacity, and about $3.677B of contracted net earning assets. Each battery-backed household is buying outage insurance as a multi-year subscription, giving Sunrun recurring, contracted revenue that persists even when quarter-to-quarter install demand wobbles - which it does, since Sunrun cut full-year 2026 aggregate subscriber value guidance to $4.6B-$4.9B citing softer affiliate channels and a delayed direct-sales ramp. The install cadence is volatile; the contracted back book is not.

Why now

The inflection is dated and specific. Generac secured its second hyperscale agreement on 2026-06-24 and pushed data-center backlog to ~$1.6B in Q2 2026; Cummins raised full-year 2026 revenue guidance to up 10-13% on the strength of standby-power orders and called data-center backup demand 'stronger than expected' as early as Q1 2026. EMCOR's RPO jumped from $11.91B (mid-2025) to $17.14B (Q2 2026), a ~$5.2B swing in a year with data-center construction as the largest contributor. Alliant's contracted large-load demand climbed from about 3 GW at year-end 2025 to 3.4 GW by Q1 2026. Sunrun's storage attachment rose from 71% (Q4 2025) to 74% (Q2 2026). Every layer independently shows the same acceleration inside the same three quarters - this is a synchronized demand ramp that has not yet peaked, which is why pricing power is elevated across all four layers at once rather than rotating between them.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Hyperscalers and their downstream customers foot the OEM and contractor bill, because the cost of downtime dwarfs the cost of redundancy, and that flows through to cloud and AI-service pricing. Alliant's ratepayers and its large-load customers pay the utility premium, softened by the regulatory promise to protect residential affordability and keep Iowa base rates stable. Households pay the residential premium directly through Sunrun subscriptions, which skew toward homeowners who can finance a battery. The burden is uneven by design: large tech buyers absorb cost inflation easily, regulated utilities pass costs through the rate base, and the resilience households buy is the resilience wealthier households can afford.
Who profits
All four layers profit simultaneously - unusual, and possible only because the underlying demand for guaranteed power is genuinely rising rather than being reallocated. Generac and Cummins capture full order books and hyperscale contracts. EMCOR captures a $17.14B backlog it can price against scarce labor. Alliant captures rate-based earnings on a $13.4B plan protected by regulation. Sunrun captures $3.677B of contracted net earning assets that keep paying regardless of any single quarter's installs. The relative winners are the layers with the deepest structural protection: EMCOR's labor scarcity, Alliant's regulatory framework, and Sunrun's contract back book are harder to erode than an OEM's negotiated hyperscale price.

The tension

Fragility clusters at the OEM end. Generac's and Cummins' hyperscale pricing is only as durable as the buyer's willingness not to renegotiate, dual-source, or in-source; a slip in AI capex or a jump in model efficiency could compress the data-center build cycle faster than OEMs can redeploy capacity. Generac's ~$71M tariff refund and Cummins' incentive-comp drag show how much of the recent margin optics are policy- and timing-driven rather than structural. Contractors face execution and skilled-labor risk if backlog outruns crews. Utilities face the opposite risk - stranded capital if contracted large-load demand fails to materialize on schedule (Alliant itself flags that large-customer load must still show up as forecast). And Sunrun's cut to 2026 guidance is the reminder that the residential channel is the most demand-sensitive of the four even as its back book is the most stable.

The surprising part

The pricing power is most durable for the players furthest from the hyperscaler, not closest to it. Intuition says the OEM with the direct hyperscale contract sits in the best seat - but Generac's and Cummins' prices are negotiable against buyers many times their size, and their headline margins are partly propped by a tariff refund and comp timing. The seat that actually holds is Alliant's, whose data-center earnings are locked into individual-customer rates and Iowa base-rate stability that no competitor can undercut, followed by Sunrun's contracted back book and EMCOR's labor-scarcity backlog. Durability of the reliability premium rises with distance from the negotiating table: regulatory moat and long recurring contracts outlast negotiable OEM pricing.

Why it matters

This is a regressive distribution of resilience. When continuous power stops being something the grid delivers universally and becomes something you buy - a Sunrun battery, a Generac standby unit, priority interconnection for a data center - the households and firms that can pay lock in reliability while everyone else absorbs more outage risk. Alliant's explicit affordability framing (protecting residential rates while onboarding gigawatts of data-center load) is the political pressure valve, and it is load-bearing: if the public comes to see AI data-center power draw as the cause of their own grid stress rather than a parallel demand, the regulatory bargain that lets utilities capture the premium could invert into constraints on the very buildout the whole stack is built to serve.

The same fear of the lights going out is being tolled four times - OEM, contractor, utility, subscriber - and the tollbooths with regulatory walls and multi-year contracts will still be collecting long after the hyperscalers have renegotiated the OEMs' price.
