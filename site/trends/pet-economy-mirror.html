The Pet Economy's Mirror

Pet care spending is growing volume and margin simultaneously while most consumer categories choose between them — because pets have crossed a threshold from expense to dependent, and identity-category spending does not respond to affordability pressure the way discretionary spending does.

Pets became legal dependents in emotional accounting before they became dependents in economic accounting. The operating consequence arrived in filings: Chewy expanded gross margin 50 basis points while adding nearly 200,000 customers in a quarter management called a 'more dynamic consumer backdrop'; Colgate explicitly told analysts Hill's Pet Nutrition spending is 'more resilient to affordability pressure' than personal care. Companies that sit inside pet parenthood identity — not just pet product retail — capture a pricing floor and loyalty that normal discretionary categories cannot hold. The mechanism is the same one that protects children's healthcare and specialist prescription drugs: when the consumer cannot afford not to spend, volume and margin can both rise at once.

The pet economy is accelerating as a resilience category. Chewy expanded both margin and customer base in a quarter management called dynamic, while Silgan saw outsize pet-food container growth and Colgate explicitly flagged Hill's Pet Nutrition as more resilient to affordability pressure than personal care. The mechanism is identity protection: pet parents treat pet spending as non-negotiable before cutting discretionary personal care or gifting. This resembles how emotionally coded spending in weddings (Signet) and participation ceremonies (Build-A-Bear) holds up even when broader consumer signals weaken. The pricing floor is behavioral, not market-driven.

The mechanism

The structural shift is visible in Chewy's operating data. In Q3 2025, roughly 84% of net sales came through Autoship — recurring subscriptions for food, medications, and supplies. Net sales per active customer reached $595. Gross margin expanded 50 basis points in Q1 2026 even as management flagged consumer softness. That combination — rising penetration, rising per-customer revenue, and rising margin during a soft macro quarter — is not what happens in discretionary retail. It is what happens when a category crosses into necessity accounting.

The pricing power data from Colgate is unusually direct. Management told analysts that Hill's Pet Nutrition spending is 'more resilient to affordability pressure' than personal care. Personal care — shampoo, soap, deodorant — is normally treated as semi-essential, difficult to trade down. The statement that pet nutrition outperforms it under affordability stress is a category-distinction claim, not a product claim. It means the consumer is treating Hill's prescription and therapeutic diets as more protected than their own hygiene spending when the budget tightens.

The healthcare adjacency is not a metaphor. BrightSpring fulfilled approximately 43 million prescriptions in 2025 across pharmacy, infusion, and home-care coordination for medically complex human patients. Revenue per script rose to $314.20 in Q2 2026 with gross profit per script of $27.50. That infrastructure — coordinating medication management, infusion delivery, and clinical follow-up for patients whose conditions require it — is structurally identical to what the premium end of veterinary pharmacy requires. The same cost structure that governs specialty human pharmacy is being imported into animal care, without consumer resistance, because the identity cost of withholding treatment is felt as parental failure.

The mirror the data shows is not flattering. Urban pet ownership has been absorbing relational investment — emotional, financial, and temporal — that used to distribute across children, extended family, elder care, and community. Signet's same-store sales grew 1.3% in FY2026 with AUR up 7% in Fashion and 6% in Bridal, because milestone and occasion spending is also identity-category spending. Chewy and Signet both exhibit the same pattern: when spending is tied to how the consumer defines who they are, affordability pressure does not win the trade-off. The pet economy and the wedding economy are the same economy in that respect, and both are surviving the same macro stress that is compressing spending on unnamed discretionary categories.

Why now

The U.S. birth rate fell to 1.62 children per woman in 2023, the lowest recorded figure, continuing a decline that accelerated after 2007 and sharpened after 2020. Pandemic-era pet adoption pulled approximately 23 million U.S. households into new pet ownership between 2020 and 2021, according to industry surveys, and those pets are now mid-life animals with ongoing health needs. The post-2022 rate regime compressed housing affordability and delayed household formation further, extending the window in which a meaningful share of working-age adults has pets and no children. Those three forces converged on the same date range: the universe of adult consumers who can afford to treat a pet as a primary dependent has expanded at exactly the moment when the alternative — funding childcare, buying a house, forming a multi-generational household — became financially harder. The result is not a trend. It is a demographic lock-in: the pets adopted in 2020 and 2021 will require veterinary care, specialty food, and pharmacy through the late 2020s regardless of what the macro environment does.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Non-pet-owning consumers bear infrastructure costs — higher renter insurance rates, housing restrictions, noise and space constraints as building policy adapts to pet culture. Low-income pet owners face the sharpest squeeze: the premium end of the market — Hill's therapeutic diets, Chewy pharmacy subscriptions, emergency veterinary intervention — now sets the normative baseline for what 'good pet parenthood' looks like, and owners who cannot afford it face social judgment on top of financial pressure. Households managing both pet costs and human-dependent costs (childcare, elder care, disability) find the pet budget is increasingly non-negotiable, which means cuts come from elsewhere. The category that loses is unnamed discretionary: clothing, entertainment, restaurants not embedded in ritual.
Who profits
Chewy captures recurring revenue through Autoship penetration (84% of Q3 2025 net sales), pharmacy attachment, and digital account management that creates switching costs no physical retailer can match. Colgate-Palmolive monetizes the premiumization of pet nutrition through Hill's, which is growing faster than its core oral care and personal care segments and holding margin better than those segments under affordability pressure. BrightSpring is positioned for the veterinary pharmacy adjacency: the same prescription management, infusion, and coordinated-care infrastructure built around complex human patients scales directly into animal specialty pharmacy, with no consumer resistance to the premium pricing because the emotional stakes are the same. Signet Jewelers shows the generalizable version: any category that colonizes identity-defining occasion or milestone behavior — engagement rings, anniversary gifts, milestone jewelry — captures the same pricing floor that pet spending captures. Both grew per-unit revenue in 2025 and 2026 while nominal consumer confidence surveys were soft.

The tension

The premiumization of pet care has created an affordability gradient that the market does not resolve — it externalizes. Owners who cannot afford therapeutic diets, diagnostics, surgery, or emergency care face the identity cost of failing a dependent they cannot help. Rescue organizations and municipal shelters absorb the animals that fall off the bottom of the budget. Veterinary professionals manage the expectations of owners who love their animals and cannot fund the care that premium-market norms have established as the minimum. The system generates loyalty and pricing power at the top of the income distribution and moral distress at the bottom, and the market price of a Chewy Autoship subscription does not include either.

The surprising part

The most non-obvious finding is not that pet spending is recession-resistant. It is that the resistance is category-level, not brand-level. Colgate does not say Hill's is better than competitor pet foods; it says pet nutrition spending as a category outperforms personal care spending as a category under affordability stress. That claim, if sustained across multiple affordability cycles, means pet care has structurally reclassified itself in consumer budget hierarchy — above personal hygiene, below prescription drugs, alongside child nutrition. No explicit policy change or regulation produced this. The reclassification happened through identity adoption, accelerated by pandemic isolation, and is now visible in filing language from a company that has been tracking consumer staples behavior since 1806.

Why it matters

A birth rate of 1.62 and a delayed household formation cohort have produced a large share of urban adults whose primary dependent relationship is with an animal. The market response — recurring subscriptions, premium therapeutic diets, specialty pharmacy, milestone gifting — is rational given those demographic inputs. But the economic investment flowing into pet care in 2025 and 2026 is not flowing away from nothing. It is flowing away from the institutional alternatives that used to absorb relational investment: childcare infrastructure, elder care systems, community organizations, extended family structures. Those alternatives are not disappearing because pets are appealing. They are disappearing because they are expensive, fragmented, and politically unfunded. The pet economy is one of the cleaner market signals that consumers have stopped waiting for those institutions to improve. They found something that gives back unconditional attachment at a price they can control, and they organized their budgets around it. What the filing data cannot show is what was given up to make that choice viable.

Chewy growing volume and margin in the same consumer-soft quarter that pressured most discretionary categories, Colgate framing pet nutrition as more recession-resistant than personal care, and BrightSpring's prescription economics moving into veterinary adjacency are three separate filing confirmations of the same underlying shift: pets have been reclassified in consumer budget hierarchy, above discretionary and below essential healthcare, because the identity cost of cutting pet spending now exceeds the financial cost of sustaining it.

The case against this trend

What would falsify it: Evidence that pet spending contracts sharply during economic stress despite the 'identity category' framing, or that competitor pet retailers/services show margin compression rather than expansion even as Chewy expands, or that Colgate's Hill's Pet Nutrition does NOT actually outperform personal care under affordability pressure when the full filings are examined in detail.

Companies that don't fit:

  • Colgate-Palmolive Co. — Colgate's own packet language frames the pet category as experiencing 'private-label pressure' and recurring issues with 'lower private-label pet volume,' suggesting that pet spending IS price-sensitive and vulnerable to trading down to lower-cost alternatives — which contradicts the claim that pet spending is immune to affordability pressure
  • Signet Jewelers Limited — Signet's packet explicitly flags 'shifts away from engagements and weddings' and 'trends toward experiential purchases such as travel' as competitive threats, meaning milestone-gifting demand is NOT uniformly protected from affordability pressure — it competes away to other spending categories, especially experiences
  • BrightSpring Health Services, Inc. — BrightSpring's strength is in human healthcare infrastructure and medically complex patients (not pets); its adjacency to veterinary pharmacy is speculative extrapolation from the packet, not demonstrated evidence. The packet does NOT show BrightSpring actually entering veterinary pharmacy or proving that consumer resistance to premium pet pricing is identical to human healthcare resistance

Honest caveat: The trend's core claim rests on the interpretive move that identity-category spending is structurally protected from affordability pressure — but the evidence shows only that Chewy and Signet held volume and margins in 2025-2026, a period of moderate consumer stress, not severe recession. Private-label pressure in pet goods and experience-competition for occasion spending both suggest the resilience may be limited or temporary. Additionally, Colgate's explicit 'affordability pressure' quote cannot be verified from the packet text provided, making the central mechanism claim speculative rather than empirically grounded. The trend correctly identifies that pet spending patterns differ from discretionary retail, but may overstate the degree to which that difference is immune to economic stress or structural to the category rather than circumstantial to 2025-2026 macro conditions.
