Nutrition as the New Pharmacy: Hill's and the Premium Pet Food Wedge

Hill's Pet Nutrition is outgrowing Colgate's oral and personal care segments because specialty pet food has been repositioned from commodity kibble to science-backed therapeutic nutrition.

Hill's Pet Nutrition is Colgate's highest-growth and highest-margin business because premium pet food has been reframed the way human vitamins were reframed: from commodity treat to health intervention. This repositioning created escape velocity from affordability pressure squeezing personal care. The market now treats pet food as healthcare rather than indulgence, which means consumers protect pet-nutrition spending even when cutting other budgets.

The mechanism

Hill's Pet Nutrition is Colgate-Palmolive's most strategically important asset because it successfully repositioned pet food from bulk commodity to therapeutic nutrition. This was not accidental. Colgate acquired Hill's in 1976 and has spent decades building the science narrative: prescription diets for kidney disease, obesity-management formulas, digestive health blends, and joint-support products. The company marketed these as veterinary-grade therapeutic products, not treats. That repositioning created a moat against private-label and value competitors. A consumer might shop for cheaper toothpaste (oral care), but they won't buy cheap kidney-diet food for a pet with renal disease if they believe the premium formulation actually helps. The premium positioning converted pet food from a discretionary category into a medical necessity equivalent.

The escape velocity from affordability pressure is visible in Colgate's portfolio. The company's oral care segment (44% of sales) and personal care segment (17% of sales) both face relentless private-label and value-brand pressure. Consumers trade down on toothpaste and shampoo when money tightens. Hill's Pet Nutrition doesn't face the same pressure because pet parents have already accepted that premium food prevents or manages health issues. Chewy's fiscal 2025 data reinforces this: net sales reached $12.60B with 21.3M active customers, and 84% of sales came through Autoship (subscription), meaning consumers pre-committed to recurring purchases at premium prices. The pet-nutrition category's defensibility comes not from brand loyalty alone, but from the belief that the premium formulation is medically necessary.

The supply chain beneath the premium tier is equally revealing. Silgan Holdings reported 7% growth in metal containers for pet-food markets in Q4 2025. That level of growth in a commodity packaging category signals massive throughput acceleration. Pet food now moves through premium metal cans at higher speeds than most packaged-food categories. The infrastructure spend (Silgan's capital, their mill integration and optimization) shows that the supply chain itself is being built around the expectation that premium pet nutrition is structural, not cyclical. When the supply chain starts scaling for a category, it's betting that the trend is durable.

Why now

Three factors converged to create the escape velocity. First: veterinary schools and nutritionists successfully promoted the idea that pet food directly affects pet health outcomes — obesity, joint health, kidney function, digestion. This transformed the category from commodity to medical. Second: pet ownership patterns shifted. Millennials and Gen X treat pets as family members with identical healthcare concerns as humans, driving spending on premium and specialty products. Third: e-commerce made it possible to bundle premium pet food with subscription convenience, lowering friction and turning impulse purchases into recurring charges.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Pet owners pay premium prices for specialty formulations. The irony is that while these formulations may provide health benefits, the pricing power largely comes from the belief in health benefit more than from clinical evidence superiority. Pet owners absorb the premium because they believe Walmart private-label would harm their pet.
Who profits
Hill's Pet Nutrition captures the premium through Colgate's financials. Chewy profits through higher average customer value and Autoship lock-in. Silgan profits through increased throughput in premium metal containers. Veterinarians profit by recommending premium formulations (though this is a minor part of most vet practice economics).

The tension

The tension is overdiagnosis and over-treatment. If many pet health issues are actually addressable through cheaper dietary adjustments, the premium market depends on convincing pet owners that their specific pet requires the specific premium product. The second tension is Walmart and private-label pressure: mentioned in Colgate's own 10-K, private-label pet food threats remain the main structural risk to Hill's margin expansion.

The surprising part

The most underappreciated fact is that pet nutrition's escape velocity from affordability pressure is not based primarily on clinical superiority over lower-cost alternatives. It's based on emotional positioning. Pet parents have been convinced that premium formulations prevent or manage disease, and that belief is worth a 30-50% price premium. This is similar to how human vitamin and supplement markets operate: premium positioning for a relatively undifferentiated commodity that consumers believe prevents health problems.

Why it matters

As pet ownership becomes more central to human identity and emotional fulfillment (especially as human birth rates decline), pet-health spending takes on greater psychological and cultural significance. This accelerates the medicalization of pet care and concentrates spending on premium formulations. For lower-income pet owners, this creates pressure to spend beyond their budget on premium pet food to avoid guilt about underinvesting in their pet's health.

Hill's Pet Nutrition escaped affordability pressure by repositioning pet food as therapeutic healthcare rather than commodity kibble — a positioning Colgate's traditional consumer segments cannot match.
