The Presence Gap: Affordability Exclusion and the Two-Tier Cultural Commons

When presence becomes a luxury that the majority cannot afford, cultural participation bifurcates: the priced-out majority consumes algorithmically personalized digital highlights alone, while the affluent experience shared moments live—fracturing the civic commons that broadcast television once provided for everyone.

The presence premium is not just economic; it is social and cultural. Live Nation's event-related deferred revenue reached record $6.6B in Q1 2026, signaling future live-experience revenues beyond reach of middle-income households. Nexstar's Q2 2026 distribution revenue hit $1.116B, showing the value trapped in linear broadcast—but that broadcast ecosystem is collapsing (EchoStar Pay-TV subscribers down to 6.391M in Q2 2026 from higher prior levels, Gannett digital-only subscriptions 1.5M, up from print subscriptions). The cultural commons—the shared real-time experience that binds communities—is being replaced by two tiers: premium presence (live events, in-person travel) for those who can pay, and algorithmic personalization (highlight reels, fragmented streaming) for everyone else. This fractures civic identity and social cohesion in ways that have no digital substitute.

The mechanism

Broadcast television created a cultural commons: a shared moment when most households watched the same content at the same time. The Super Bowl, network news, major award shows, and live events all created synchronized national attention. The experience was affordable (free or $50/month for cable) and inclusive; everyone saw the same thing. That commons is disintegrating because streaming atomizes viewing (everyone watches different content at different times), and live-event pricing increasingly segments audiences by income. Live Nation's 2025 revenue of $25.2B in concerts and live events shows massive aggregate demand, but the pricing structure is not flat. Stadium concerts cost $100-500+ per ticket for floor seats, $30-50 for nosebleeds. VIP experiences, parking, and concessions add another $50-200. A family of four attending a live concert therefore costs $400-800+, which is beyond reach for a household earning $50K-$70K. That family can watch highlights on YouTube (free), read reviews (free), and consume the cultural echo through social media (free). But they are not present. They did not experience the shared moment. That absence compounds across live sports, theatrical events, theme parks (Disney theme parks now charge $150-200/day), and premium travel experiences (RevPAR bifurcation shows luxury hotels rising while mid-market options contract).

The media landscape reinforces the split. Nexstar's business model still depends on distribution revenue ($2.924B in 2025), but that revenue is shrinking because MVPD households are collapsing. EchoStar's Pay-TV subscriber base fell to 6.391M in Q2 2026 after years of attrition. Gannett's print circulation continues to decline; the company now relies on digital-only subscriptions (1.5M) and marketing services to SMBs. What all three have in common is that they no longer offer a mass commons. Nexstar's local news still reaches some people, but it requires either a cable subscription (declining) or app-based consumption (fragmented). Gannett's newspapers reach 186M monthly unique visitors across digital properties, but that is not shared attention; that is algorithmically sorted, personalized, and atomized. EchoStar shows the limit case: as pay-TV subscriptions decline, the company becomes dependent on satellite capacity leasing and wireless partnerships rather than on a coherent consumer relationship. The cultural commons that broadcast once provided—where everyone watched Walter Cronkite, the Super Bowl, or the Moon landing together—is gone. What remains is either premium presence (live events for the affluent) or algorithmic personalization (fragmented digital streams for everyone else).

The permanence of this bifurcation is visible in the data and in management's strategic choices. Live Nation is not lowering ticket prices to expand the commons; it is using dynamic pricing to maximize yield and is growing revenue through premium experiences, VIP packages, and international expansion into affluent markets. Nexstar is not trying to rebuild broadcast commons; it is extending into streaming, sports rights, and hybrid distribution to capture whatever audience value remains. Gannett is explicitly building a subscription-and-services model that depends on direct relationships with affluent readers (digital subscriptions) and small businesses (LocaliQ marketing services), not on mass distribution. EchoStar's collapse shows what happens to the old broadcast infrastructure when no one wants it anymore. This is not a cyclical downturn that will recover. This is a structural shift where the operators have accepted that the mass commons is dead and are optimizing for segmented, premium, and personalized monetization instead. The social cost is borne by whoever is priced out.

Why now

The presence premium inflected after streaming hit profitability ceilings (around 2023-2024) and it became clear that digital video alone could not replicate the emotional and cultural value of live shared moments. When Netflix, Disney+, and others believed they could replace theatrical and live experiences with streaming content, they were wrong. People still want presence. But presence is now rationed by price rather than made freely available. At the same time, the broadcast commons that once provided free or cheap access to shared moments has become economically unviable. Linear TV audiences are shrinking (Nexstar's advertiser base is shrinking as political cycles fluctuate), print newspapers are unprofitable on their own (Gannett survives through subscriptions and services, not circulation), and satellite pay-TV is being abandoned (EchoStar shrinking). The operators have no choice but to monetize presence as a premium good because they no longer have a mass-market business model that works. 2024-2026 is the moment this shift became visible and undeniable.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Households in the middle income range ($50K-$150K) and those below pay the cost. They cannot afford $500+ tickets to live concerts, $150+/day theme park visits, premium hotel stays, or regular travel. They consume the cultural echo through free or low-cost digital platforms—YouTube clips, TikTok summaries, social-media highlights. But they miss the shared moment, the social cohesion, the feeling of participating in a cultural event in real time with others. Affluent households ($150K+) pay the premium and get the presence. Working-class households ($30K-$50K) are entirely priced out. Low-income households have essentially zero access to the paid presence tier and increasingly limited access to the broadcast commons (if they can afford cable/streaming, it is fragmented and personalized, not shared). The cost is ultimately borne as lost social cohesion and fractured civic identity.
Who profits
Live Nation captures ticket and sponsorship revenue ($25.2B in 2025), growing through international expansion and premium tiers. Venues capture higher rents from Live Nation events. Credit-card companies and fintech lenders profit from the financing of $400-800 concert trips. Luxury hotel chains and fine-dining operators profit from affluent travel. Digital-media companies (Netflix, YouTube, Disney+, social-media platforms) profit by monetizing the attention of the priced-out majority through algorithmic recommendations and targeted ads. Gannett and Nexstar profit by converting loyal readers and local audiences into high-lifetime-value subscribers. The media structures that once provided a free or cheap commons (broadcast TV, newspapers) collapse or shrink, but the companies that replace them (streaming platforms, subscription media, live-event operators) are more profitable per unit of attention captured. This is how the presence premium works: it maximizes revenue per consumer while minimizing the number of consumers who can participate.

The tension

The model depends on the priced-out majority accepting algorithmic personalization as an acceptable substitute for shared presence. If that acceptance breaks—if audiences start demanding access to live moments, if political backlash against cultural exclusion mounts, if FOMO (fear of missing out) creates psychological pressure that translates into unsustainable debt—the economics shift. A second tension: the broadcast commons, though economically unviable, still holds cultural legitimacy in ways that streaming personalization does not. Gannett's audience still wants local news; Nexstar's local stations still matter for community identity. If those operators go away, what replaces them? A third tension: the bifurcation creates political risk. If large portions of the population are systematically excluded from shared cultural moments, the legitimacy of the commons itself erodes. That can lead to regulatory pressure, antitrust scrutiny, or demands for public broadcasting that undermine the premium-monetization model. Finally, if the priced-out majority simply opts out (stops trying to consume the cultural echo, stops caring about live moments), the shared attention that grounds civic identity dissolves entirely.

The surprising part

The surprise is not that presence is becoming premium; it is how thoroughly and quickly the operators are accepting that the mass commons is dead and will not return. Five years ago, Live Nation still talked about 'accessibility' and 'democratizing access to live music.' By 2026, the company is openly optimizing for dynamic pricing, premium tiers, and international high-yield markets. Nexstar is not defending broadcast as a public trust; it is converting local stations into content-delivery nodes for hybrid streaming-linear experiences. Gannett is not trying to revive mass-circulation newspapers; it is building a subscription and SMB-services business that explicitly targets affluent and commercial consumers, not the mass market. EchoStar shows the ultimate logic: when the mass market is unprofitable, the company exits it (deconsolidating pay-TV from financial statements) and retains only strategic assets that can be monetized through partnerships with larger platforms. The operators have not mourned the loss of the commons; they have simply accepted it and moved on to a more profitable model. The surprise is the speed of that acceptance and the explicit acknowledgment that the old commons is gone.

Why it matters

This fracturing of the cultural commons is a fundamental shift in how civic identity forms. When everyone watched the same broadcast, read the same newspapers, or attended the same public events, there was a shared baseline of reality and a sense of common membership in a larger community. That is gone. What replaces it is algorithmic personalization—each person sees what the algorithm predicts they want to see—and economic segmentation where only the affluent experience live moments while everyone else consumes highlights. This erodes the sense of shared citizenship and common purpose. It also concentrates cultural power: whoever controls access to presence (Live Nation, luxury travel operators, premium events) shapes what is culturally valuable and who gets to participate. For society, it means that future civic memory, shared identity, and common cultural reference points become exclusive to the affluent. The priced-out majority will have fragmented, personalized media diets that do not intersect with each other or with the presence experiences of the affluent. That is a recipe for deepened social stratification and fractured national identity.

The live and shared-moment experiences that once bound communities together are being rationed as premium goods, fracturing the cultural commons and leaving the majority to consume algorithmically personalized echoes alone.
