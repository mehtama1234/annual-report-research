Presence Arbitrage: Where the Value Is Captured and Why It Compounds

The companies capturing maximum value from presence-driven spending share a structural feature: they own inventory that is fundamentally scarce and cannot be replicated by a competitor with capital alone—and that scarcity compounds over time.

Presence economics produce durable pricing power not through innovation but through structural scarcity. Live Nation's venue network, Disney's park destinations, Host Hotels' upper-upscale properties, and Fox's sports rights portfolio all share one trait: supply cannot be materially increased with capital. You cannot build another iconic arena in downtown Chicago. You cannot add more prime FIFA World Cup dates. Physical control points create permanent pricing leverage because the bottleneck is real and growing, not temporary and erasable.

The mechanism

Presence arbitrage works through a simple mechanism: companies that own fixed physical capacity can raise prices on that capacity as demand rises, and they cannot increase supply no matter how much capital they deploy. Live Nation owns venues through Ticketmaster relationships and direct ownership. That venue capacity is fixed on any given night. When Taylor Swift announces a tour, Live Nation can fill those venues at premium pricing. But the company cannot build a new 20,000-seat arena to add five more nights. The venue inventory is the constraint. Fox owns NFL broadcasting rights, which are time-limited, scarce, and cannot be manufactured. When the Super Bowl arrives, Fox can charge premium advertising rates because no competitor can offer that inventory. The scarcity is permanent and auction-driven every few years. Host Hotels owns upper-upscale hotel properties in premium locations. You cannot build a Ritz-Carlton on Fifth Avenue or a Park Hyatt on the Vegas Strip just because demand is rising. The land is owned, the operating licenses are held, and new supply faces geographic and regulatory barriers that capital alone cannot overcome.

Marriott demonstrates a variant of this scarcity economics through the loyalty-plus-scale model. The company ended Q2 2026 with 10,082 properties and nearly 1.8M rooms. That is not infinite, but it is large and durable. Marriott can offer worldwide status, co-branded credit card networks, and meetings coordination in a way that a new entrant cannot replicate without building the same scale over decades. The system benefits from network effects: an owner wants to join Marriott because Marriott has global brand reach and bookings. That global scale is self-reinforcing because every incremental owner added makes the network more valuable. Competitors cannot easily catch up because Marriott's 610,000-room development pipeline and co-brand relationships with JPMorgan Chase and American Express are structural moats, not temporary advantages. Fox's approach adds rights-based scarcity: the company paid for NFL, World Cup, and political-cycle broadcast rights. Those rights command premium advertising in ways that generic content does not. A competitor could invest billions building new digital platforms, but they cannot produce a Super Bowl halftime audience or political election coverage with the same cultural weight.

Host Hotels shows that scarcity economics can coexist with capital heaviness. The company owns expensive, fully depreciated properties in premium markets. New entrants would need to acquire land, build to luxury standards, and operate in competitive markets—all at massive capital cost. But Host already owns the assets, already has the licenses, and already operates the properties. The company raised comparable hotel Total RevPAR to $417.58 in Q2 2026, up 5.9 percent, because it controls supply and demand is rising. Management explicitly tied strength to affluent travel demand and out-of-room spending. Those are not categories where supply can quickly increase. The barrier is the combination of real estate ownership, operating expertise, market positioning, and brand relationships. That is why Host can keep raising prices without losing occupancy—the property owner has more defensible scarcity than anyone trying to build a competing hotel.

Why now

Three forces have converged to make presence scarcity explicit. First, streaming reached a profitability ceiling. Companies investing hundreds of billions in digital content (Netflix, Disney+, Amazon Prime) are discovering that attention fragmentation limits pricing power. Digital supply feels infinite—there is always more content, more shows, more platforms. That infinite supply pressure limits margins. By contrast, Live Nation has 49 million fans attending shows in one quarter. That is scarce. You cannot watch Taylor Swift from home and get the same experience. Second, the post-pandemic rebound showed that spending on presence—concerts, luxury travel, live sports—was not suppressed by streaming but accelerated by familiarity with digital. People wanted the real thing more, not less. Third, inflation and cost pressures have hit digital businesses (rising content costs, talent costs, infrastructure costs) while leaving physical scarcity businesses largely untouched. You cannot reduce the cost of running a 20,000-seat arena. You cannot manufacture new World Cup dates. But you can raise prices.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Four groups pay the presence premium. First, consumers and fans pay directly through ticket prices, hotel rates, and travel costs. Live Nation ticket fees, hotel RevPAR increases, and luxury travel spending all transfer to the customer. Second, advertisers pay through premium inventory pricing—Fox's 78 percent advertising revenue jump in Q4 FY2026 on World Cup coverage means marketers are paying more per impression for scarce live attention. Third, venue owners and hospitality franchisees pay through fee structures and royalties. Marriott property owners pay for brand access and customer reservation systems; Ticketmaster fees flow back to Live Nation. Fourth, in some cases, workers in hospitality and event services pay through wage suppression, limited benefits, and task-based contracting.
Who profits
Three groups capture value. First, the direct owners of scarce inventory: Live Nation's Ticketmaster operations, Fox's sports rights, Host Hotels' property portfolio, and Marriott's fee engine all extract rent on physical scarcity. Second, the financial investors backing these businesses—REITs holding hotel properties, private-equity sponsors in entertainment and hospitality, and public shareholders in the scaled platforms. Third, the smaller set of artists, creators, and talent who can fill the scarce inventory and negotiate from strength. Taylor Swift fills venues at premium pricing; major sports franchises command expensive broadcast rights. The distribution is not democratic—the value concentrates at the very top.

The tension

The breaking point comes when prices rise so far that demand actually declines, or when alternative experiences become viable. Luxury travel faces competition from second homes and private aviation. Concerts face security costs and accessibility pressure. Sports broadcasting faces cord-cutting and streaming alternatives. For Marriott and Host, geopolitical disruption (Middle East conflict disrupted Q2 international travel for both) shows that scarcity does not protect against demand shocks. The second tension is regulatory: antitrust scrutiny of Live Nation's Ticketmaster integration and Fox's sports rights accumulation are active. If these companies are forced to divest or separate, the scarcity premium could erode. The third tension is that some forms of presence are not actually scarce—hotel rooms exist everywhere, entertainment options are abundant. The companies that win are those controlling truly scarce inventory: pristine locations, major league sports, global brand recognition.

The surprising part

The surprise is not that presence commands a premium—that is forecastable. The surprise is that the scarcity compounds while digital scale plateaus. Streaming expected to win by making content abundant and cheap. Instead, abundance in digital has compressed margins while scarcity in presence has raised prices. Live Nation's $6.6B of event-related deferred revenue in Q1 2026 means customers are pre-paying at rising prices. Host Hotels' RevPAR is rising even though hotel rooms technically exist in abundance. Marriott is signing more owners and expanding the pipeline even as room growth theoretically saturates the market. The second surprise is that capital-intensive businesses can still generate high returns if they control scarce assets. Host Hotels, a capital-heavy REIT, is outperforming many asset-light digital businesses. That upends the assumption that capital intensity is always a disadvantage.

Why it matters

This is how inequality gets baked into experience and culture. The presence premium concentrates spending with the wealthy who can afford premium travel, concerts, and events, while cheaper digital alternatives fragment the audiences available to working-class people. Marriott's luxury properties, Live Nation's arena economics, Fox's premium content, and Host Hotels' high-RevPAR properties are all designed for affluent travelers and consumers. That is not inherently unjust, but it means the best experiences increasingly concentrate with the highest spenders. For society, the implication is that scarcity-driven businesses will keep extracting more value as demand for presence rebounds—and that value extraction may be sustainable indefinitely because the underlying constraint (you cannot manufacture more live moments, more iconic venues, more cultural events) is real.

Presence economics create durable pricing power because the scarcity bottleneck is real, growing, and immune to disruption by capital investment—unlike digital supply, which can always be replicated with enough money.
