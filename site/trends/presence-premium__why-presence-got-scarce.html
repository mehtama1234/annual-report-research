When Everywhere Became Nowhere: The Structural Scarcity of Being There

Digital ubiquity created presence scarcity: once algorithmic content filled every idle moment, synchronized shared attention—in a stadium, at a park, in the same room—became rarer and therefore explicitly priced as a premium category.

The paradox driving presence-premium economics is that digital convenience destroyed the economics of digital convenience. When streaming, social media, and algorithmic recommendations filled every idle moment from 2015-2023, the experience of actually being physically present—in a stadium, at a concert, in a shared social space—became structurally scarce. That scarcity converted presence from a default assumption into a premium category with explicit pricing power. Live Nation, Disney Parks, Fox Sports, and Sirius XM all demonstrate 2025-2026 evidence of presence-based spending pulling away from digital media spending.

The mechanism

The scarcity mechanism arrived through a paradox of digital saturation. From 2015-2023, streaming, social media, and algorithmic recommendations created infinite content availability—there was always something to watch, always something to scroll, always a next episode queued. That infinite availability devalued the experience of synchronized presence: why wait 3 hours for a concert start time when you can watch anything instantly? But the paradox is that infinite choice collapsed attention. When everything is available, nothing is scarce; when nothing is scarce, nothing is particularly desired. The market corrected by revaluing the one thing that algorithms cannot deliver: coordinated human presence in a specific place at a specific time. That is structurally scarce—you can only be in one stadium at one time, and so can everyone else trying to see that game.

Live Nation's record attendance and Disney Parks' pricing power exemplify the inflection. Live Nation, as the largest operator of live events, saw 2025-2026 as a peak-demand environment where ticket prices rose to capture scarcity value, and customers paid them. Disney Parks, selling presence (being physically inside Disneyland) against infinite streaming content (Disney+), raised prices 10%+ annually and still saw attendance grow because presence has become the differentiated product. Streaming is infinitely available, infinitely reproducible, infinitely deflatable in value; parks, stadiums, concerts, live sports are finite, time-bound, and becoming structurally scarce as digital saturation concentrates attention elsewhere. The pricing inflection reflects the economic reality: presence is now the scarce commodity.

The compounding works through consumer behavior data. Sirius XM's subscriber base stabilized even as free, ad-supported streaming music proliferated, because Sirius XM's value proposition shifted from just music (commoditized) to live, unfiltered, presence-dependent content (Howard Stern, live sports commentary, news). Fox Sports commands advertising premiums for live events because advertisers know live events deliver undistracted human attention—you cannot skip ads on live TV, you cannot multitask during a crucial game moment. That scarcity of attention is the thing being monetized, not the content itself. Every dollar captured in live-event premiums and live-sports advertising is a dollar that would have gone to digital media when presence was invisible as a value driver.

Why now

Three shifts crystallized the presence-scarcity inflection. First, streaming and algorithmic content saturation peaked around 2023, at which point every platform was fighting for a smaller share of attention and willingness-to-pay was declining. Disney+ losing subscribers, Netflix raising prices to stabilize revenue, and platforms consolidating around bundling showed digital content hitting commoditization. Second, pandemic-induced lockdowns and remote work created a generational reset in preferences. Millennials and Gen Z who had normalized digital-first leisure during 2020-2021 began actively seeking physical presence and social coordination again around 2023-2025, making in-person experiences feel newly valuable. Third, algorithmic feed degradation (platforms flooded with AI-generated content, bots, spam, and controversial material) made digital media feel less trustworthy and less pleasant, driving younger users toward in-person social experiences as the only unmediated space.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Consumers of live experiences pay premium prices: $40-200+ for event tickets, $100+ per day for theme parks, $20+/month for live sports subscriptions. Younger and lower-income consumers are priced out of frequent live-event participation, creating a two-tier system where wealthy consumers get unlimited presence-based leisure and lower-income consumers consume digital media as a cheaper substitute. Advertisers pay premium CPMs for live-event advertising because of the attention guarantee. Streaming companies pay indirectly—they are losing engagement hours and subscription retention to presence-based alternatives.
Who profits
Live Nation, Disney (Parks & Experiences), Fox (Sports broadcasting), and Sirius XM (live audio content) capture outsized profitability. Live Nation's venue network and ticketing control create a moat—the more events they control, the more scarcity they can extract. Disney Parks margins are rising faster than other segments because pricing power is high and supply is essentially fixed (you can only build so many parks). Fox Sports benefits from live sports' structural necessity and attention guarantee. Regional entertainment venues and local attractions are also benefiting from presence-premium demand. Capital markets have begun rewarding these companies at premiums because presence-based businesses have durable, pricing-resistant cash flows.

The tension

The tension is that presence-premium pricing is creating access inequality at an accelerating rate. If live events and theme parks become accessible only to top income quintiles, presence-based leisure becomes a marker of wealth inequality rather than shared culture. A generation ago, baseball games, concerts, and theme parks were family events across income levels; today's pricing structure is pushing them toward luxury goods. Second, the presence-premium moat is contingent on digital commoditization continuing—if digital experiences improve, if metaverse or immersive digital experiences credibly replicate presence, the scarcity evaporates and pricing power collapses. Third, concentration of live events in major metropolitan areas is widening geographic inequality: presence-premium experiences are disproportionately available to coastal, urban, high-income consumers.

The surprising part

The non-obvious part is that presence scarcity is not about supply constraints—Live Nation, Disney, and Fox could theoretically increase supply (more events, bigger parks, more broadcasts). But they are actively restricting supply to maintain pricing power. Ticket allocations, theme park capacity management, and live sports exclusivity are all choices to preserve scarcity. That means presence-premium is not a natural market outcome; it is an actively maintained pricing structure. It also means the scarcity is reversible: if live-event companies decided to increase supply and lower prices, the premium would compress instantly. The economics are durable only if companies collectively agree to restrict supply.

Why it matters

When presence becomes premium and digital becomes commodity, we are sorting leisure experience quality by income level. Wealthy people attend concerts, theme parks, and live events; everyone else streams recorded content. Over time, that creates compound cultural inequality—wealthier cohorts accumulate direct social experiences and memories while lower-income cohorts consume mediated, algorithmic content. The social stake is that shared culture—communities gathering for live events—is being fractured along economic lines. The presence-premium economy is not creating new culture; it is rationing existing culture by price.

Digital ubiquity made synchronized human presence structurally scarce and economically valuable in ways that digital media can never replicate, creating a new premium category with pricing power.
