Why Presence Got Scarce: Live Attention as Premium Inventory

Streaming saturation, fragmented digital attention, and the loss of cultural appointment television forced live events and real-time experiences into a premium category. Live Nation, Disney, Fox, and Sirius XM all benefited from scarcity pricing and deferred-revenue strength in 2025-2026. Live Nation's event-related deferred revenue hit record $6.6B by Q1 2026; Disney's streaming losses (Disney+ churn pressure) pushed advertising and parks experience monetization higher; Fox's Q4 fiscal 2026 advertising jumped 78% on FIFA World Cup inventory; Sirius XM's Q2 2026 churn hit record-low 1.4%, showing that exclusive live content and in-car presence create utility-like stickiness. The pattern revealed that shared, time-sensitive, real-world presence (concerts, sports, parks, exclusive audio) commands premium pricing when digital alternatives are abundant but interchangeable.

Scarcity of shared, time-specific, cultural attention will sustain premium pricing for live events and exclusive content. Companies that own appointment-based or in-person channels (Ticketmaster, parks, sports, live radio) will command pricing power even as digital-only content faces commoditization.

The mechanism

Streaming competition (Netflix, Disney+, Amazon Prime, YouTube, TikTok) commoditized on-demand video content. Consumers have infinite choice at low subscription cost. This eroded premium pricing and drove churn for streaming-only services (Disney+ lost subscribers in 2024-2025 before stabilizing).

Live, appointment-based content (sports, concerts, news, exclusive broadcasts) became the refuge for scarcity pricing because it cannot be recorded and watched later at convenience. Time-sensitivity creates FOMO (fear of missing out) and forces real-time consumption. Ticketing, deferred revenue, and dynamic pricing all followed.

Presence-based experiences (concerts, parks, live performances) became even scarcer than broadcast live content because they require physical attendance. This layered supply constraint (limited venues, artist schedules, dates) into pricing power that streaming aggregators could not replicate.

Exclusive content relationships (Sirius XM's Howard Stern, Live Nation's artist relationships, Fox's sports rights, Disney's parks IP) created switching costs and reduced churn to utility-like levels.

Why now

The inflection came in late 2023-2024 as streaming subscriber growth plateaued and churn ticked up. Netflix's crack-down on password sharing, Disney+ losses, and the broader 'streaming wars' narrative showed that the 2015-2020 growth model was exhausted. Simultaneously, live-event demand rebounded post-pandemic in 2023-2024. By 2025-2026, deferred-revenue strength and pricing power for live events were undeniable. Live Nation Q1 2026 deferred revenue hit record $6.6B. Fox fiscal 2026 advertising soared 78% largely on FIFA World Cup timing. Sirius XM achieved record-low churn (1.4% in Q2 2026) by defending its direct-billed subscriber base and adding Pandora ad inventory. Disney's Experiences segment hit record operating income even as Entertainment segment struggled, signaling that physical presence was outperforming digital content. This was not a temporary spike; it was structural recognition that digital-only content is commodity and live presence is scarce.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Consumers of live experiences (concert/sports tickets, theme-park visits, subscription audio services, premium advertising-free streaming) pay via ticketing, subscriptions, and dynamic pricing.
Who profits
Live Nation (tickets + venue economics), Disney (parks + resort experiences), Fox (sports rights + advertising), Sirius XM (subscriber base), and the artists/leagues/creators who own exclusive content. Consumer-facing digital-only platforms (Netflix, YouTube, TikTok) face margin pressure as their content pool becomes commoditized.

The tension

The tension is whether live-event premium pricing can be sustained if economic stress forces households to cut discretionary spending. If recession arrives, luxury concert tickets and parks visits are first to be deferred. Additionally, if streaming platforms successfully differentiate live content (e.g., Netflix sports initiatives, Amazon Prime sports rights), they could erode the scarcity premium of pure-play live operators.

The surprising part

Disney's Experiences segment hit record operating income ($10.0B) in FY2025 while Entertainment segment strained (streaming losses, content-cost pressure). This complete inversion—physical experiences thriving while digital content monetization stalled—was not predicted in 2021-2022 streaming-enthusiasm discourse. It revealed that Disney's real moat was not subscriber count but the emotional power of the parks experience and the ability to capture families' discretionary spending on vacations rather than on streaming subscriptions.

Why it matters

Cultural access and shared experience. If live-event pricing escalates, lower-income households lose access to live culture. If streaming provides the only entertainment alternative, it concentrates attention and cultural narrative among platform algorithms. If live experiences remain accessible, they preserve community gathering and shared memory.

Streaming saturation commoditized on-demand content, making live, time-sensitive, and presence-based experiences the scarcity value, allowing premium pricing for concerts, sports, parks, and exclusive audio.
