The Private Capital Colonization

Alternative asset managers have absorbed trillions in insurance float, pension obligations, and retail savings since 2022 — capturing fee structures 20x higher than regulated banks for identical economic risk, while remaining invisible to the regulatory safety net built to handle the next crisis.

The financial system's credit-intermediation function is migrating from regulated banks into alternative asset platforms that face far fewer capital constraints and charge far higher fees. KKR's $796B AUM (Q2 2026) now spans private equity, credit, infrastructure, and insurance through Global Atlantic. Blackstone's $1.346T AUM includes $523.6B in perpetual capital. Prudential's PGIM manages $1.49T in assets alongside its insurance underwriting. These platforms are not replacing banks at the margin — they are replacing the core. A pension fund that once paid a bank 10 basis points for a deposit or credit line now pays a private-credit fund 200 basis points for an illiquid commitment covering the same duration and credit risk. The manager's economics increase 20-fold; the client's risk-adjusted return is comparable. The driver is regulatory arbitrage: post-2022 bank capital tightening pushed credit supply toward alternative managers who face comparatively few constraints. Basel III endgame proposals, the SVB/Signature/First Republic failures, and FDIC-driven emergency sales all occurred in a single 12-month window in 2022-2023 — compressing a decade of gradual drift into a structural rupture.

Private capital colonization is accelerating across the data. Blackstone, Apollo, Ares, and Brookfield all show record or near-record capital scale, with alternative managers now absorbing tens of billions per quarter from institutions and insurance pools. Fee-generating AUM growth, perpetual capital expansion, and record available capital are outpacing realizations, proving that platforms are successfully converting former bank credit functions into higher-fee private capital channels. Franklin's alternatives surge to $294B confirms the trend extends across manager types. The regulatory arbitrage is structural: insurance balance-sheet capital and institutional outsourcing are flowing toward firms with fewer capital constraints and fatter fee structures.

The mechanism

The architecture of the shift is three-layer. First, alternative managers absorb insurance float. KKR owns Global Atlantic; Prudential's PGIM manages $1.49T alongside its underwriting book. Insurance premiums collected but not yet paid out — historically held in Treasuries or investment-grade bonds — are now being deployed into long-duration private-credit funds, infrastructure equity, and real-estate debt. The float goes into illiquid vehicles rather than liquid holdings. This works cleanly until claims spike or policyholders withdraw at scale.

Second, pension and sovereign capital outsources liability management. Pension de-risking and retirement-risk transfer create durable institutional demand for private-market liability matching. Prudential's fiscal 2025 after-tax adjusted operating income of $5.161B is substantially built on capturing this flow. Blackstone's $523.6B perpetual capital base is the same phenomenon at scale — capital that does not redeem, because the liability it matches is long-dated. The manager earns recurring fees on that capital indefinitely.

Third, consumer credit migrates outside banking entirely. Affirm's Q3 2026 GMV of $11.6B — up 48% year-over-year — represents installment credit advanced by capital-markets-funded vehicles, not FDIC-insured banks. Over 65% of approved U.S. loan applications on Affirm's platform were presented with a 0% APR offer, funded by merchants rather than by the borrower paying interest to a bank. Affirm's average annualized cost of funds reached its lowest level in three and a half years in Q3 2026, meaning capital-markets investors are pricing this private consumer credit tightly — a sign of confidence that also signals systemic concentration risk if a large merchant or consumer segment deteriorates simultaneously.

The fee math makes the migration self-reinforcing. A regulated bank holding $100M in loans under Basel III endgame rules must hold approximately $10M in capital, leaving the bank earning a constrained net interest margin of roughly 2-3% on assets. A private-credit fund holding the same $100M in loans through a leveraged vehicle might hold $3-5M of equity, charge a 2% management fee plus 20% carried interest, and earn 3-4x the economics per dollar of equity deployed. The supply of institutional capital flowing into private markets is not yet capacity-constrained, so the arbitrage persists.

Why now

Three specific events in a compressed window created the structural break. In March 2023, Silicon Valley Bank failed — the second-largest U.S. bank failure in history — after duration mismatches on its bond portfolio exposed it to deposit flight in a rising-rate environment. Signature Bank closed the same weekend. First Republic Bank was seized by the FDIC and sold to JPMorgan in May 2023. All three failures occurred within 60 days. The regulatory response was accelerated Basel III endgame rulemaking, higher capital surcharges on large bank holding companies, and FDIC stress-testing expansions — all tightening the constraints on regulated bank credit supply. Alternative managers stepped into the gap. KKR explicitly labeled asset-based finance a 'major live growth vector' and positioned it as nonbank credit supply. In 2025 alone, KKR raised $129B — a record — and deployed $95B. Blackstone raised $239.4B in full-year 2025 inflows and grew AUM to $1.2749T. These numbers are not the product of a decade of secular drift. They are the product of a single regulatory rupture in 2023 that redirected credit supply at scale.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Limited partners — pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, endowments, and increasingly retail investors via advisor channels — pay 2-and-20 fee structures on private-market commitments, compared to 10-30 basis points for equivalent bank or mutual-fund exposure. Bank customers face tighter credit availability because bank capital is now directed toward higher-return regulatory-arbitrage activities rather than incremental lending. Small businesses and individuals whose banking relationships were routed through regional banks — particularly those similar to SVB, Signature, and First Republic — have fewer institutional alternatives. Communities where local banks consolidated decades ago are losing the private-credit alternatives that partially replaced them. Retail consumers borrowing through Affirm and similar platforms pay merchant-funded 0% APR offers that appear free but are priced into merchant economics and ultimately consumer prices.
Who profits
Alternative asset manager founders, partners, and shareholders capture the fee spread and carried interest. KKR's 2025 fee-related earnings reached $3.7B; Blackstone's reached $5.7B. Institutional allocators with early or preferred access to private-market vehicles earn returns unavailable to retail. High-net-worth individuals gain access through advisor channels as platforms like KKR's K-Series and Blackstone's BREIT distribute private exposure to individual investors. Insurance companies that deploy float into higher-yielding private alternatives retain more spread income. Fintech platforms that intermediate consumer credit outside banking — Affirm at $11.6B quarterly GMV — capture merchant fees and underwriting economics at scale.

The tension

The private-capital system is not backstopped. When a bank deposit is withdrawn, FDIC insurance pays within days and the Fed can act as lender of last resort. When a pension fund needs liquidity and calls for redemption from a private-credit fund, it faces gate provisions, extended settlement periods, and suspension of withdrawals. Blackstone's BREIT — a $70B-plus nontraded REIT — activated redemption gates in late 2022 when institutional investors sought liquidity during the rate-shock selloff. That was a dry run with contained damage. At $1.346T of AUM and $523.6B in perpetual capital, a similar gate activation by Blackstone in a genuine crisis — not just a rate normalization — would cascade. Pension funds forced to sell public equities to meet obligations would hit public-market prices; insurance companies with illiquid private holdings facing claim spikes would face a simultaneous funding and asset-value crisis. Regulators cannot monitor this in real time. Private-credit concentration, collateral quality, and interconnection between insurance float, pension capital, and consumer-credit vehicles are not reported on any public register.

The surprising part

Blackstone is now a larger credit issuer than many money-center banks measured by annual deployment. Blackstone deployed $138.2B in 2025 — more than the annual loan origination of most regional U.S. banks — and raised $239.4B in new inflows. None of this capital flows through the FDIC-insured banking system. None of it sits on a balance sheet visible to the Fed's stress-testing regime. The institutional investors funding Blackstone's deployment are pension funds and insurance companies whose own liabilities are regulated — but whose assets (once committed to Blackstone) disappear into a structure that is not. A second non-obvious fact: Invesco's QQQ converted from a unit investment trust to an open-end ETF on December 20, 2025, modernizing one of the largest single pools of indexed equity capital ($407.2B as of Q2 2026) in a structural change that makes it easier to absorb and distribute institutional capital — another quiet migration of financial plumbing that happens below most regulatory radar.

Why it matters

Capital is migrating from institutions that are required to report, insured to protect depositors, and backstopped by the Fed into vehicles that are opaque, illiquid, and outside the safety net. The concentrated economics benefit a narrow tier of asset-manager founders, institutional allocators with preferred access, and high-net-worth individuals with advisor relationships. Workers whose pensions are committed to private platforms have no recourse if those platforms gate withdrawals or mark assets down. Communities that already lost local-bank credit access face a second squeeze as private-credit alternatives price to institutional investors rather than local economic need. Retirees whose pension obligations have been outsourced to Prudential, Blackstone, or KKR through pension-risk-transfer deals receive promises whose credit quality depends on asset-management performance in illiquid markets — not on FDIC insurance or Fed backstops. The systemic risk is not hypothetical: it has already been partially demonstrated in the 2022 BREIT gate activation and the 2023 regional-bank collapses that drove capital further into the same private system.

A $2-3 trillion annual flow of insurance float, pension capital, and consumer credit has left the regulated banking system for private platforms that charge 20x higher fees, face no deposit insurance requirement, and cannot be seen — let alone stress-tested — by the regulators who would manage the next crisis.

The case against this trend

What would falsify it: Evidence that regulatory constraints on banks have NOT tightened, or that private-market fee multiples are NOT superior to bank economics, or that capital flows have NOT shifted materially away from regulated banks since 2023.

Companies that don't fit:

  • Invesco Ltd. — Despite record Q2 2026 inflows of $45.1B, Invesco's operating revenue of $1.826B on $2.170T of AUM generates a net revenue per dollar of AUM that is 2-3x LOWER than a private-credit manager charging 2-and-20. This suggests that not all capital flowing to alternatives is accepting the higher-fee model; ETF and index products still attract substantial capital at far lower economics.
  • Prudential Financial — Despite PGIM's $1.49T AUM, Prudential's fiscal 2025 after-tax adjusted operating income was only $5.161B. This works out to ~32 basis points of operating profit on AUM — comparable to or LOWER than many traditional banks' return on assets. The private-capital thesis assumes superior fee capture, but Prudential's earnings show that combining insurance liabilities with asset management does not automatically drive higher fee economics than traditional financial intermediation.
  • Affirm Holdings — Affirm achieved Q3 2026 adjusted operating margin of only 27% of revenue on $1.039B of revenue. This translates to roughly 10-20 basis points of margin on assets financed. For all the media about merchant-funded 0% APR offers and innovative underwriting, Affirm's actual margin profile is not demonstrably superior to consumer-finance competitors or regulated lenders. The company is growing rapidly, but not proving that private-market consumer credit economics outstrip regulated alternatives.

Honest caveat: This trend accurately describes the flow of INSTITUTIONAL capital (insurance float, pension obligations, sovereign wealth) into alternatives, and it correctly identifies the regulatory arbitrage in private credit and infrastructure. However, the trend does NOT prove that private alternatives are a systemic replacement for banking as a whole. Retail deposits continue flowing into banks despite regulatory uncertainty. Small-business lending, which makes up a large fraction of actual bank originations, is NOT being captured at scale by private-credit players. Public fixed-income markets still dominate absolute capital deployment. The thesis describes a real financial migration at the TOP of the system (institutions, platforms, ultra-large capital pools), but conflates scale and visibility with systemic replacement. A more precise claim would be: 'Alternative managers are capturing a disproportionate share of GROWTH in institutional capital deployment, converting this into higher fees, but legacy banking systems persist for retail and small-business credit, ensuring no single point-of-failure crisis across the entire financial system.'

How It Happened
Origin

When the Banks Stepped Back

Post-2008 Basel III capital requirements and post-2022 regional bank failures forced regulated lenders to shrink credit supply and raise capital ratios, creating a regulatory vacuum that alternative asset managers rushed to fill with private credit, asset-based finance, and direct lending.

Sub-pattern

Insurance-Float Capture: Permanent Capital for Private Investments

Alternative asset managers embed insurance subsidiaries to access premium float (policyholder premiums collected today for claims paid in the future) as permanent capital funding higher-fee private investments. KKR, Apollo, Brookfield, and Berkshire operate insurance platforms converting premiums into permanent deployment capital.

Sub-pattern

Pension De-Risking: The $Trillion Liability Transfer

Corporate pension sponsors are systematically paying life insurers to assume longevity risk, creating durable, fee-generating asset flows that have become a structural earnings driver for Prudential, MetLife, and other liability managers.

Sub-pattern

The Retail Unlock: Selling Private Markets to Main Street

Since 2024, KKR, Blackstone, Franklin, and Invesco have accelerated distribution of private equity, private credit, and infrastructure through registered investment advisors and wealth platforms. Retail now holds illiquid private-market exposure that was historically reserved for pensions and sovereigns.

Apollo's Two-Engine Machine: Fee Earnings Meet Spread Earnings

Apollo is the clearest proof that the colonization is structural: $1.05T AUM earning fee-related earnings AND spread-related earnings through Athene, making it simultaneously an asset manager and a credit-manufacturing balance sheet without being regulated as a bank.

Who profits

Who Profits: Fee Arbitrage

KKR raised $129B in 2025 and deployed $95B with $3.7B in FRE on $744B AUM. Prudential's PGIM manages $1.609T alongside insurance underwriting. Invesco reported $45.1B in record net long-term inflows in Q2 2026, led by ETFs and private markets. These alternative asset managers shifted capital from regulated banks earning 10 basis points on deposits into private-credit funds earning 200+ basis points in management fees—a 20-fold fee arbitrage. Post-2022 bank failures and tightened capital rules accelerated the shift.

The Fragility Underneath
Who pays

Who Pays: The Burden Carriers

Credit creation is leaving regulated banks for asset managers and fintech operators. The winners bank recurring fees; the bill lands on pensioners, small businesses, and communities that get cut off first when the cycle turns.

The tension

The Systemic Blind Spot: Private Capital's Shadow Credit System

Ares holds $170B of dry powder with realized income declining per AUM unit, Carlyle's performance revenues swung 5.5x between quarters (proving exit timing, not fundamentals, drives returns), and Brookfield locked $44B of insurance capital into illiquid strategies. Regulators cannot see LP redemption pressure, portfolio credit quality, or cascade risk because private capital systematically escapes transparency that governs public markets and banks.
