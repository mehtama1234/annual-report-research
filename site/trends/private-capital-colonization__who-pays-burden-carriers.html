Who Pays: The Burden Carriers

Credit creation is leaving regulated banks for asset managers and fintech operators. The winners bank recurring fees; the bill lands on pensioners, small businesses, and communities that get cut off first when the cycle turns.

The largest alternative-asset managers and consumer-finance operators are betting that lending, once a bank function, is now a fee-and-network business they run better and price better. They capture the high-quality borrowers and the recurring fee streams; regulated banks keep the residual, and the downside risk migrates to whoever holds the long-duration capital and whoever needs credit when it withdraws.

The mechanism

Alternative managers raise long-duration and perpetual capital from pension funds, insurers, and increasingly individual investors, then lend it directly, sidestepping deposit-funded regulated banks.

The economics favor recurring fee-related earnings on committed capital over one-time spreads: Blackstone books $5.7B of fee-related earnings on $921.7B of fee-earning AUM, and KKR frames insurance, asset-based finance, and wealth distribution as core fee engines, not sidelines.

Insurance float and perpetual vehicles give these firms permanent, non-callable balance sheets that let them underwrite credit banks can't hold, capturing prime borrowers at tight spreads while banks keep the residual book.

At the consumer end, Affirm originates installment credit at checkout and funds it through capital markets rather than deposits, with 72% of FY2025's $36.7B GMV interest-bearing, so credit creation itself moves off bank balance sheets.

Because the funding is committed rather than demand-deposit, the providers can withdraw fast in a downturn without a bank-run constraint, which is exactly when small businesses and communities need credit most.

Why now

The inflection is the 2025-2026 fundraising and deployment surge into private credit while banks retrenched. KKR raised a record $129B and deployed $95B in 2025; Blackstone crossed a record $1.346T AUM by 2Q26 with $523.6B of it perpetual capital; Carlyle called 2Q26 one of its strongest quarters in years with record fee-related earnings. Simultaneously Affirm logged its tenth consecutive quarter of 30%-plus GMV growth ($11.6B in Q3 2026) at the lowest cost of funds in three and a half years. Credit formation visibly shifted venue at scale in a single window.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Pension beneficiaries and insurance policyholders whose long-duration capital pays the recurring fees that make up billions in fee-related earnings; small businesses that lose access as prime borrowers are cherry-picked and residual credit is repriced; and communities exposed to procyclical private credit that can be pulled fast because the funding is committed rather than deposit-based.
Who profits
Blackstone, KKR, and Carlyle, which convert scale fundraising into durable fee-related earnings ($5.7B, $3.7B, and $1.2B respectively in 2025) that recur regardless of market direction; and operators like Affirm that own the point-of-sale credit surface and monetize merchant fees, interest, and card-linked flows outside the regulated banking perimeter.

The tension

These firms argue they price and underwrite credit more efficiently than banks and take risk with permanent capital that can't run. The counter is that the same permanence lets them exit borrowers on their own schedule, and the fees are paid by the pension and insurance savers who have the least ability to negotiate them.

The surprising part

The prize isn't the lending spread, it's the fee on committed capital. Blackstone earns $5.7B in fee-related earnings on $921.7B of fee-earning AUM whether markets rise or fall, and $523.6B of that capital is perpetual and can't be redeemed. That permanence, not superior underwriting, is the structural edge over a deposit-funded bank.

Why it matters

When credit creation migrates to committed private capital, the system loses the deposit-funded, regulated backstop that historically kept lending available through downturns. Prime borrowers get cheaper credit and savers pay recurring fees, but small businesses and communities face credit that can withdraw fast and on the provider's terms, concentrating the cost of the next downturn on those with the least cushion.

The business of making credit has quietly moved from banks that answer to depositors and regulators to asset managers and fintechs that answer to fee streams, and the people funding it are the pensioners and policyholders least able to see or price the transfer.
