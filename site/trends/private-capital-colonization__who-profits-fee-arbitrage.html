The mechanism

KKR, Prudential, and Invesco sit at the axis of capital migration. KKR's $744B AUM spans private equity, credit, infrastructure, and insurance through Global Atlantic. Prudential's PGIM manages $1.609T in assets—nearly 40% more than Prudential's own insurance float. Invesco's ETF platform ($630.2B AUM) and private-markets business ($130.7B) sit alongside China exposure and traditional mutual funds. The structure is intentional: these platforms simultaneously act as asset managers, insurers, credit intermediaries, and wealth distributors. The fee mechanism is straightforward. Regulated banks earn tight spreads on deposits—roughly 10 basis points on a $1 million deposit versus what banks pay depositors. Private-credit platforms capture 2-and-20 fee structures (2% management fees + 20% carried interest) on the same $1 of capital, generating vastly higher economics for managers. A pension fund paying a bank 10 basis points for a deposit infrastructure now pays a private-credit fund 200 basis points for an illiquid fund commitment. The client gets similar duration and credit risk; the manager's take increases 20-fold.

Post-2022 bank failures (Silicon Valley Bank, Signature Bank, First Republic) and tightened regulatory capital requirements pushed credit supply toward the alternative universe. KKR explicitly framed asset-based finance as a 'major live growth vector' and positioned it as credit supply for non-bank lending. Regional banks now face 10% capital requirements against loans; private-credit funds can operate with 1% capital and fund the rest with leverage. Invesco raised $45.1B in record net long-term inflows in Q2 2026, led by ETFs and private markets. Affirm grew direct-to-consumer GMV 48% year-over-year by embedding merchant-funded 0% APR offers outside regulated banking—the credit decision and underwriting profit captured outside the banking system's visibility.

The layered risk is that insurance companies are becoming de facto private-equity platforms. Prudential's PGIM manages $1.609T, a portfolio nearly 40% larger than Prudential's insurance liabilities. Insurance float—premiums collected but not yet paid out—is being deployed into long-duration private assets rather than safe, liquid holdings. This works beautifully in a low-loss environment. In a catastrophic loss year or mass policyholder withdrawal, the float is trapped in illiquid funds and cannot be accessed for claim payments. The hidden correlation is that pension de-risking, retirement outsourcing, and insurance-liability hedging are all pushing capital into the same private platforms at the same time, creating concentration risk that regulators cannot see in real time.

Why now

The structural pressures are real and visible in 2025-2026 filings. Banks face tighter post-crisis capital rules; alternative managers face comparatively few constraints. KKR raised a record $129B in 2025 and deployed $95B, showing that limited partners have confidence despite geopolitical uncertainty. Invesco reported record net inflows led by ETFs and private markets. Prudential's PGIM AUM rose to $1.609T as retirement de-risking and pension liability outsourcing created durable demand for institutional asset management. The regulatory asymmetry is stark: a regional bank must hold 10% capital against a $100M loan; a private-credit fund can hold 1% and fund the rest with leverage. The shift is not aspirational—it is structural and accelerating between 2023 and 2026 as the capital displacement compounds.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Limited partners (pension funds, insurance companies, sovereign wealth funds, increasingly retail via financial advisors) pay 2-and-20 fee structures on private commitments without seeing the full fee stack. Bank customers face tighter credit because bank capital is now more constrained and allocated to higher-return regulatory arbitrage. Small businesses and consumers depend on bank credit for lending, equipment financing, lines of credit—all shrinking as bank capital flows toward private markets. Communities that relied on bank credit for affordable housing and local development lose a stable capital source. Workers whose pension funds are now allocated to illiquid private commitments lose liquidity, recourse, and transparency.
Who profits
Alternative asset manager founders, partners, and shareholders. KKR's $3.7B FRE on $744B AUM is a perpetual fee machine independent of deal outcomes. Invesco's 37.5% operating margin on $1.826B revenue from ETF and private-markets distribution. Prudential's PGIM segment earning substantial fees while managing $1.609T. High-net-worth individuals and ultra-wealthy with access to private-market returns and inside distribution channels. Fintech platforms (Affirm) that intermediate consumer lending outside banking capture merchant fees and underwriting profits. Insurance companies that deploy float into higher-yielding private alternatives retain more earnings.

The tension

The shift of credit creation from regulated, transparent institutions to alternative managers reduces systemic visibility and regulatory oversight. When the next liquidity crisis hits, private-credit portfolios are illiquid in ways that bank deposits are not. Withdrawals from bank deposits trigger FDIC insurance and central-bank backstops. Redemption requests from private-credit funds trigger gate locks, suspension of withdrawals, and extended settlement. The regulatory safety net does not extend to these structures. If a large pension fund suddenly needs liquidity and cannot redeem from private-credit allocations, it must liquidate stocks or bonds at fire-sale prices, cascading losses through markets. Meanwhile, regulators lack real-time visibility into private-credit concentration, collateral quality, or interconnection.

The surprising part

Insurance companies are becoming de facto private-equity platforms without the transparency or governance that equity partnerships require. Prudential's PGIM manages $1.609T—a portfolio 40% larger than Prudential's own insurance business. This works beautifully when losses are low and markets are stable. In a catastrophic year, the float is trapped in illiquid private funds and cannot be accessed for claim payments. The hidden correlation is that pension de-risking, retirement outsourcing, and insurance-liability hedging are all pushing capital into the same platforms simultaneously, creating concentration risk that regulators cannot measure. The second surprise is that the regulatory capture is so complete that regulators cannot even describe what is happening. Private-credit fund size, collateral quality, and interconnection are opaque. The fee stack (management fees, carried interest, hidden GP stakes in underlying funds) is invisible to LPs. The efficiency gains from regulatory arbitrage flow entirely to asset managers.

Why it matters

Capital is migrating from regulated, transparent institutions into private structures that are opaque, difficult to audit, and not backstopped by deposit insurance or central-bank facilities. This concentrates systemic risk in places regulators cannot see. When credit freezes, workers dependent on small-business lending are first to suffer. Communities that lost local banks decades ago are losing access to alternatives. Retirees whose pension obligations are held by private platforms lose recourse if those platforms fail. The concentration of fee economics in private hands means returns that were once democratized through bank deposits and mutual funds are now hoarded by the wealthy and well-connected.

Trillions in capital are migrating from regulated banks into private asset platforms at higher fees, leaving regulators blind to systemic risk and depositors, borrowers, and workers bearing the externality when the next crisis hits.
