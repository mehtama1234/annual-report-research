Who Profits: Fee Arbitrage

Alternative asset managers are quietly colonizing the financial system, moving capital from regulated banks into higher-fee private markets while regulators remain structurally blind. Trillions in capital that once flowed through low-fee bank products now flows through 2-and-20 private-equity and private-credit funds, concentrating returns in the hands of asset managers while traditional sources of credit shrink.

Massive private-capital platforms are converting insurance float, retail savings, and pension obligations into fee-generating assets under management at scale. The mechanism is fee-rate arbitrage: shifting low-fee regulated bank products into higher-fee alternatives while providing the same economic function. Post-2022 bank failures and tighter capital rules pushed credit supply from banks toward private platforms that face fewer constraints.

The mechanism

KKR, Prudential, and Invesco sit at the axis of this shift. KKR's $744B AUM spans private equity, credit, infrastructure, and insurance through Global Atlantic. Prudential's PGIM manages $1.609T alongside insurance underwriting. Invesco's QQQ ETF platform ($407.2B) and private markets ($130.7B) sit alongside a China JV. The structure is intentional: these platforms simultaneously act as asset managers, insurers, credit intermediaries, and wealth distributors. The fee mechanism is straightforward. Regulated banks earn tight spreads on deposits and lend at slightly higher rates. Private-credit platforms capture 2-and-20 fee structures (2% management fees + 20% carried interest) on the same $1 of capital, generating vastly higher economics for managers. A pension fund paying a bank 10 basis points for a deposit now pays a private-credit fund 200 basis points for an illiquid fund commitment. The client gets similar duration and credit risk; the manager's take increases 20-fold. The shift accelerated post-2022. Silicon Valley Bank, Signature Bank, and First Republic Bank failed, spurring regulatory tightening on bank capital requirements and deposit concentration. This pushed credit supply toward the alternative universe. KKR explicitly framed asset-based finance as a 'major live growth vector' and positioned it as credit supply for non-bank lending. Invesco raised $45.1B in net long-term inflows in Q2 2026, led by ETFs, private markets, and China exposure. Affirm grew direct-to-consumer GMV 48% YoY, embedding merchant-funded 0% APR offers outside regulated banking entirely.

Why now

The structural pressures are real. Banks face tighter post-crisis capital rules; alternative managers face comparatively few constraints. KKR raised a record $129B in 2025 and deployed $95B. Invesco reported record net inflows led by ETFs and private markets. Prudential's PGIM AUM rose to $1.49T at Q2 2026 as retirement de-risking and pension liability outsourcing created durable demand for institutional asset management and liability assumption. The regulatory asymmetry is stark: a regional bank must hold 10% capital against a $100M loan; a private-credit fund can hold 1% and fund the rest with leverage.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Limited partners (pension funds, insurance companies, sovereign wealth funds, increasingly retail via financial advisors) pay 2-and-20 fee structures on private commitments. Bank customers face tighter credit because bank capital is now more constrained and allocated to higher-return regulatory arbitrage. Small businesses and consumers depend on bank credit for lending, equipment financing, and lines of credit—all of which are shrinking as bank capital flows toward private markets. Communities that relied on bank credit for affordable housing and local development lose a stable capital source. Workers whose pension funds are now allocated to illiquid private commitments lose liquidity and recourse.
Who profits
Alternative asset manager founders, partners, and shareholders capture the fee spread and carried interest. Institutional allocators with access to private markets and inside distribution earn outsized returns unavailable to retail. High-net-worth individuals and ultra-wealthy gain access through advisor channels. Fintech platforms (Affirm, others) that intermediate consumer lending outside banking capture merchant fees and underwriting profits. Insurance companies that deploy their float into higher-yielding private alternatives (Prudential's model) retain more earnings. KKR's $3.7B FRE on $744B AUM creates a perpetual fee machine.

The tension

The shift of credit creation from regulated, transparent institutions to alternative managers reduces systemic visibility and regulatory oversight. When the next liquidity crisis hits, private-credit portfolios are illiquid in ways that bank deposits are not. Withdrawals from bank deposits trigger FDIC insurance and central-bank backstops. Redemption requests from private-credit funds trigger gate locks, suspension of withdrawals, and extended settlement periods. The regulatory safety net does not extend to these structures. If a large pension fund suddenly needs liquidity and cannot redeem from private-credit allocations, it must liquidate stocks or bonds at fire-sale prices, cascading losses through markets. Meanwhile, regulators lack real-time visibility into private-credit concentration, collateral quality, or interconnection.

The surprising part

The most non-obvious twist is that insurance companies are becoming de facto private-equity platforms. Prudential PGIM manages $1.609T, nearly a 40% premium to its own insurance underwriting portfolio. Insurance float—premiums collected but not yet paid out—is being deployed into long-duration private assets rather than safe, liquid holdings. This works beautifully in a low-loss environment. In a catastrophic loss year or mass policyholder withdrawal, the float is trapped in illiquid funds. Loews Corporation uses CNA's insurance float as optionality for capital deployment, but even disciplined allocators are stretching into riskier assets. The hidden correlation is that pension de-risking, retirement outsourcing, and insurance-liability hedging are all pushing capital into the same private platforms at the same time, creating concentration risk invisible to regulators.

Why it matters

Capital is migrating from regulated, transparent institutions into private structures that are opaque to the public, difficult to audit, and not backstopped by deposit insurance or central-bank facilities. This concentrates systemic risk in places regulators cannot see. When credit freezes, workers dependent on small-business lending are first to suffer. Communities that lost local banks to consolidation decades ago are losing access to the credit alternatives that once replaced them. Retirees whose pension obligations are now held by private platforms lose recourse if those platforms fail. The concentration of fee economics in private hands also means returns that once were democratized through bank deposits, mutual funds, and public pensions are now hoarded by the wealthy and well-connected.

Trillions in capital are migrating from regulated banks into private asset platforms at higher fees, leaving regulators blind to systemic risk and depositors, borrowers, and workers bearing the externality when the next crisis hits.
