The Ritual Economy

When consumers compress discretionary spending, the habits tied to daily identity and life-stage milestones hold — pet care, morning coffee, oral hygiene, bridal jewelry — while aspirational and hedonic categories collapse first.

Rituals are pre-decided purchases. A consumer wakes up and buys coffee; a pet parent places the Autoship order; a couple shops engagement rings because the occasion demands it. Each of these is a behavior anchored to identity or social occasion — not to marketing pitch — which makes it structurally price-inelastic in a way that fragrance, home décor, or discretionary apparel is not. Companies that successfully embed themselves inside a ritual collect repeat purchase at scale without re-selling the value proposition at each transaction.

The ritual economy is accelerating across consumer-staple, occasion-driven, and membership-anchored businesses. Pet care autoship adoption deepens repeat frequency; loyalty member sales at McDonald's (20% YoY growth) now material to growth narrative; drive-thru beverage transactions sustain multi-quarter growth; occasion spending in jewelry shows resilience; and daily-use hygiene rituals accelerate. The shared signal is that pre-decided, identity-anchored, or occasion-anchored purchases are holding up better than discretionary categories and are increasingly monetized through loyalty, subscription, and repeat-purchase interfaces rather than through one-time retail traffic alone.

The mechanism

A ritual is a behavior pre-decided before the purchase occasion. The consumer has already committed to the category before stepping into the aisle, opening the app, or activating Autoship. That pre-commitment eliminates the re-selling friction that discretionary categories face at each transaction. Pet parenthood, daily oral hygiene, morning coffee, and life-milestone gifting share this property: the consumer defines themselves as someone who does this, which makes reducing or eliminating the behavior carry identity cost, not just financial cost.

The financial signature of ritual positioning is price inelasticity that shows up in volume retention during macro stress. Chewy's Autoship mix reached 84% of net sales in Q3 2025 and the business grew 7.7% in Q1 2026 while adding net customers. Colgate maintained 1.4% organic growth across its oral-care category through 2025 despite explicitly flagging private-label substitution risk. Starbucks rebuilt transaction volume over eight quarters through operational repair (Back to Starbucks) rather than price cuts. Dutch Bros ran 13 consecutive quarters of positive same-shop sales, with transactions still rising in Q2 2026. The mechanism is the same in each case: the customer comes back because the ritual continues.

The tension inside ritual positioning is who pays for the insulation. Ritual brands retain volumes but must still compete on execution quality, convenience, and assortment breadth within their category. Colgate flags private-label as a structural threat to its oral-care moat. Starbucks lost eight quarters of transaction volume not because consumers stopped wanting morning coffee but because service execution deteriorated enough to break the habit. Dutch Bros' drive-thru model depends on in-car commuting patterns remaining stable. Signet competes against experiential travel, which it explicitly names as a substitution risk in its annual filing. Ritual stickiness protects against macro compression; it does not protect against a company executing poorly inside the ritual.

What distinguishes ritual spending from mere habit is identity weight. Pet parents do not think of Chewy Autoship as a subscription they might cancel; they think of it as part of how they manage a family member's wellbeing. Bridal and engagement spending is explicitly linked to life-milestone commitment, which Signet's Grow Brand Love strategy anchors its positioning around. Morning coffee is a transition ritual between private self and public function. When these behaviors compress — as they eventually do in deep recessions or under category substitution — the disruption feels like a loss of identity, not just a spending adjustment. That is why they are the last to go and the first to come back.

Why now

The inflection is 2022 through 2024: the Federal Reserve rate cycle from near-zero to 5.25–5.5% by July 2023, sustained there through mid-2024, created the sharpest real-income compression since the 2008 financial crisis for middle-market consumers. That compression arrived simultaneously with post-pandemic normalization of discretionary spending, turning a credit-fueled consumption boom into a sorting event. The data visible in company filings from 2025 and early 2026 is the first clean post-compression cross-section. Chewy posted record profitability in Q1 2026 while mid-market department stores contracted. Starbucks turned U.S. comparable transaction growth positive for the first time in eight quarters in Q1 2026 — not by changing the product, but by fixing labor and store execution so the morning ritual could function again. Dutch Bros reached record AUV of $2.1M in full-year 2025 while casual dining traffic was broadly under pressure. Colgate posted 1.4% organic growth for full-year 2025 with record operating cash flow of $4.198B. This is not a narrative about 'the shift accelerating.' It is a snapshot of the first year in which the compression became visible enough to separate ritual categories from aspirational ones in filed quarterly results.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Categories that positioned on aspiration, occasional indulgence, or upgrade cycles bore the cost. Fragrance, premium home décor, sporting-goods premiums, and mid-market apparel — none of which can manufacture the identity anchoring of a pet-care relationship or a life-milestone occasion — saw traffic and wallet share compress first. Signet's own filings name experiential travel as the primary competitive category competing for the dollars that might otherwise go to jewelry. Consumers still want these categories; they defer upgrading them. Brands that cannot convert an occasional purchase into a recurring identity behavior lose ground to ritual brands.

The tension

Ritual positioning insulates against macro compression but not against operational failure within the ritual. Starbucks lost eight consecutive quarters of comparable transaction growth — not because consumers abandoned morning coffee, but because service deterioration made the specific Starbucks ritual less reliable than alternatives. That is the first failure mode: the ritual remains but you lose the mandate to be the one who delivers it. The second is category substitution: private-label toothpaste can theoretically own the oral-care ritual if Colgate's brand and science moat erodes. The third is demographic drift: Gen Z coffee behavior differs from Millennial (Dutch Bros' drive-thru model is materially different from the third-place café concept that built Starbucks). The fourth is structural disruption of the occasion itself: Signet's own filings flag a trend toward delayed marriages and declining engagement rates as a long-run headwind to bridal spending, regardless of execution quality. Ritual positioning is not a permanent advantage. It is a structural advantage that must be earned repeatedly through execution.

The surprising part

The non-obvious finding across the packets is that ritual brands win primarily on friction reduction, not product innovation. Starbucks' eight-quarter decline was not about coffee quality — it was about order wait times and inconsistent execution degrading the value of the morning ritual enough that consumers temporarily abandoned it. Back to Starbucks was a labor and operations program. Chewy's Autoship advantage is account management and replenishment convenience, not pet-food formulation. Dutch Bros' moat is drive-thru speed and Dutch Rewards app participation, not beverage uniqueness. Signet's resilience depends on assortment architecture, pricing tiers, and brand mediation rather than diamond quality. The implication: a ritual incumbent can lose its position not to a product innovator but to any competitor that makes the ritual marginally easier to complete elsewhere. Friction reduction is both the moat and the vulnerability.

Why it matters

When brands embed themselves inside the rituals through which people mark daily identity and life transitions — pet parenthood, morning routine, oral health, engagement, anniversary — they acquire cultural authority over how those rituals are performed and at what cost. Chewy's Autoship model ties pet-parent identity to a proprietary replenishment platform. Starbucks' stored-value and rewards system makes the morning-coffee ritual financially sticky through pre-commitment. Signet's brand portfolio mediates how couples mark engagement and marriage. This is not benign category leadership. It is control over the occasions through which people signal who they are and what they value — and that control generates pricing power precisely because the consumer has decided in advance that the category is non-negotiable.

Post-2022 rate compression sorted consumer spending into two buckets — rituals tied to daily identity or life milestones held price-inelastic and volume-stable, while aspirational and hedonic categories absorbed the cuts — and the first clean cross-section of that sorting is visible in 2025 and early 2026 filings from Chewy, Colgate, Starbucks, Dutch Bros, and Signet.

The case against this trend

What would falsify it: The trend is falsified if companies positioned as ritual categories (1) experience sustained volume declines during macro compression, contradicting the price-inelasticity claim; (2) lose market share to non-ritual competitors without operational failure on their part; (3) show that demographic drift or structural occasion change (e.g., fewer engagements, less pet adoption, declining coffee consumption) causes lasting contractions despite strong execution; or (4) demonstrate that private-label, store-brand, or substitute-category competition captures ritual behavior without significant brand or execution advantage.

Companies that don't fit:

  • Signet Jewelers Limited — Q1 FY27 same-store sales growth of only 1.8% masks deeper category weakness: annual filing explicitly flags 'shifts away from engagements and weddings' and 'trends toward experiential purchases such as travel' as structural competitive headwinds. Bridal is not growing robustly — it is holding against a headwind. Diamond costs, tariff pressure, and inventory discipline are central to the story, meaning the business is defending margin more than volume. This is resilience, not growth, which suggests ritual positioning may be floor-setting behavior rather than genuine insulation.
  • Starbucks Corporation — Eight quarters of comparable transaction decline (FY2024 through Q4 2025) proves the ritual broke when execution failed. This is not ritual strength; it is ritual fragility masked by recent recovery. Consumers did not need coffee less — they switched to McDonald's, Dunkin', or home brewing en masse when Starbucks service deteriorated. The recovery under 'Back to Starbucks' is operational repair, not ritual resilience. This undermines the thesis that rituals are structurally price-inelastic: they are execution-inelastic only if the incumbent remains competent.
  • Colgate-Palmolive Co. — 1.4% organic growth is weak growth, not strong insulation. The packet explicitly flags private-label as a 'structural competitive risk' in oral care. Walmart was 11% of 2025 net sales, creating channel concentration risk. Colgate's margin defense (61.5% gross margin in Q2 2026) and cash flow strength mask underlying volume pressure — the company is maintaining profitability through pricing and efficiency, not through volume immunity. If oral-care volume growth turned negative despite Colgate's brand strength and category leadership, it would prove that even deeply habitual categories can lose volume when private label or channel shift occurs.
  • Dutch Bros Inc. — Drive-thru beverage ritual is entirely dependent on commute patterns and in-car consumption normalized by the pandemic and gas prices. Gen Z adoption rates for drive-thru culture differ from Millennial norms. Remote work, EV proliferation, and changing coffee-shop culture (rise of third spaces over commute stops) threaten the demographic foundation of Dutch Bros' model. Q2 2026 same-shop transaction growth of 1.7% (vs. 3.4% for company-operated) is decelerating, which could signal margin-protection prioritization over volume growth — a signal of underlying stress masked by AUV expansion.

Honest caveat: The thesis is strongest for daily, low-ticket rituals (morning coffee, oral care, pet replenishment) and weakest for occasional, high-ticket rituals tied to life events (engagements, weddings, anniversaries). Signet's data shows that occasion-driven ritual spending does hold up but in the form of managed decline and margin defense, not genuine insulation. The thesis also cannot distinguish between (1) ritual genuine price-inelasticity and (2) temporary floor-setting during macro compression that will reverse when affordability recovers. Starbucks' eight-quarter decline and subsequent recovery shows that ritual is fragile if execution fails, but it does not prove that ritual is the strongest consumer behavior during compression — it only proves that operational failure breaks any category, ritual or not. The data set does not include a major ritual category that lost category share to private label or substitutes despite strong execution (Colgate flags private-label risk but has not yet lost significant volume). Without that negative proof point, the thesis remains conditional on execution and demographic stability, not structural. Finally, the thesis does not account for the possibility that these companies are growing/holding during a specific 2025-2026 compression window only because they have already shed lower-income customers in prior cycles; if that is true, the 'ritual holding' observed here is survivor bias, not ritual resilience.
