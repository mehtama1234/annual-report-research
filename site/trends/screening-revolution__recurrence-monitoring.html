Trends · The Screening Revolution

Recurrence Monitoring

Post-treatment molecular monitoring has become a distinct market layer. Patients screened for early disease are now monitored continuously through liquid biopsies and genetic testing, creating recurring test volume and information-layer value independent of drug companies.

Natera's Signatera oncology platform, Guardant's blood-based cancer monitoring, and Quest's expanding molecular portfolio show that post-treatment surveillance is becoming standard of care. Natera's FY2025 oncology tests grew 51.6%, Q2 2026 oncology tests grew 57.2%. This is not one-time screening; it is longitudinal molecular monitoring paid by payers as part of cancer care. The value is not in drug approval or treatment volume; it is in information—detecting recurrence earlier through liquid biopsies, informing treatment decisions, and enabling closer patient monitoring.

The mechanism

The screening-revolution operates through three interlocking mechanics. First, clinical evidence and guideline adoption: Natera's 2024-2025 data readouts for Signatera (early cancer detection and recurrence monitoring) triggered guideline shifts in the American Society of Clinical Oncology and National Comprehensive Cancer Network recommendations. Payers explicitly referenced these guidelines when expanding reimbursement. This is not marketing persuasion; it is evidence-based coverage policy. The ASCO guideline timeline matters: once circulating tumor DNA monitoring was recommended, payer coverage followed within 6-12 months, not years. Second, test-volume multiplication mechanics: early cancer screening identifies patients for monitoring; post-treatment surveillance requires quarterly or semi-annual tests for 2-5 years depending on cancer stage. A single patient who tests positive on screening becomes 8-20 recurring tests over the monitoring window. Natera's oncology test growth (+51.6% FY2025, +57.2% Q2 2026) outpaces total test growth (+24.9% FY2025, +22.4% Q2 2026) precisely because oncology represents this recurring layer. Third, information-layer monetization independent of drug makers: Signatera detects recurrence whether the patient received Keytruda, Opdivo, or any other immunotherapy. The test creates optionality—it can inform switch decisions or escalation—without being locked to a single drug. This inverts the old diagnostics model where a test was bundled with one drug's approval. The modern pattern is: develop a molecular test, accumulate evidence, win guideline inclusion, secure payer coverage, and capture recurring volume regardless of treatment choice.

The adoption speed reveals the binding constraint. Natera raised guidance twice in the first half of 2026, signaling that adoption exceeded internal expectations. The company is not supply-constrained on testing capacity; it is execution-constrained on lab automation, payer coverage expansion into international markets, and clinical-evidence generation for newer indications. That constraint pattern—evidence and coverage pace, not volume—is the hallmark of a frontier market shifting to standard of care.

The reimbursement tension is material. Payers cover these tests because clinical evidence justifies cost. If future studies show that monitoring does not reduce overall treatment cost (because recurrence detection does not prevent metastatic disease), or if too many low-risk patients are screened and monitored unnecessarily, payers will restrict utilization or demand outcomes-based pricing. Natera's business model depends on evidence durability and clinical-guideline entrenchment. If guideline recommendations weaken or payer pushback on utilization occurs, the recurring model hits a ceiling.

Why now

Three facts aligned simultaneously. First, liquid-biopsy science passed a maturity threshold: Natera's Signatera and Guardant's platforms can now detect circulating tumor DNA at clinically actionable sensitivity and specificity, enabling reliable tests at scale. Second, clinical guideline bodies published recommendations for post-treatment monitoring: ASCO and NCCN now recommend ctDNA monitoring for select early-stage cancers and all intermediate/high-risk patients, formalizing surveillance as standard of care rather than research. Third, Medicare and commercial payers explicitly tied coverage to these guidelines, starting in 2023-2024, accelerating through 2025-2026. The reimbursement window opened because the evidence was strong enough and payers needed a way to manage patient outcomes in an era of immunotherapy where recurrence risk is higher than in older chemotherapy regimens. This is not anticipation; it is response to clinical fact.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Payers (Medicare, commercial insurers, employers) who now budget recurring molecular tests as standard oncology spend; patients who face copays and coinsurance if coverage is incomplete; healthcare systems that must order, track, and act on recurring test results over multi-year monitoring windows.
Who profits
Molecular diagnostics platforms (Natera, Guardant Health, Invitae) that capture recurring test volume, higher average selling prices as assays mature, and information-layer revenue independent of drug makers. Centralized lab operators that scale infrastructure to handle volume multiplication. National lab networks (Quest Diagnostics, LabCorp) that can absorb oncology testing into existing logistics and payer relationships.

The tension

The tension is between test expansion and clinical utility. As screening detects more cancers and monitoring volumes grow, payers may demand outcomes-based pricing or utilization caps if monitoring does not reduce overall metastatic disease incidence or treatment cost. Overuse of monitoring in low-risk patients creates reimbursement cliff risk. Additionally, competition from imaging (MRI, CT, PET surveillance) creates a parallel pricing pressure. If newer imaging modalities prove equivalent or superior for recurrence detection, payer preference may shift and volume growth stalls. Natera's recurring model depends on monitoring remaining clinically indispensable and evidence-based; any weakening of guideline recommendations or payer coverage tightening creates cliff risk.

The surprising part

The surprising element is how complete the transition from one-time confirmatory testing to continuous monitoring has been, and how quickly payers accepted the cost and complexity. Ten years ago, a cancer diagnosis meant one genetic test to confirm and guide initial treatment. Now the model is: initial screening test, then 2-5 years of quarterly or semi-annual monitoring tests, totaling 8-20 tests per patient. That multiplies test volume by an order of magnitude compared to screening alone. The surprise is not that monitoring helps clinically—that is expected—but that payers standardized the practice so rapidly once evidence appeared. Payers typically move slowly on new technology adoption. Here, the speed of guideline-to-coverage adoption (6-12 months) and the acceptance of recurring costs suggests either genuine clinical urgency or very strong reimbursement pressure from oncologists.

Why it matters

An economy where cancer surveillance depends on continuous molecular testing creates two countervailing pressures. Efficiency gain: monitoring genuinely detects recurrence earlier than imaging, potentially improving outcomes for subset of patients. Cost pressure: every test extracts information rent and creates healthcare cost; testing must be paired with evidence that outcomes improve and overall treatment cost declines. Access inequality is real: patients with insurance coverage and oncologists who order tests benefit from earlier detection; uninsured or under-insured patients do not. The system becomes more dependent on diagnostics companies maintaining evidence quality and payers maintaining coverage equity.
