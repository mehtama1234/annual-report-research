The Screen That Caught Too Much: When the Business Model Rewards Volume, Not Outcomes

Guardant's Shield went from ~38,000 tests in Q4 2025 to ~66,000 in Q2 2026, and the company raised its 2026 growth guidance to 36-38%. Every one of those tests is revenue whether or not it improved a life. The screening revolution's structural flaw is that the people paid per test do not bear the cost of the anxiety, biopsies, and overtreatment that expansion into healthy populations produces.

Exact Sciences grew to $3.25 billion in 2025 revenue and Natera to $2.31 billion, both by expanding screening into asymptomatic, average-risk populations. But these companies are paid per test performed, not per life improved, so the incentive is to maximize volume, frequency, and positive results. Overdiagnosis (catching slow-growing conditions that would never have caused harm) is a real cost in anxiety, unnecessary procedures, and iatrogenic harm, and it is externalized onto patients and payers, not the test makers. The benefits of earlier detection also accrue unequally, to the insured, the digitally literate, and those near health systems that have adopted new protocols.

The mechanism

The incentive is mechanical: screening companies are paid per test ordered. Exact Sciences books revenue when Cologuard is used, Natera when Signatera or Panorama is ordered, Guardant when Shield is run. More people screened means more tests, means more revenue, and the test maker does not bear the downstream cost of a false positive (patient anxiety), overdiagnosis (treatment of a slow-growing cancer that would never have progressed), or iatrogenic harm (a complication from the workup a positive test triggers). Guardant runs $(69.5) million of quarterly free-cash burn specifically to fund Shield adoption, which tells you volume growth, not near-term profit, is the objective.

Overdiagnosis is mathematically unavoidable when you screen large populations for rare conditions with imperfect tests. Multi-cancer and single-cancer blood assays like Exact's Cancerguard and Guardant's Shield are tuned to specificity high enough to be actionable but not so high that they miss cases, so as the screened population grows, the tests will catch many cancers that would have stayed slow-growing or clinically silent. Some would have progressed and killed; many would not. The test maker has no financial incentive to distinguish the two, because both show up as a positive result and both are revenue.

Prenatal screening adds a behavioral dimension. Natera's Panorama NIPT detects trisomy 21 with high sensitivity and is now routine in many pregnancies, but that same sensitivity catches chromosomal anomalies that would end in miscarriage or very short life expectancy, cases some parents would not act on, and cases that impose an enormous decision-making burden. Natera enhanced Panorama in 2026 and its oncology mix grew fastest, but the test maker profits from the order regardless of whether the result improved an outcome, and the average selling price improvements management cites come from mix, not from proving mortality reduction.

The inequality layer is structural, not incidental. Screening expansion reaches the insured, the connected, and those near systems that have adopted new protocols; Guardant's own Q1 2026 wins were UnitedHealth coverage and guideline inclusion, both of which advantage the already-covered. Uninsured and low-income populations do not access Exact, Natera, or Guardant tests at the same rate, so the benefit of earlier detection (when real) skews to higher-income groups, while the broader health system absorbs the cost of managing overdiagnosed cases and the anxiety-driven care-seeking that follows positive tests across all segments.

Why now

The inflection is a specific 2025-2026 convergence of coverage, guidelines, and consumer channel, and the cleanest date-stamped signal is payer acceptance moving in lockstep with volume. Medicare coverage expanded for Natera's Prospera and Exact's Oncodetect; Guardant's Shield won UnitedHealth coverage and guideline inclusion by Q1 2026, and its per-quarter test count roughly doubled from ~38,000 in Q4 2025 to ~66,000 in Q2 2026. On the consumer side, Guardant ran direct-to-consumer and influencer campaigns to drive colorectal screening awareness, and Exact struck a Quest Diagnostics collection partnership to widen patient access. Institutional consolidation validated the category: Exact's Abbott acquisition closed on March 23, 2026. What tips this from a snapshot into a trend is the simultaneity, because consumer adoption, guideline and coverage acceptance, and corporate consolidation all moved in the same eighteen months, and the companies are still trading current profitability for reach (Guardant burning $(69.5)M a quarter to expand Shield). The honest caveat is that population-scale mortality-reduction evidence has not moved as fast as the volume, which is precisely the gap this tension is about.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Patients pay through anxiety, unnecessary biopsies, unnecessary surgery, and complications from procedures done to investigate positive screens. A woman with an abnormal Panorama result may undergo amniocentesis, which carries its own miscarriage risk, for an anomaly that would have self-resolved. A person with a positive Shield screen may face a diagnostic workup for a cancer that would never have progressed. Health systems pay for the workup and follow-up on positive screens that turn out false-positive or indolent. Payers absorb the diagnostic and treatment cost of overdiagnosed conditions, even as they expand coverage that drives the volume. Workers lose productivity to anxiety and unnecessary procedures.
Who profits
The test makers profit from every test ordered, regardless of outcome impact. Exact Sciences, Natera, and Guardant benefit from larger screened populations and higher frequency, and the growth rates (37.7% for Natera in Q2 2026, 36-38% guided for Guardant) are driven by volume and mix, not by demonstrated outcome improvement or better specificity. Abbott, having closed the Exact acquisition in March 2026, captures the screening franchise at institutional scale. Physicians benefit from simplified decision-making, because the test tells them what to do next, even when the underlying decision tree is flawed. Digital health platforms and consumer channels benefit from patient engagement and data capture around results.

The tension

The first tension is that screening companies cannot openly acknowledge overdiagnosis without undermining their own value proposition, so investor communications emphasize volume and coverage wins while the overdiagnosis rate goes undisclosed. The second is regulatory: the pathway for laboratory-developed tests is permissive, and once a test is in clinical use, outcome tracking is weak, with little infrastructure to measure whether population-scale screening reduces mortality or merely captures more disease. A third is that guidelines have incorporated these tests (Guardant cites guideline inclusion for Shield; NIPT is now routine) even without clear population-level mortality-reduction data, and the guidelines do not state the overdiagnosis rate or the decision-making burden. The fourth is financial: Guardant is burning $(69.5)M a quarter to expand Shield, a bet that only pays if scaled volume eventually proves both operating leverage and clinical value, and the second has not been established as fast as the first.

The surprising part

The striking shift is how openly these companies now market screening to asymptomatic, average-risk people rather than to symptomatic or high-risk patients. Guardant's Shield is promoted as routine colorectal screening for average-risk adults, backed by direct-to-consumer and influencer campaigns; Natera's Panorama is routine prenatal screening; Exact's Cancerguard is marketed as multi-cancer early detection for the general adult population. That is a departure from the traditional model of screening only high-risk or symptomatic groups, justified by 'earlier is better,' even though the population-scale outcome evidence is weaker than the 36-38% growth rates suggest. The second surprise is the silence around overdiagnosis: the medical literature reports overdiagnosis rates for multi-cancer screens, but the companies do not disclose them in investor materials and payers expanding coverage do not demand them, so the fastest-growing part of diagnostics is scaling on a cost that nobody in the transaction is accountable for.

Why it matters

The screening revolution threatens to medicalize normal aging and risk. If every adult undergoes routine multi-cancer screening, a predictable share will get positive results that trigger workups, many of them false positives or indolent conditions, producing a cascade of anxiety, procedures, and spending that does not clearly extend or improve life. For pregnant women, the proliferation of screening options increases decision-making burden and anxiety even when outcomes are good. The inequality dimension is profound: high-income, well-insured populations benefit when real cancers are caught early, while lower-income populations get less access yet the system still bears the cost when overdiagnosis or anxiety-driven care-seeking occurs. And the normalization of preventive testing shifts medical identity, so more people come to see themselves as potentially ill or at-risk even when a screening test did nothing for their individual outcome. A society that detects cancer early and treats it before it metastasizes is genuinely better off, but this revolution is arriving unevenly, and the business model is not designed to solve that.

When test makers are paid per test and not per outcome, screening expansion into healthy populations inevitably manufactures overdiagnosis whose costs land on patients and health systems, not on the shareholders funding the growth.
