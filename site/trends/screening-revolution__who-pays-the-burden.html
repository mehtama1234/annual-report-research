Trends · The Screening Revolution

Medicare Absorbs the Bill — and So Does the Health System That Has to Act on Every Positive

Diagnostic companies profit from generating more screening results, but the healthcare system absorbs the cost of acting on every positive result.

The screening revolution has two invisible cost centers. Medicare and Medicaid bear the direct testing cost as coverage expands. Healthcare systems—clinics, oncologists, surgeons—absorb the downstream burden: every positive screening result requires confirmatory workup, anxiety management, follow-up visits, and sometimes treatment for conditions that might never have caused clinical harm. This page traces who actually pays, in money and in capacity, for a system designed to screen more and act on every signal.

The mechanism

Diagnostic companies (Labcorp, Quest Diagnostics, Natera, Guardant Health) have built revenue models around increasing test volume and expanding test panels. Natera's multi-cancer early detection tests and Exact Sciences' colorectal-cancer screening tests are generating positive top-line growth by converting normal screening into algorithmic testing at scale. When Medicare or Medicaid begin covering a new test, volume often accelerates because the test is now subsidized. The diagnostic company's revenue grows because the market expands and is now government-funded.

But the healthcare system bears the downstream cost. Every positive (or falsely positive) screening result triggers a cascade of follow-up appointments, confirmatory tests, specialist referrals, anxiety visits, and sometimes unnecessary treatments. A multi-cancer early detection test might flag a potential signal that requires a PET scan, colonoscopy, and eventually shows benign findings—but the patient underwent $5,000-10,000 of imaging and specialist time. The clinic and hospital that performs those follow-ups absorb the cost in staff time, imaging resources, and opportunity cost (full schedules reduce availability for truly symptomatic patients). Medicare and Medicaid pay the direct testing cost but also pay for all the follow-up, which multiplies the system cost of each test.

The asymmetry is structural: the diagnostic company profits from test volume and sees growing revenue as testing expands. The healthcare system (clinic, hospital, oncologist) absorbs the cost of managing positive results and anxious patients, but has no direct profit center from testing. The payer (Medicare/Medicaid) covers both the test and the follow-up but has limited visibility into the downstream costs and harms of false-positive cascades. This creates a system optimized for generating test volume, not for clinical outcomes.

Why now

Multi-cancer early detection and liquid biopsy technologies have finally demonstrated sufficient specificity to gain FDA clearance and insurance coverage. Regulatory approval is now unlocking government payer coverage, which is expanding the addressable market from self-pay and employer-insured to the full Medicare and Medicaid base. Simultaneously, aging populations in developed countries are driving more screening (population grows, prevalence rises), and regulatory review is accelerating (FDA has moved faster on some cancer diagnostics). The combination of better technology, payer coverage, and population aging is creating rapid testing volume growth.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Medicare and Medicaid through direct testing costs and follow-up care; healthcare systems (hospitals, clinics, oncologists) through staff time, imaging resources, and opportunity costs of managing follow-up workups; patients through time, anxiety, co-pays for follow-up visits, and sometimes unnecessary treatments or procedures; insurance companies through increased claims volume from follow-up testing and procedures
Who profits
Diagnostic companies (Labcorp, Quest, Natera, Guardant) through increased testing volume and higher revenue per patient as test panels expand; technology vendors who sell sequencing equipment and software to labs; some specialists (oncologists, surgeons) who benefit from increased referrals and procedures tied to positive screening results

The tension

The system has no mechanism to limit unnecessary follow-ups or hold diagnostic companies accountable for false positives. If healthcare systems and payers recognize the cost burden and push back, they may demand pricing changes, test-utilization limits, or clinical-outcome measures that reduce diagnostic company revenue. Alternatively, if screening continues to expand without cost controls, payers will face escalating bill for follow-up care tied to earlier detection. Additionally, the opioid litigation precedent suggests that diagnostic companies could eventually face class-action suits from patients harmed by unnecessary follow-up procedures tied to false-positive results. Regulatory intervention requiring outcome tracking and limiting coverage of low-specificity tests could also constrain growth.

The surprising part

The most counterintuitive insight is that the diagnostic companies have almost zero financial accountability for the downstream costs they generate. A test that screens 1M patients, generates 50,000 flagged results, of which 45,000 are false positives, still generates revenue for the diagnostic company. The 45,000 false positives cost the healthcare system $2-3B in follow-up workups, but the diagnostic company keeps its test revenue. The financial incentives are completely misaligned with clinical outcomes.

Why it matters

If screening expands without outcome accountability, resource allocation becomes skewed—oncologists and surgical oncologists spend time on early-detection false positives instead of treating symptomatic patients with real disease. This delays care for the sickest patients and erodes healthcare system capacity for core missions. For patients, rapid screening expansion creates anxiety and unnecessary procedures that cause harm and reduce quality of life. Regulatory intervention that requires outcome tracking and holds diagnostic companies accountable for false positives could improve outcomes, but might reduce access to legitimate screening tests if profitability declines and companies exit markets.

Diagnostic companies profit from test volume while healthcare systems absorb the hidden cost of acting on every positive result, creating a system designed to maximize screening at any clinical cost.
