Trends · The Screening Revolution

Diagnostic Companies Profit From Test Volume; Healthcare Systems Absorb Follow-Up Costs

Multi-cancer early-detection and liquid-biopsy tests are generating revenue through expanded volume. Medicare and Medicaid cover the tests. Healthcare systems absorb downstream cost of acting on every positive—confirmatory workups, anxiety management, follow-up visits, unnecessary treatments.

The screening revolution has two invisible cost centers. Medicare and Medicaid bear direct testing cost as coverage expands. Healthcare systems—clinics, oncologists, surgeons—absorb downstream burden: every positive screening result requires confirmatory workup, anxiety management, follow-up visits, sometimes treatment for conditions that might never have caused clinical harm. This creates system optimized for generating test volume, not for clinical outcomes. Diagnostic companies profit from test volume and see growing revenue as testing expands. Healthcare system absorbs cost of managing positive results and anxious patients but has no direct profit center from testing. Payer (Medicare/Medicaid) covers both test and follow-up but has limited visibility into downstream costs and harms of false-positive cascades.

The mechanism

Diagnostic companies have built revenue models around increasing test volume and expanding test panels. Natera's multi-cancer early detection tests and Exact Sciences' colorectal-cancer screening tests generate positive top-line growth by converting normal screening into algorithmic testing at scale. Exact Sciences now has 12.78M subscribers (analogous to media subscription model) and reported FY2025 revenue $3.25B. When Medicare or Medicaid begin covering new test, volume often accelerates because test is now subsidized. Diagnostic company's revenue grows because market expands and is now government-funded. Natera's Q2 2026 oncology tests grew 57.2% year-over-year to 296,700 tests with total tests at 1.04M (up 22.4% YoY), showing volume acceleration from Medicare coverage expansion.

But healthcare system bears downstream cost. Every positive (or falsely positive) screening result triggers cascade: follow-up appointments, confirmatory tests, specialist referrals, anxiety visits, sometimes unnecessary treatments. Multi-cancer early detection test might flag potential signal requiring PET scan, colonoscopy, eventually showing benign findings. Patient underwent $5,000-10,000 imaging and specialist time. Clinic and hospital performing follow-ups absorb cost in staff time, imaging resources, opportunity cost (full schedules reduce availability for symptomatic patients). Medicare and Medicaid pay direct testing cost but also pay for all follow-up, which multiplies system cost of each test. Financial asymmetry is structural: diagnostic company profits from test volume with no accountability for follow-up costs. Healthcare system (clinic, hospital, oncologist) absorbs follow-up costs but has no direct profit center from testing.

Labcorp's Q2 2026 revenue growth to $3.73B (up 6% YoY) proves the diagnostic-company model is working. But that growth is driven by volume increases that are creating downstream follow-up cascades in healthcare systems. The diagnostic company sees only test revenue. The system cost is invisible in the financial statements. Payer (Medicare/Medicaid) covers both test and follow-up but has limited visibility into the rate of false positives or the magnitude of unnecessary follow-ups. This creates system designed to maximize screening volume at any clinical cost.

Why now

Multi-cancer early-detection and liquid-biopsy technologies finally demonstrated sufficient specificity to gain FDA clearance and insurance coverage starting 2024-2025. Regulatory approval unlocked government payer coverage, expanding addressable market from self-pay and employer-insured to full Medicare and Medicaid base. Simultaneously, aging populations in developed countries driving more screening (population grows, prevalence rises), and regulatory review accelerating. FDA moved faster on cancer diagnostics in 2025-2026. Combination of better technology, payer coverage expansion, and population aging creating rapid testing volume growth. Exact Sciences' 12.78M subscribers and Natera's 57.2% YoY oncology growth in Q2 2026 reflect convergence of improved technology, payer acceptance, and consumer participation in screening.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Medicare and Medicaid through direct testing costs and follow-up care. Healthcare systems (hospitals, clinics, oncologists) through staff time, imaging resources, opportunity costs of managing follow-up workups. Patients through time, anxiety, co-pays for follow-up visits, sometimes unnecessary treatments or procedures. Insurance companies through increased claims volume from follow-up testing and procedures resulting from positive screening results.
Who profits
Diagnostic companies (Labcorp, Quest, Natera, Exact Sciences) through increased testing volume and higher revenue per patient as test panels expand. Technology vendors selling sequencing equipment and software to labs. Some specialists (oncologists, surgeons) benefiting from increased referrals and procedures tied to positive screening results.

The tension

System has no mechanism to limit unnecessary follow-ups or hold diagnostic companies accountable for false positives. If healthcare systems and payers recognize cost burden and push back, they may demand pricing changes, test-utilization limits, or clinical-outcome measures reducing diagnostic company revenue. Alternatively, if screening continues expanding without cost controls, payers face escalating bill for follow-up care tied to earlier detection. Diagnostic companies could eventually face class-action suits from patients harmed by unnecessary follow-up procedures tied to false-positive results. Regulatory intervention requiring outcome tracking and limiting coverage of low-specificity tests could constrain growth.

The surprising part

Most counterintuitive insight: diagnostic companies have almost zero financial accountability for downstream costs they generate. Test that screens 1M patients, generates 50,000 flagged results, of which 45,000 are false positives, still generates revenue for diagnostic company. Those 45,000 false positives cost healthcare system $2-3B in follow-up workups. Diagnostic company keeps test revenue. Financial incentives completely misaligned with clinical outcomes. Natera's 57.2% YoY oncology growth demonstrates volume acceleration without corresponding disclosure of false-positive rates or follow-up outcomes.

Why it matters

If screening expands without outcome accountability, resource allocation becomes skewed. Oncologists and surgical oncologists spend time on early-detection false positives instead of treating symptomatic patients with real disease. This delays care for sickest patients and erodes healthcare system capacity for core missions. For patients, rapid screening expansion creates anxiety and unnecessary procedures causing harm and reducing quality of life. Regulatory intervention requiring outcome tracking and holding diagnostic companies accountable for false positives could improve outcomes but might reduce access to legitimate screening tests if profitability declines and companies exit markets.

Diagnostic companies profit from test volume while healthcare systems absorb hidden cost of acting on every positive result, creating system designed to maximize screening at any clinical cost.
