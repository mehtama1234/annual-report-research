The Value Map: Where Money Flows When Medicine Screens More People

In a screening revolution that simultaneously grows volume and compresses price per test, profits cluster with whoever owns the front door—demand formation and patient access—while throughput operators capture only recurring margin.

The screening revolution distributes profits unevenly based on position in the pathway. Exact Sciences ($3.25B revenue 2025, screening $695M Q4) and Guardant Health ($335M Q2 2026 revenue with Shield screening $52.9M) own the front door—patient access, physician ordering, demand formation—and command higher valuations and more pricing power. Labcorp ($3.73B Q2 revenue, 6% growth) and Quest Diagnostics ($3.04B Q2 revenue, 10.2% growth) process the throughput at scale but compete on margin compression. Abbott ($12.593B Q2 sales, Diagnostics $3.092B) captures category consolidation by combining diagnostics with devices and pharmaceutical breadth. Position in the pathway determines who profits most.

The mechanism

The screening pathway has three distinct layers: (1) Demand formation and patient access—who owns the relationship and how people enter the testing system; (2) Test processing and throughput—who runs the labs and handles volume; (3) Category consolidation—who bundles diagnostics with devices, pharmaceuticals, or adjacent services. Exact Sciences owns layer 1. Its Cologuard, Cologuard Plus, Oncodetect, and Cancerguard create their own demand through direct-to-consumer marketing, physician relationships, and payer coverage agreements. The company does not wait for patients to request testing; it drives awareness and conversion. That front-door ownership commands premium valuations because it controls the flow. Guardant Health is moving toward the same position with Shield, its colorectal cancer screening test, by partnering with Quest for distribution, pursuing guideline inclusion, and securing UnitedHealth coverage. Labcorp and Quest sit in layer 2: they process volume at scale. Labcorp's 15.8% adjusted operating margin (Q2 2026) and Quest's double-digit organic growth show throughput business logic—volume multiplied by margin per test. But neither controls demand formation. Patients do not choose to use Labcorp because Labcorp is more compelling; they use Labcorp because their physician orders through Labcorp or because an employer uses Labcorp for workplace health screening. Abbott sits across all layers: it owns diagnostics testing systems (layer 2), but also owns chronic-care device franchises (layer 1 demand) and can bundle Exact Sciences into the platform (after the March 2026 merger close).

The value distribution is starkly unequal because front-door control creates pricing power. Exact can raise the price of Cologuard year-over-year while growing volume because the test brand has become synonymous with colorectal screening. Guardant can command premium reimbursement for Shield because payers perceive the test as higher-quality and worth covering. Labcorp and Quest compete on volume and efficiency, not on brand or premium pricing. They earn steady incremental growth through volume increase, operational leverage, and test-mix improvement (shifting toward higher-reimbursement advanced diagnostics). But their business model is inherently a margin-compression game: as screening volume grows, payers and employers push back on price, driving rates down. Labcorp's margin of 15.8% is respectable, but it is the result of processing billions in tests, not the result of commanding premium pricing. Abbott's advantage is different: it bundles diagnostics into a larger ecosystem where it can sell devices, pharmaceuticals, and monitoring services to the same customer (hospitals, health systems, physicians). That bundling creates stickiness and cross-selling that pure diagnostics companies cannot match.

The inflection point is visible in recent M&A: Abbott acquired Exact Sciences in March 2026, explicitly consolidating layer 1 demand-formation capability into a larger platform. That was not Abbott buying higher throughput; it was Abbott buying front-door control and the ability to drive screening behavior across its customer base. Exact shareholders were paid a premium because the company owned valuable patient relationships and demand formation. Quest is responding by investing in questhealth.com and direct-to-consumer channels, trying to build layer 1 capabilities alongside its layer 2 infrastructure. Labcorp is investing in biopharma services and central-laboratory capability, leaning into the infrastructure advantage rather than competing on demand formation. Guardant is the wildcard: it has venture-scale capital, high growth (36-38% guidance), and positioning as a growth story rather than a mature infrastructure operator. If Guardant can build Shield into a primary colorectal screening platform and expand into other cancers, it can compete with Exact on front-door control. If it cannot, it remains a high-growth but niche precision-oncology company that competes on specialty testing, not on mass-market screening.

Why now

Screening volume is accelerating because guidelines have shifted (colorectal screening now recommended at age 45, down from 50), awareness campaigns are increasing (Guardant and Exact both run direct-to-consumer campaigns), and payers are covering more tests as preventive medicine. At the same time, price pressure is mounting because screening tests are becoming commodified—once one test is proven to work, other tests follow, and payers view them as substitutable. The result is a squeeze: volume growing, price per test compressing. In that environment, whoever owns the front door—whoever can drive ordering volume and patient preference—wins. That is why Exact is worth a premium valuation and why Abbott paid to acquire it. The company that controls demand can sustain margin even as commodities compete on price.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Payers (Medicare, commercial insurers, employers) and ultimately consumers pay for the screening expansion. A patient covered by insurance pays a copay for Cologuard or Guardant Shield, but the bulk cost flows through payers. As screening volume grows and more tests are ordered, total spending on diagnostics rises. Payers negotiate aggressively to keep per-test costs low, but aggregate spending still increases because volume grows faster than prices fall. Employers see higher health-plan costs. Medicare faces higher Part B expenditures for lab tests. Consumers with high-deductible plans face higher out-of-pocket costs if they need follow-up testing or treatment after a positive screen.
Who profits
Exact Sciences and Abbott (post-merger) capture the highest margins through demand control. Guardant Health captures growing market share and premium valuations because it is perceived as a growth company building new screening markets. Labcorp and Quest capture steady operational leverage and share in the absolute growth of testing volume, but face persistent margin compression as payers negotiate and substitutes proliferate. Abbott captures cross-platform value—diagnostics linked to devices and pharmaceuticals mean higher customer switching costs and more opportunities to sell additional services. Shareholders of Exact and Guardant benefit from the premium valuations and growth stories. Shareholders of Labcorp and Quest benefit from stable cash generation but not from premium valuation multiples.

The tension

The model breaks if screening volume slows—either because guideline recommendations reverse, awareness campaigns lose effectiveness, or payers stop covering tests as preventive. It also breaks if the screening pathway becomes purely commodified and price compression accelerates faster than volume can offset it. A third tension: if overscreening and overdiagnosis become publicly visible issues (more positive tests than true cancers, unnecessary anxiety and follow-up procedures), payers and patients may push back on screening adoption rates. The final tension is that everyone is trying to build layer 1 capabilities simultaneously. Quest is building direct-to-consumer channels. Labcorp is trying to embed in health systems and payers. Guardant is expanding beyond oncology into broader screening. Abbott is consolidating both platforms. When everyone competes on the same variable, margins compress.

The surprising part

The surprise is not that screening is growing; it is that front-door control has become more valuable than throughput scale. In an older model, Labcorp and Quest's massive infrastructure—billions of tests processed annually—would be defensible. But in a screening revolution, the company that controls patient demand (Exact, soon Abbott) captures more value than the company that processes volume (Labcorp, Quest). That represents a power shift away from infrastructure operators toward demand-shapers. The second surprise is the speed of consolidation: Abbott did not slowly build screening capabilities; it acquired Exact outright. That signals how important it has become to own the front door rather than wait to process someone else's volume. The third surprise is that pure screening plays (Exact, Guardant) trade at much higher multiples than mature infrastructure operators (Labcorp, Quest) even though Labcorp and Quest have more stable earnings. Market is pricing growth and demand control more than profitability.

Why it matters

This matters because it shapes how screening gets prioritized and who gets screened. If demand-formation companies (Exact, Guardant, Abbott) drive screening behavior through marketing and payer relationships, they will push the tests they own—which is good for early detection but can create overscreening in some populations and underscreening in others. If screening becomes driven by payer and employer logic (cost-effectiveness, coverage decisions), access depends on insurance status rather than medical need. The consolidation into large players (Abbott absorbing Exact) means fewer competing screening platforms and potentially less diversity in what gets screened and how. For society, the stake is whether screening remains driven by medical evidence and public health goals, or increasingly by corporate profit optimization and payer incentives.

In the screening revolution, whoever owns patient demand and testing behavior commands premium value, while throughput operators face persistent margin compression despite processing growing volume.
