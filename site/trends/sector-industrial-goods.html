Sector synthesis

Industrial Goods

Industrial Goods has bifurcated into an AI-infrastructure execution race and a residentially-tied collapse. Contractor backlogs at Quanta ($53.4B), Comfort Systems ($14.06B), and Vertiv ($15.0B) are record-setting—but demand is now completely outpaced by execution capacity. The constraint is craft labor, not orders. Meanwhile, housing-tied equipment distributors and builders are drowning in affordability gravity.

The state of the sector

Industrial Goods in 2025-2026 splits into two structural stories with almost no overlap. The first is AI and grid infrastructure: data-center power distribution, electrical contracting, cooling systems, and fiber buildout have created the largest single backlog buildup in a generation. Quanta's $53.4B total backlog and $33.6B RPO at Q2 2026, Comfort Systems' backlog explosion from $5.99B (18 months prior) to $14.06B at Q2 2026, Vertiv's $15.0B backlog at 2.9x book-to-bill with 252% organic order growth in Q4 2025, and EMCOR's RPO reaching $17.14B all measure the same thing: customers will pay whatever price execution requires because supply is the chokepoint, not demand. Eaton shipped record Q2 2026 sales of $8.531B with 14% organic growth. nVent Electric grew organic revenue 47% in Q2 2026 on data-center liquid cooling. Powell Industries hit 3.0x book-to-bill on switchgear that is a hard chokepoint for both AI data centers and LNG terminals.

The second structural story is grid modernization and electrification: T&D contractors Quanta, MYR Group, and Dycom are converting transmission, distribution, and renewable-interconnection work into multi-year backlogs ($3.16B for MYR at Q2 2026, $11.906B and 2.2x book-to-bill for Dycom in Q1 FY2027). This wave is driven by utilities spending on substation upgrades, transmission capacity, EV load integration, and fiber (BEAD broadband). Utility capex is a policy-driven multi-decade commitment, not cyclical.

The third wave is waste-and-water infrastructure as a pricing moat: Waste Management ($2.937B FCF in FY2025), Republic Services (adjusted EBITDA $5.31B with 50 basis points margin expansion), and Casella (pricing up 4.9% while volumes grew 0.2-0.6%) are all raising prices faster than cost inflation because customers cannot switch—service is route-based and highly regulated. Pricing discipline in the waste sector has no equivalent anywhere else in the economy.

The losing side of the sector is acute. Residential construction is broken: D.R. Horton's cancellation rates at 20% in Q3 FY2026, Lennar at 12.9% incentives with 15.2% gross margin, and Builders FirstSource down 8.8% net sales and down 34.9% adjusted EBITDA in Q2 2026. Equipment distributors tied to housing—and general MRO players unable to differentiate—are bleeding margin. ACCO Brands (net sales down 8.5% in 2025) is a textbook case of a category in irreversible decline.

The distributed execution moat is what matters. Comfort Systems, Quanta, EMCOR, Eaton, and Fastenal ($3.625B Endless Assortment with 15.7% growth) all share the same structural characteristic: they have installed-base relationships, branch density, local labor, or digital-embedding that competitors cannot replicate at speed. Vertiv achieved net cash in Q2 2026 off pure power-and-cooling demand. Sterling Infrastructure hit 20%+ EBITDA margins for the first time in company history by concentrating on mission-critical data-center and semiconductor sites. United Rentals ($16.1B FY2025 revenue, $2.2B FCF) is shifting toward specialty rental (60 new specialty branches in FY2025) because customers prefer to rent than own capital-intensive equipment during multi-year infrastructure ramps.

Sub-trends inside the sector

AI Data Center as Demand Anchor for Power, Cooling, and Switchgear

Hyperscaler data-center construction is the largest single demand signal in U.S. industrial goods. Power distribution, cooling systems, electrical enclosures, switchgear, and site development are all executing against backlogs that dwarf prior cycle peaks. The constraint is not demand but craft-skilled labor and manufacturing throughput. Every day of delay costs customers billions in lost AI compute. Vertiv, Eaton, nVent, Powell Industries, and EMCOR are direct beneficiaries converting this urgency into pricing and execution advantage.

Vertiv Holdings Co, Eaton Corporation plc, nVent Electric plc, Powell Industries Inc., EMCOR Group, Inc.

T&D and Grid Electrification as Sustained Multi-Decade Policy Wave

Utility capex for transmission, distribution, substation, and renewable-interconnection work is running at sustained highs driven by EV load, grid modernization policy (BEAD), data-center power delivery, and transmission bottleneck relief. Quanta's $53.4B total backlog and Dycom's $11.906B backlog both reflect multi-year project visibility. MYR's backlog reached record $3.16B with T&D at $1.27B and customers now extending contract durations to secure scarce labor. This is not cyclical demand; it is policy-driven infrastructure buildout spanning decades.

Quanta Services, Inc., MYR Group Inc., Dycom Industries Inc., Valmont Industries Inc., United Rentals, Inc.

Waste and Water as Route-Based Pricing Moats

Solid waste, hazardous waste, and water-infrastructure distribution share one structural characteristic: customers cannot easily switch because service is route-based, regulated, or tied to local permit rights. Waste Management, Republic Services, and Casella are all maintaining pricing well in excess of cost inflation—Waste Management raised FCF from $2.317B to $2.937B on pricing discipline alone, and Casella achieved 4.9% pricing growth for the fifth consecutive year while volumes grew only 0.2-0.6%. Route density and local permit access are genuine competitive moats that lock in pricing power across economic cycles. Addition of RNG and recycling assets layers sustainability narrative premiums onto already captive demand.

Waste Management, Inc., Republic Services, Inc., Casella Waste Systems Inc., Clean Harbors Inc., Allied Waste Industries

Industrial Distribution Bifurcating on Execution Depth

MRO and industrial distribution is cleaving sharply. Winners are those embedding themselves in customer workflows—Fastenal's digital footprint reached 61.4% of 2025 sales through onsite staffing and FMI vending; Grainger's Endless Assortment generated $3.625B with 15.7% growth capturing price-transparency demand; WESCO's data-center sales hit $4.3B in 2025, up ~50%, converting from spot-order distribution into project-supply-chain with forward backlog. Losers are generalist MRO players (MSC Industrial) facing customer mix shift toward larger accounts and government programs where margins compress even as volume grows. Fastenal is gaining large-customer share through embedded density; MSC is losing gross margin to that same shift.

Fastenal Company, W.W. Grainger, Inc., WESCO International Inc., MSC Industrial Direct Co., Inc., Applied Industrial Technologies Inc.

Specialty Rental Outpacing Ownership as Infrastructure Model Shift

United Rentals ($16.1B FY2025 revenue, $2.2B FCF) and Herc Holdings are recording a secular shift away from equipment ownership toward rental. Large infrastructure projects prefer rental for capital flexibility; reshoring manufacturers avoid balance-sheet intensity. Specialty rental is outpacing general rental inside both companies—United Rentals opened 60 new specialty branches in FY2025 alone. Fleet productivity at United Rentals reached +3.4% in Q2 2026. This is not a cyclical uptick but a structural model shift driven by multi-year project visibility and capital constraints.

United Rentals, Inc., Herc Holdings Inc., McGrath RentCorp

Residential Construction Decoupled from Infrastructure Boom

The one segment pulling in the opposite direction of the sector's dominant trends is new residential construction. D.R. Horton's cancellation rates at 20% in Q3 FY2026, Lennar at 12.9% incentives with 15.2% gross margin, Builders FirstSource down 8.8% net sales with 34.9% EBITDA decline in Q2 2026—housing affordability has completely decoupled residential construction from the infrastructure and industrial investment boom. Equipment tied to housing (builders' FirstSource, Pool Corp) and legacy categories in secular decline (ACCO Brands down 8.5% in 2025) are real weights that barely register against the infrastructure tailwind.

D.R. Horton, Inc., Lennar Corporation, Builders FirstSource, Inc., ACCO Brands Corporation, Pool Corp

Standout companies

This is the field-execution operator that converts grid modernization, data-center load growth, and electrification policy into wire in the ground. Multi-year visibility and execution depth on infrastructure that cannot be delayed make Quanta's backlog durability a sector bell-weather.

This proves that skilled-trade execution capacity, not demand, is the binding constraint in this cycle. Comfort's same-store backlog of $13.70B shows organic growth, not M&A. Labor depth and project management are now more valuable than capital or customer willingness to pay.
  • Vertiv Holdings Co — Most direct bridge from AI demand to physical infrastructure: $15.0B backlog at 2.9x book-to-bill achieved net cash position by Q2 2026. 252% organic order growth in Q4 2025 shows single-quarter surge driven entirely by hyperscaler data-center power and cooling. Vertiv is proving that supply-constrained beneficiaries of the AI buildout can flip from capital-intensive to cash-generative.
  • nVent Electric plc — Fastest-turning beneficiary of the AI cooling infrastructure wave: 47% organic growth in Q2 2026 on liquid cooling and data-center electrical enclosures. $2.6B backlog with new products contributing 30%+ of growth shows product innovation capturing pricing power. This is the direct play on thermal infrastructure as an AI bottleneck.
  • Powell Industries Inc. — Book-to-bill at 3.0x in Q3 FY2026 on custom medium-voltage switchgear—a hard chokepoint component for both AI data-center power distribution and Gulf Coast LNG terminals. $2.4B backlog at 30.6% gross margins proves pricing power when you have the only viable option. Niche component supplier with structural pricing advantage.
  • Sterling Infrastructure, Inc. — Achieved 20%+ EBITDA margins for the first time in company history in FY2025 by concentrating 92% of E-Infrastructure backlog on mission-critical data-center and semiconductor sites while deliberately exiting lower-margin transportation work.   • Waste Management, Inc. — Free cash flow of $2.937B in FY2025, up from $2.317B, on pricing discipline raising rates 5%+ annually while volumes grew only 0.2-0.6%. Q2 2026 daily sales up 14.7% driven by share gains with larger customers. The tension

The sector's biggest fault line is labor and execution capacity. Every high-performing company—Comfort Systems, Quanta, Dycom, EMCOR, MYR Group—describes extraordinary backlogs that cannot be converted faster because craft-skilled workers are genuinely scarce. This creates two compounding risks: (1) customers extend contract durations and lock in capacity to secure execution, raising switching costs but also concentrating customer exposure and creating revenue timing risk; and (2) fixed-price contracts—where a backlog book is a string of bets on future labor and materials costs—could produce earnings air pockets even as headline backlog numbers grow. Tariff volatility (Eaton, Valmont, MSC all flagged tariff friction; Generac's $71M pretax tariff refund temporarily boosted Q2 2026 margins) adds cost uncertainty inside a labor-constrained environment. The question hanging over every backlog: at what margins will all this locked-in work convert as wage inflation and supply-chain volatility compound?

Who wins · who loses

Winners are execution platforms with craft-skilled depth, proprietary distribution moats, or infrastructure-criticality pricing power: Vertiv, Eaton, Quanta, Comfort Systems, EMCOR, Waste Management, Republic Services, Fastenal, Powell Industries, and specialty rental operators (United Rentals, Herc). These companies are either irreplaceable in the physical infrastructure supply chain or structurally embedded in customer workflows in ways that insulate pricing. Losers are clear: Builders FirstSource and residential building-materials distributors tied to an unaffordable housing market; ACCO Brands pivoting out of a category in secular decline; generalist MRO distributors (MSC Industrial) losing gross margin to large-customer mix shift without yet capturing enough digital-embedding premium to offset it. Mid-tier contractors without the scale to win mega-projects or the labor depth to execute complex work are being squeezed out of the most lucrative work in the cycle.

Outlook

The sector is entering a sustained execution phase rather than a demand-generation phase. Order books at the infrastructure-exposed companies are already full; the next 12-24 months will be determined by who can actually convert backlog into revenue and cash without cost overruns or labor shortfalls. AI infrastructure buildout is not moderating—hyperscaler capex guidance remains elevated, and the physical infrastructure required (power, cooling, fiber, site development) has multi-year lead times that sustain contractor demand even if hyperscaler investment plateaus. Grid modernization and T&D buildout driven by utility capex span decades, not quarters. Waste pricing power is structural, not cyclical. Residential construction is a real drag but is completely offset by the infrastructure and industrial investment boom. The binding resource in the next 24 months is craft-skilled labor, not the order book. Companies best positioned are those that have visibility into labor availability (whether through M&A to acquire crews, aggressive wage investment, or automation), can price fixed-price contract risk carefully, and deploy capital into manufacturing capacity or branch density that locks in the next wave of backlog. Fastenal's workflow embedding, United Rentals' specialty branch expansion, Comfort's labor reputation, and Quanta's geographic execution depth are the templates for how to win in this environment.

Industrial Goods is a tale of two sectors: an AI-and-grid infrastructure execution race constrained only by craft labor and willing to pay unlimited prices, and a residential-construction collapse offset by near-zero economic weight—the distance between them is growing.
