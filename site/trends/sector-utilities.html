Sector synthesis

Utilities

Utilities are executing the largest capital buildout in decades — data-center load, climate hardening, aging-pipe replacement — but regulatory approval gates every dollar of earnings. The fault lines are between who locked in cost recovery before politics shifted and who still needs approvals they may never get.

The state of the sector

The utilities sector has fractured into three distinct capital stories with radically different risk profiles. First: data-center-anchored electric utilities. Alliant Energy has 3.4 GW of contracted data-center demand (Q1 2026) — Google, Meta, QTS — justifying a $13.4B capital plan for 2026-2029 and projecting 60% system peak-load growth by 2031. This is contracted, not speculative. The second story: climate-driven grid hardening in single high-stress jurisdictions. Edison International is spending $38B-$41B from 2026-2030 not to grow load but to harden grid infrastructure against wildfire ignition risk in California, where the 2025 Eaton Fire created material balance-sheet exposure and where the entire 5-7% core EPS CAGR depends on Wildfire Fund reimbursement mechanics, securitization capacity, and regulatory cost-recovery approval that can be withdrawn. The third story: the quiet compounders grinding out 5-9% earnings growth via regulatory rate-base expansion. Atmos Energy is doubling rate base from $21.4B (FY2025) to ~$42B (FY2030) through $26B of capital, 85%+ safety and reliability focused. American Water Works is adding 40,000 customer connections annually (2025: 40k added; $582M acquisitions under agreement at year-end for 104k more connections) while maintaining residential bills below 1% of median household income through rate-case execution and infrastructure surcharges.

All three stories depend entirely on regulatory architecture. Alliant has advance-ratemaking tools and Iowa base-rate stability through the decade. Atmos tracks annualized regulatory outcomes ($355M Q3 FY2026 as the material operating variable). American Water converts acquired systems into earnings via rate-case approval and infrastructure surcharges. Edison's entire value hinges on California's Wildfire Fund and securitization framework holding. When regulatory relationships work — when rate designs permit cost recovery and regulators approve capital plans in advance — the utilities compound reliably at 5-9% EPS. When they stall — as in California where wildfire attribution questions created cost-recovery uncertainty, or in gas jurisdictions where transition pressure is starting to creep into regulatory vocabulary — the same monopoly structure becomes a liability trap.

Two unresolved structural tensions frame the sector's medium-term risk. First: energy transition. Gas utilities (Atmos, ONE Gas) have durable near-term moat — weather-normalization mechanisms made ONE Gas grow earnings in Q2 2026 despite weather 42% warmer than normal — but face a multi-decade question about whether new gas distribution infrastructure remains a regulated growth asset or becomes stranded cost. Water utilities (American Water, Essential Utilities) face PFAS/PFOS treatment mandates that are raising capital requirements faster than rate cases can absorb, with consolidation pipelines dependent on municipal goodwill that can evaporate. Second: customer concentration. Alliant's own filings show a single large industrial customer departure cuts guidance $0.12/share. For utilities banking on data-center load tailwinds, the same demand concentration that drives peak-load growth creates lumpy downside risk.

Fortis operates the explicit diversification playbook: five Canadian provinces, ten U.S. states, Caribbean — FY2025 revenue $12B, $28.8B five-year capital plan, rate base $42.4B → $57.9B through 2030, 4-6% annual dividend growth. Consolidated Edison is equivalent in a single dense urban geography: $24.3B aggregate capital 2028-2030, planning 28 new substations by 2035. Transmission and grid infrastructure (NextEra, ConEd, Alliant) are the highest-confidence capex categories — regulated returns, long asset lives, no commodity exposure, bipartisan political support — because the energy transition requires more wires regardless of generation mix.

Sub-trends inside the sector

Contracted data-center load anchoring regional electric utilities into decade-long capex plans

Alliant Energy has 3.4 GW of contracted data-center demand (Q1 2026) with Google, Meta, QTS — not pipeline or expected load, but executed ESAs justifying a $13.4B capital plan for 2026-2029 and advance-ratemaking tools. Management projects 60% increase in system peak load from 2025 to 2031. This is the inflection point where regulated electric utilities shift from load-flat compounding to growth-equity narratives backed by signed contracts. The model works because data-center customers commit to long-term fixed demand, allowing utilities to plan capital deployment with visibility that never existed in commodity-driven demand cycles.

Alliant Energy Corporation

Gas distribution earnings decoupled from commodity volume through weather normalization and rate-base expansion

ONE Gas and Atmos Energy both grew earnings in recent periods despite weather headwinds (Q2 2026: 42% warmer than normal for ONE Gas) through weather-normalization mechanisms and new-rate implementation. Earnings are no longer a function of selling more gas; they flow from capital deployment and regulatory lag reduction. Atmos expects rate base to double from $21.4B to $42B by FY2030 through $26B capex, 85%+ focused on safety and reliability — not throughput. The model is structurally sound as long as new gas infrastructure remains a regulated asset with cost-recovery guarantees, but the long-term threat is transition risk: if state regulators begin treating new gas distribution capex as potentially stranded, the entire compounding thesis breaks.

Wildfire liability and grid hardening as core balance-sheet operating variables in high-risk geographies

Edison International's 2025 Eaton Fire created material financial exposure that forced wildfire management into the center of operating discipline, not a disclosure footnote. The company's 5-7% core EPS CAGR through 2030 depends on California's Wildfire Fund reimbursement mechanics, securitization capacity, and regulatory cost-recovery approval. A $38B-$41B capex plan exists not because demand is growing but because grid infrastructure requires physical hardening to reduce ignition risk. This is climate-adaptation capex funded through regulated returns — a model that only works if California's regulatory architecture (Wildfire Fund, cost recovery, securitization) holds stable. Regulatory withdrawal of any component creates material earnings risk.

Water utility consolidation converting fragmented municipal systems into regulated rate-base earnings

American Water Works added 40,000 customer connections in 2025 and had $582M of acquisitions under agreement representing 104,000 connections at year-end — growth engine is not organic demand but system fragmentation. Thousands of small water operators lack capital, face PFAS/PFOS mandates, and find regulated consolidation attractive. American Water converts acquired systems into compounding earnings through rate-case execution and infrastructure surcharges, maintaining residential bills below 1% of median household income. Essential Utilities runs the parallel playbook. The growth is durable as long as municipalities remain willing to sell systems to regulated operators and rate cases continue to grant cost recovery.

Multi-jurisdiction regulatory diversification insulating platform compounding from single-regulator risk

Fortis operates across five Canadian provinces, ten U.S. states, and the Caribbean with FY2025 revenue $12B, a $28.8B five-year capital plan guiding rate base from $42.4B to $57.9B through 2030, and explicit 4-6% annual dividend growth guidance. The thesis is disciplined: no single regulator can materially disrupt the compound because earnings are spread across independent regulatory jurisdictions. Consolidated Edison achieves similar insulation in a single dense urban territory — $24.3B aggregate capital program 2028-2030 with regulatory approval already in place and 28 new substations planned by 2035. Both are versions of the same principle: own the infrastructure, navigate regulation methodically, and convert the monopoly into a compounding machine.

Fortis Inc., Consolidated Edison, Inc.

Transmission infrastructure as the regulated capital frontier with bipartisan support and commodity-neutral returns

Transmission investment is the highest-confidence capex category across electric utilities because it offers regulated returns with long asset lives, no commodity exposure, and bipartisan political support for grid expansion. The energy transition requires more wires regardless of whether the power source is solar, gas, wind, or nuclear — which means transmission capex is structurally insulated from the political debate about generation mix. NextEra Energy is explicitly framing transmission infrastructure as a core regulated capital-return story. Consolidated Edison is deploying 28 new substations in New York by 2035. Alliant is backing data-center demand with transmission buildout. The returns are predictable, the lives are long, and the political cover is durable.

The tension

The sector's central fault line is the regulatory compact under stress. Utilities need massive, long-duration capital commitments justified by demand signals (data-center contracts) or climate mandates (grid hardening), but the earnings on that capital depend entirely on regulatory decisions that can be delayed, revised, or denied. In California, Edison's multi-decade grid-hardening plan rests on Wildfire Fund reimbursement and cost-recovery rulings that the state can withdraw. In gas jurisdictions, Atmos and ONE Gas are compounding capital with 30-50 year asset lives into a system whose regulatory status is uncertain as energy-transition pressure grows. In electric markets with data-center anchors, Alliant's load-growth thesis depends on contracted customers actually building and absorbing power — customer concentration risk at scale. The utilities that win have already locked in regulatory frameworks, advance-rate tools, and multi-year capital plans before political conditions shifted. The utilities that lose are those whose capital programs still depend on approvals that haven't been granted.

Who wins · who loses

Winners: electric utilities in regulatory-friendly jurisdictions with credible contracted load growth (Alliant Energy). Water utilities executing regulated consolidation in fragmented markets (American Water Works). Multi-jurisdiction platforms with diversified regulatory exposure (Fortis, Consolidated Edison). Near-term winners with long-term uncertainty: gas utilities like Atmos and ONE Gas that are compounding capital reliably now but face multi-decade question about whether regulators will treat new gas infrastructure as stranded cost. Losers: utilities in single high-stress jurisdictions where political risk is elevated (Edison International managing wildfire liability and affordability politics in California right now). Smaller utilities with meaningful customer concentration (Avista: one large industrial departure = $0.12/share guidance cut). Hydro-dependent operators (water availability exposure) with no offsetting geographic diversification.

Outlook

Utilities are entering a sustained capital supercycle driven by three independent mandates requiring the same infrastructure deployment: data-center demand accelerating load growth in select territories; climate adaptation forcing grid hardening regardless of demand; aging infrastructure across gas, water, electric networks triggering replacement capex that regulators cannot defer without safety consequences. The sector's 5-9% EPS growth ceiling is not constrained opportunity but the mathematical result of regulated return on equity translating capital into earnings. Companies with clearest visibility have already locked in contracted demand (data centers), approved capital plans, and advance-ratemaking tools into decade-long compounding. Medium-term risk: affordability politics. As customer bills rise to fund infrastructure buildout, regulators in high-cost-of-living states face pressure to slow capital recovery, creating earnings lag for exactly the utilities taking aggressive infrastructure positions. Long-term risk: regulatory status of gas infrastructure. If state regulators begin treating new gas capex as potentially stranded, the compounding model for gas utilities breaks regardless of near-term visibility.

Utilities are locked into a decade of infrastructure capital deployment, but every dollar of earnings on that build depends on regulatory frameworks that are increasingly subject to political revision as customer bills rise and climate liability concentrates in high-risk geographies.
