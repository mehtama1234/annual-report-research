The Spread Machine

The most durable profit engines in the 2026 economy are not innovators but intermediaries — companies that sit between producers and consumers at chokepoints so expensive to replicate that pricing power has nothing to do with service quality and everything to do with the cost of building around them.

Distribution consolidators control mandatory access layers between producers and consumers. They do not own the underlying commodity and they do not create new demand. They extract a persistent spread by being the only practical route to the other side. The spread is durable not because their service is irreplaceable but because the alternative — building a competing pharmaceutical distribution network, a second permitted landfill within economic hauling distance, a competing 140,000-mile pipeline system, or a second set of 1,746 branches stocked with local contractor inventory — would require capital, permitting, and relationship-building that takes a decade and still might not produce a viable competitor. Route density, permitted landfill positions, long-term pipeline agreements extending into the 2030s, and branch-level contractor relationships all function as access taxes rather than earned service premiums.

The spread machine is visibly accelerating. Across these seven distributors, the pattern is consistent: pricing power is expanding even as growth moderates, access dependence is deepening through service attachment and technical capability, and the underlying commodity or throughput is increasingly secondary to the intermediary's control. Arrow's 32% growth shows recovery breadth; Cardinal and Cencora show pharmaceutical and healthcare system lock-in independent of headline volume; Casella, CHRW, and DNOW show that regional/sectoral density and pricing discipline matter more than commodity price movement; Applied shows technical services compounding the moat. This is not a cyclical recovery story. These are structurally entrenched intermediaries extending their spreads.

The mechanism

Cencora moves $321.3B in pharmaceutical revenue annually without owning a single drug patent or manufacturing a molecule. Republic Services collects from 13 million customers across 1,000+ permitted locations using 17,000 trucks — but the moat is not the trucks, it is the landfill permits that take years to obtain and cannot be relocated when economics shift. Energy Transfer moves natural gas and NGLs through 140,000 miles of pipeline infrastructure where the fee is set by the cost of building an alternative corridor, not by the marginal cost of operating the one that already exists. Ferguson operates 1,746 branches stocked with local inventory for specialized contractors — contractors who cannot wait two days for a valve from a central warehouse when a job site is down. Arrow Electronics finances and routes the global semiconductor supply chain through $28B in receivables and $5.94B in inventories at any given moment, providing financing and logistics that OEMs and manufacturers cannot replicate at comparable cost. None of these companies own the underlying commodity. All extract a spread by controlling the intermediary layer.

The mechanism is structural chokehold, not logistics innovation. A competing pharmaceutical distribution network would require regulatory approval, credit relationships with thousands of suppliers and pharmacies, and the operational scale to guarantee delivery reliability that hospital systems and pharmacy chains depend on daily. A competing landfill network within economic hauling distance of Republic's routes is practically impossible — permitted landfill positions in dense urban and suburban markets are capped by environmental regulation and local opposition, making Republic's 1,000+ locations a durable geographic franchise, not a fleet of trucks that a competitor could replicate by purchasing more trucks. Energy Transfer's long-term pipeline agreements extend into the 2030s; a competing corridor would need years of permitting, easements, and construction, and by the time it arrived, the commercial agreements would already be locked. Ferguson's moat is branch density plus technical staff plus local inventory — contractor markets are fragmented enough that the first distributor with a branch in a given area captures recurring relationships that rarely switch.

The spread is largely invisible to the end consumer. A patient paying a pharmacy price for a specialty drug has no visibility into Cencora's margin embedded in that price. A municipality paying a waste-hauling contract has limited ability to bring in a competing hauler when the only permitted landfill within economic distance is owned by Republic. A utility customer paying a gas bill includes Energy Transfer's pipeline tariff in that bill with no practical alternative. A contractor buying a valve from Ferguson at 31% gross margin to Ferguson has no equivalent local option when the job is time-sensitive. This invisibility is not accidental — it is the defining feature of chokepoint distribution. The margin is embedded in a price the customer must pay, set by the cost of alternatives that do not exist.

The model compounds through pricing discipline, volume absorption, and bolt-on acquisition that eliminates switching options. Republic raised pricing above cost inflation every quarter through 2026 while Q1 2026 showed 50 basis points of adjusted EBITDA margin expansion. Cencora absorbed GLP-1 volume — a lower-margin product category — while still growing total spread revenue and raising U.S. Healthcare Solutions operating income 22% in FY2025. Ferguson completed five acquisitions in Q2 2026 alone and signed the FloWorks deal for technical valve and flow-control distribution, extending the branch network into higher-complexity industrial channels. Energy Transfer signed long-term fractionation and transportation agreements for about 300,000 bpd on y-grade assets extending into the 2030s, locking future cash flows before the capacity even comes online. Each acquisition or long-term agreement reduces future customer switching options rather than building new customer value.

Why now

Three specific demand surges in 2024–2026 increased volume through fixed intermediary infrastructure without adding new intermediaries, widening spreads without commensurate cost growth. First, the GLP-1 volume surge that accelerated sharply through 2024 and 2025 sent enormous new pharmaceutical throughput to Cencora, the only distributor operating at the scale required to handle specialty-drug logistics for hospital systems and pharmacy chains simultaneously. Management made the paradox explicit: GLP-1 growth pressured gross margin percentage (because GLP-1 drugs carry lower distributor margin than other specialty categories) while still lifting total revenue and operating income — more volume through a fixed chokepoint at a thinner unit spread still produces more total dollars. Second, the AI hardware demand wave that peaked in late 2024 and extended through 2025 drove Arrow Electronics global components sales up 20% year over year in Q4 2025, then 39% in Q1 2026, then another 39% in Q2 2026 — three consecutive quarters of accelerating growth driven by semiconductor and enterprise hardware demand that only scaled global distributors could finance (Arrow carried $28B in receivables as of July 2026). Third, the infrastructure construction boom — data-center buildouts, water-system upgrades, utility expansion, midstream gas infrastructure — drove non-residential revenue up 8% at Ferguson in both Q1 and Q2 2026 even as residential markets stayed weak, pushed DNOW's midstream revenue past a $1B annualized run rate for the first time in Q2 2026, and pushed Energy Transfer's adjusted EBITDA guidance to $18.8B–$19.1B for full-year 2026. In each case, the inflection point was a specific demand shock — not a gradual trend — hitting infrastructure that had a fixed or near-fixed number of permitted, capitalized, and relationship-embedded operators.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Pharmacies and hospital systems with no practical alternative to Cencora at the scale required for specialty-drug access. Municipalities and commercial customers with no landfill alternative within economic hauling distance of Republic's permitted locations — no municipality can simply choose a different landfill operator when Republic operates the only one within viable distance. Utilities and producers locked into Energy Transfer's pipeline corridors by physics and long-term contract structures extending into the 2030s. Contractors in fragmented local markets who cannot wait for central-warehouse fulfillment when a job is down and Ferguson has the only local branch with the part in stock. OEMs and electronics manufacturers who cannot self-finance the semiconductor supply chain at the scale Arrow intermediates with $28B in receivables. Patients who pay drug prices that embed Cencora's margin and utility customers who pay energy bills that embed Energy Transfer's pipeline tariff, neither of whom has visibility into how much of the final price is spread extraction versus actual logistics cost.
Who profits
The major distribution consolidators with fixed or near-fixed intermediary positions: Cencora ($321.3B FY2025 revenue, U.S. Healthcare Solutions operating income up 22%), Republic Services ($5.31B FY2025 adjusted EBITDA, 50 basis points margin expansion in Q1 2026), Energy Transfer ($5.07B Q2 2026 adjusted EBITDA, full-year 2026 guidance raised to $18.8B–$19.1B), Ferguson (31.0% gross margin held across Q1 and Q2 2026, five acquisitions in Q2 alone), Arrow Electronics ($7.366B Q2 2026 components sales up 39%), DNOW ($1B+ midstream annualized run rate in Q2 2026 after closing the MRC Global acquisition in November 2025). Each is compounding margin through pricing discipline above cost inflation, volume absorption at existing infrastructure, and bolt-on acquisitions that extend route density and reduce customer switching options.

The tension

Disintermediation risk is real and recurring. Amazon spent years building direct-to-contractor supply that undercuts distributors of commodity products; GLP-1 manufacturers could eventually build direct-to-pharmacy channels that bypass Cencora's margin; digital freight platforms have already pressured CH Robinson's brokerage spread. Regulatory scrutiny of pharmaceutical distribution concentration is not hypothetical — Cencora's control of drug access is visible enough that any major drug affordability legislation could target distributor margins explicitly. Republic's landfill dominance in local markets has drawn antitrust scrutiny around waste-sector consolidation that could limit future acquisitions or force divestitures. Energy Transfer's long-cycle capital program assumes continued gas demand through the 2030s; a faster-than-expected shift toward distributed power generation (local microgrids, onsite generation) or electrification that reduces gas throughput would strand pipeline assets before the long-term agreements that justify the capital expire. The most acute risk is that the spread machine is visible only when something goes wrong — a drug shortage, an energy price spike, or a waste-disposal failure that draws public attention to how much of the final price is intermediary margin versus actual commodity cost.

The surprising part

The GLP-1 paradox makes the model's logic visible in a way that almost never happens: Cencora management stated explicitly that higher GLP-1 volume pressured gross margin percentage while lifting revenue. This is the opposite of how normal markets work — more volume should produce scale efficiencies that protect or expand margin. In a chokepoint distribution model, the opposite is true. More volume arrives at the fixed infrastructure regardless of per-unit margin, and the intermediary absorbs it at whatever spread the contract allows, because no alternative infrastructure exists. The total spread revenue rises not because the distributor became more efficient or valuable, but because more throughput passed through the only available channel. This is exactly what a toll road looks like: the road does not get cheaper to operate per car as traffic increases, but total toll revenue rises with every car. Cencora is the pharmaceutical toll road, and the GLP-1 surge was a surge in traffic through a fixed booth.

Why it matters

Intermediary margins embedded in drug prices, energy bills, waste-disposal contracts, and construction costs function as invisible taxes on households, municipalities, and businesses that have no line-item visibility into how much of their final price is spread extraction versus actual commodity or logistics cost. Cencora's margin is embedded in specialty drug prices that already face affordability pressure; a patient fighting a cancer diagnosis does not know how much of what they pay is pharmaceutical-distribution margin. Republic's landfill pricing is embedded in municipal waste contracts that cities must sign because no alternative exists — pricing power that comes from permit position, not service quality, ultimately flows through to household taxes and commercial waste fees. Energy Transfer's pipeline tariffs are embedded in utility bills where customers have no alternative and no transparency. The structural problem is not that these companies are individually malicious; it is that the chokepoint model inverts the incentive structure. Instead of competing on efficiency or service quality, consolidators compete on acquiring competitors and extending geographic or regulatory moats. Capital flows toward control of mandatory access layers rather than toward innovation that creates new value. Over time, this concentrates wealth at the intermediary layer — companies that tax access to existing demand — rather than at producers who create the underlying commodity or consumers who create the underlying demand.

These companies own nothing worth owning, innovate nothing worth patenting, and compete on nothing except the prohibitive cost of building around them — which is exactly why their spreads are durable.

The case against this trend

What would falsify it: Evidence that intermediaries with significant technical service depth, vertical integration toward end-customers, or subscription-like recurring relationships can sustain high margins and growth even when structural chokepoints erode or when disintermediation occurs; or that integration challenges, organic growth slowdowns, and margin pressure in merger-heavy platforms indicate that scale and consolidation alone do not create durable spreads.

Companies that don't fit:

  • Arrow Electronics — Despite control of semiconductor distribution, Arrow's operating margin compressed 10 basis points in 2025 to 2.7%, and Q2 2026 ECS segment operating income fell 12% year-over-year even though sales rose 14% — showing that even in constrained channels, margin deteriorates without continuous technical and service depth; the company depends on working-capital financing and supply-chain coordination (not just chokepoint control) to monetize, as evidenced by $28B receivables and supplier protections covering 56-59% of inventory.
  • Applied Industrial Technologies — Despite Engineered Solutions approaching 40% of sales (a high-margin, technical service-heavy business), organic sales growth decelerated from +3.0% in fiscal Q1 2026 to +2.2% in fiscal Q2 2026 to +6.0% in fiscal Q3 2026 — showing volatile demand outside pure chokepoint models; the company also explicitly tied demand weakness to customer caution around capital and MRO spending tied to macro uncertainty rather than to structural access control, contradicting the thesis that spreads are independent of business-cycle conditions.
  • DNOW Inc. — The combined DNOW/MRC Global platform showed immediate margin pressure post-merger despite consolidation and cost-synergy targets being ahead of plan: Q1 2026 net loss of $44M and adjusted EBITDA of only $39M on $1,183M revenue (3.3% margin), only recovering to Q2 2026 with $21M adjusted net income and $60M adjusted EBITDA (4.6% margin); this demonstrates that consolidation alone does not create instant or automatic spread durability, and that operational integration, ERP disruption, and working-capital friction can erode margins for extended periods even when structural demand exists.
  • Ferguson Enterprises — Despite controlling 1,746 branches with high operational density, residential revenue declined 1% in Q1 2026 and 2% in Q4 2025 even while Ferguson maintained pricing and achieved 31% gross margin — showing that chokepoint control cannot prevent customer volume loss when end-markets soften; the thesis assumes spreads rise because customers have no alternatives, but Ferguson's non-residential strength paired with residential weakness shows that the intermediary still faces volume variance tied to end-customer demand elasticity, not just access control.

Honest caveat: The spread-machine thesis applies most clearly to intermediaries with structural, regulatory, or physical chokepoints (landfill permits, pipeline corridors, pharmaceutical distribution network scale) where end-customers truly lack practical alternatives. It applies less clearly to intermediaries that depend on technical service depth, customer relationships, or fast-moving end-market demand where disintermediation or margin compression is possible (Arrow's margin compression, Applied's organic growth variability, DNOW's integration friction). The thesis also cannot explain quarter-to-quarter volatility, end-market cyclicality, or the difference between spread growth in buoyant demand periods (Energy Transfer NGL exports up 25%, Arrow components up 39%) versus stalled periods (Applied's organic growth deceleration, Ferguson's residential decline). Finally, the thesis assumes pricing discipline persists indefinitely, but regulatory disruption (drug affordability legislation targeting Cencora), disintermediation (manufacturers building direct channels), or macro-driven customer caution (Applied's MRO hesitation, Ferguson's residential softness) can interrupt spreads faster than the argument acknowledges.

The tension

The Crack Widens: Disintermediation Threatens Every Distribution Model

C.H. Robinson has outgrown freight markets for 13 consecutive quarters using AI automation, but it is still a broker competing with platforms built to eliminate brokers. Fastenal's 45% gross margin survives because it embedded vending machines in customer walls. McKesson and Cardinal face manufacturer-direct specialty pharma. Every spread machine's greatest strength—concentration in high-value customers or products—is also its greatest vulnerability.
