Origin Chokehold: Distribution Consolidation Creating Pricing Power

Distribution consolidation—landfills, pipelines, pharmaceutical licenses, HVAC distribution, plumbing supply—creates durable pricing-power platforms. Suppliers cannot bypass these networks; customers have limited alternatives. Spread-machine economics from 30-year market consolidation.

Licensed and regulated infrastructure creates chokehold economics: competitors cannot build parallel networks economically; switching costs are high; and customers must deal with consolidated distributors. These platforms monetize through spread economics—buying low, selling high, capturing margin through system control—rather than through volume or innovation.

The mechanism

Landfill consolidation creates unbypassable waste-disposal chokehold. Republic Services operates landfills across North America with decades of permitted capacity and customer relationships. Competitors cannot build new landfills due to regulatory barriers, NIMBY resistance, and capital intensity. Republic's pricing power comes from customers having no alternative: trash must go somewhere, and Republic controls much of where. The company monetizes this through collection fees, landfill tipping fees, and ancillary services.

Pipeline infrastructure creates transportation monopolies. Energy Transfer operates natural-gas and crude-oil pipelines with fixed geography and regulatory barriers. Producers need these pipelines; buyers need these pipelines. Shipping rates (the 'spread') are regulated or determined by contract, creating durable cash flows. Competitors cannot build parallel pipelines due to cost, permits, and lack of density. Energy Transfer captures spread economics between wellhead prices and buyer locations.

Pharmaceutical and medical distribution consolidates supplier access. Cencora and Cardinal Health serve as obligatory intermediaries between manufacturers and pharmacies/hospitals. They have consolidated the market so thoroughly that manufacturers cannot bypass them and providers cannot easily switch. The spread—buying low from manufacturers, selling to providers—is protected by switching costs, volume commitments, and data integration. McKesson operates at even larger scale, controlling pharmaceutical flow to much of the U.S. healthcare system.

Why now

Through 2025-2026, distribution-consolidation chokehold economics strengthen because: (1) 30 years of M&A has left these markets with few meaningful competitors, (2) regulatory barriers to entry remain high (landfills, pipelines, pharma licenses), (3) customer switching costs are high (integrated IT systems, long-term contracts, relationship depth), (4) margins are protected by lack of substitutes, and (5) inflation and volume growth allow these platforms to raise prices without losing significant volume.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Waste generators paying Republic Services collection and disposal fees; energy producers and consumers paying Energy Transfer transportation costs embedded in fuel prices; hospitals and pharmacies paying Cencora and Cardinal Health markups on supplies; contractors and HVAC shops paying Ferguson premium prices; consumers indirectly paying through healthcare costs and utility bills.
Who profits
Republic Services, Energy Transfer, Cencora, Cardinal Health, Ferguson Enterprises, McKesson, and peer consolidated distributors. They capture durable spreads (margins between cost and selling price) protected by regulatory barriers, switching costs, and network effects. Smaller competitors in each space lose share to the consolidated giants.

The tension

The chokehold model breaks if: (1) regulatory barriers weaken (e.g., pipeline deregulation, waste-disposal policy changes), (2) substitutes emerge (e.g., waste-to-energy, alternative energy, direct-to-provider pharmaceutical sales), (3) customer concentration increases and buyers gain leverage, (4) new market entrants overcome barriers (rare but possible with sufficient capital and patience), or (5) antitrust enforcement targets these markets. Political pressure to lower healthcare costs or energy prices could accelerate regulation of these spreads.

The surprising part

These companies are not innovators or product leaders. They are infrastructure gatekeepers monetizing through pure market control. Republic Services is not better at waste disposal than competitors; it owns more landfills and has higher switching costs. Energy Transfer's pipes do not move gas faster than competitors' would; it controls the geography. Cencora and Cardinal Health are not better at logistics than others could be; they control supplier and customer relationships. This is capitalism's unglamorous but durable layer: owning the chokehold.

Why it matters

When distribution consolidates into a few platforms, prices to end-users rise and smaller suppliers and providers lose negotiating power. Consolidated waste disposal, energy transportation, and pharmaceutical distribution increase consumer costs indirectly. If these platforms' pricing power becomes obvious to regulators or policymakers, antitrust enforcement or price regulation could interrupt the model, but disruption would be wrenching for incumbents and short-term expensive for consumers.

Consolidated distributors monetize spread economics from infrastructure, licenses, and regulatory barriers that make alternatives economically infeasible.
