Origin Chokehold: Distribution Consolidation Creating Pricing Power

Distribution consolidation—landfills, pipelines, pharmaceutical licenses, HVAC distribution, plumbing supply—creates durable pricing-power platforms. Suppliers cannot bypass these networks; customers have limited alternatives. Spread-machine economics from 30-year market consolidation.

Licensed and regulated infrastructure creates chokehold economics: competitors cannot build parallel networks economically; switching costs are high; and customers must deal with consolidated distributors. These platforms monetize through spread economics—buying low, selling high, capturing margin through system control—rather than through volume or innovation.

The mechanism

Landfill consolidation created an unbypassable waste-disposal chokehold during 1990-2025. Republic Services operates landfills across North America with decades of permitted capacity and deep customer relationships. Competitors cannot build new landfills due to regulatory barriers (environmental permitting requires 5-10 years), NIMBY resistance (communities reject new landfill siting), and capital intensity ($500M+ per major regional landfill). Republic's pricing power comes from customers having no alternative: trash must go somewhere, and Republic controls much of where. The company monetizes this through collection fees, landfill tipping fees, and ancillary services. FY2025 revenue of $16.99B shows the scale. The profitability is protected by the regulatory moat; no new entrant can build competing capacity because permitting and political opposition make it economically infeasible. This is a 30-year consolidation play that reached maturity by 2020-2025.

Pipeline infrastructure created transportation monopolies during 1990-2025. Energy Transfer operates natural-gas and crude-oil pipelines with fixed geography and regulatory barriers to competition. Producers need these pipelines; buyers need these pipelines. Shipping rates (the 'spread') are regulated or determined by contract, creating durable cash flows. Competitors cannot build parallel pipelines due to capital costs ($10B+ per major interstate system), permits that require 5-7 years, and lack of density (a second pipeline is uneconomical because the first already captures most of the throughput). Energy Transfer's $14.8B FY2025 revenue represents the monetization of this spread across thousands of miles of pipeline infrastructure. The consolidation accelerated during 2000-2015 through M&A; by 2020-2025, the market is mature and the spreads are permanent. Smaller regional gas utilities have been absorbed or marginalized.

Pharmaceutical and medical distribution consolidated supplier access during 1990-2025 into three majors: Cencora ($16.7B annual revenue, FY2025), Cardinal Health ($179B annual revenue, FY2025), and McKesson ($180B+ annual revenue, FY2025). These three serve as obligatory intermediaries between manufacturers (Pfizer, Merck, Johnson & Johnson, Novo Nordisk) and pharmacies, hospitals, and providers. They have consolidated the market so thoroughly that manufacturers cannot bypass them and providers cannot easily switch. The spread—buying low from manufacturers, selling to providers—is protected by switching costs (integrated IT systems, supplier relationships, volume commitments, data integration), scale advantages (only three majors can process volume efficiently), and regulatory barriers (wholesaler licensing and track-and-trace requirements prevent new entrants). Cencora's FY2025 revenue of $16.7B shows healthcare distribution scale. McKesson's $180B+ shows pharmaceutical distribution scale (making it one of the largest distributors by revenue in the world). These three distributors control the flow of pharmaceuticals to much of the U.S. healthcare system. The consolidation took 30 years (1990-2020); by 2025, it is mature, pricing is coordinated, and switching is structurally difficult.

Why now

Distribution-consolidation chokehold economics strengthened as a permanent structural feature during 2020-2025, driven by the culmination of 30 years of market consolidation (1990-2020). By 2025, the major distribution players (Republic, Energy Transfer, Cencora, Cardinal, McKesson, Ferguson) had achieved such scale and integration that pricing power became structural, not cyclical. The inflection point was 2020-2022: (1) The COVID supply-chain disruption (2020-2021) showed customers that distribution consolidation meant they had no alternatives when the network strained; (2) Inflation (2021-2022) allowed these platforms to raise prices with limited customer defection because switching was prohibitively expensive; (3) Private equity took stakes in regional distributors (2018-2022), folding them into larger platforms and consolidating further. By 2025, the market structure was so concentrated that the spreads became permanent and predictable. Through 2025-2026, distribution-consolidation chokehold economics strengthen because: (1) Regulatory barriers to entry remain high (landfill permitting, pipeline regulation, pharmaceutical licensing); (2) Customer switching costs are high (integrated IT systems, long-term contracts, relationship depth); (3) Margins are protected by lack of substitutes; (4) Inflation and volume growth allow these platforms to raise prices without losing significant volume. The consolidation that began in 1990 has reached maturity by 2025.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Waste generators pay Republic Services collection and disposal fees; energy producers and consumers pay Energy Transfer transportation costs embedded in fuel prices; hospitals and pharmacies pay Cencora and Cardinal Health markups on supplies; contractors and HVAC shops pay Ferguson premium prices for distribution; consumers indirectly pay through healthcare costs (pharmaceutical markups), utility bills (transportation costs), and waste disposal fees. Small businesses and price-sensitive customers pay the most because they lack negotiating power that large customers (CVS, Walmart, Anthem) have. Regional competitors are systematically pushed out because they cannot match the scale of the major distributors.
Who profits
Republic Services, Energy Transfer, Cencora, Cardinal Health, Ferguson Enterprises, McKesson, and peer consolidated distributors. They capture durable spreads (margins between cost and selling price) protected by regulatory barriers, switching costs, and network effects. Republic's FY2025 revenue of $16.99B is nearly pure margin because landfill disposal is commodity-like once you control the landfills. Energy Transfer's $14.8B revenue is protected spreads from pipeline control. Cencora's $16.7B and Cardinal's $179B are spread economics from supplier-to-provider intermediation. Smaller competitors in each space lose share to the consolidated giants. The profit distribution is entirely one-directional: consolidators capture all spread economics; customers have no negotiating power.

The tension

The chokehold model breaks if: (1) regulatory barriers weaken (e.g., pipeline deregulation forcing rate competition, waste-disposal policy changes allowing new landfill permitting, pharmaceutical licensing rules simplified for new distributors); (2) substitutes emerge (e.g., waste-to-energy technology, alternative energy sources like renewables reducing pipeline need, direct-to-provider pharmaceutical distribution bypassing wholesalers); (3) customer concentration increases and buyers gain leverage (e.g., if a few mega-hospitals consolidate and threaten to bypass Cencora, or if CVS and Walmart collectively negotiate rates); (4) new market entrants overcome barriers (rare but possible with sufficient capital and political will); or (5) antitrust enforcement targets these markets (increasing probability 2024-2026). Political pressure to lower healthcare costs could accelerate regulation of pharmaceutical distribution spreads. Environmental pressure could accelerate waste-to-energy substitution. Energy transition could reduce pipeline demand. Any of these could disrupt the model, but the current structure is very durable because the regulatory and economic barriers are high.

The surprising part

These companies are not innovators or product leaders. They are infrastructure gatekeepers monetizing through pure market control. Republic Services is not better at waste disposal than competitors; it owns more landfills and has higher switching costs. Energy Transfer's pipes do not move gas faster than competitors' would; it controls the geography and regulation. Cencora and Cardinal Health are not better at logistics than others could be; they control supplier and customer relationships at scale. McKesson does not manufacture drugs; it controls the distribution infrastructure. This is capitalism's unglamorous but durable layer: owning the chokehold. The surprise is how explicit and uncontested this is by 2025. These companies do not hide their pricing power; they advertise it. Republic openly raises pricing 4-6% annually because customers have nowhere to go. Energy Transfer's long-term contracts lock in rates for decades. Cencora and Cardinal openly negotiate with suppliers and customers based on their consolidated position. This is not hidden rent-seeking; it is visible, systematic, and structurally protected.

Why it matters

When distribution consolidates into a few platforms, prices to end-users rise and smaller suppliers and providers lose negotiating power. Consolidated waste disposal, energy transportation, and pharmaceutical distribution increase consumer costs indirectly through higher landfill fees, utility rates, and healthcare costs. If these platforms' pricing power becomes obvious to regulators or policymakers (as it is by 2025), antitrust enforcement or price regulation could interrupt the model, but disruption would be wrenching for incumbents and short-term expensive for consumers. The alternative is that consolidation continues and spreads widen indefinitely, creating permanent wealth extraction from customers with limited alternatives. By 2025, the second path is more likely because the consolidation is so advanced and the regulatory barriers so high.

Consolidated distributors monetize spread economics from infrastructure, licenses, and regulatory barriers that make alternatives economically infeasible.
