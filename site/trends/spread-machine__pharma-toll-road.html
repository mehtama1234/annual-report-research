The Drug Tax: How Pharmaceutical Distributors Sit Between Every Pill and Every Patient

Three companies control 90% of U.S. drug distribution, collecting recurring spreads that have nothing to do with logistics and everything to do with structural chokehold.

McKesson, Cencora, and Cardinal Health control ~90% of U.S. drug distribution. They sit between every manufacturer and every pharmacy, hospital, and provider. The GLP-1 surge created a paradox: revenue rising while gross-margin percentage fell, because distributors are locked into percentage-of-price economics. Specialty and oncology channels (McKesson's $14.2B Oncology & Multispecialty, Cencora's OneOncology) are the escape valve toward higher-quality spread.

The mechanism

The distribution structure is a three-layer tax. Manufacturers produce drugs at cost; distributors buy and hold inventory; pharmacies and providers dispense. Distributors do not own drugs long. They hold them days or weeks, collect a small percentage spread (1-3%), and move massive volume. With $1 trillion flowing through distribution annually, even 1-2% spread generates enormous absolute dollar income. But volume does not increase infinitely. Every pill sold is one fewer pill to sell next cycle.

The GLP-1 paradox reveals the margin trap. GLP-1 volume surged 100%+, so gross-profit dollars rose. But gross-margin percentage fell because GLP-1 drugs are high-volume, thin-spread commodities. Distributors bought and moved more volume but earned less per unit. The financial engineering is to shift into higher-spread channels: specialty, oncology, and provider-facing services (McKesson's Oncology & Multispecialty at $14.2B; Cencora's OneOncology acquisition). These channels have lower volume but higher margin per unit.

The structural chokehold is that there is no bypass route. Manufacturers cannot sell directly to every pharmacy and hospital. Distributors control the logistics layer and have negotiated exclusive or preferred relationships with most providers. This creates dependency and insulates margins from pure commoditization.

Why now

GLP-1 growth exposed the limits of the percentage-of-price model. Distributors got more revenue but lower margins. This forced a strategic pivot: either push into specialty/oncology/services, or accept lower profitability. All three are pivoting simultaneously, creating consolidation (Cencora's OneOncology, McKesson's service expansion) and a race for higher-quality spread channels.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Manufacturers and providers pay through reduced wholesale margin and pressure to negotiate channel access. Patients potentially pay through higher end retail prices if distributors pass through pressure to pharmacies. Healthcare systems pay through complexity and embedded service costs.
Who profits
Distributors profit through market concentration, switching costs, and the ability to monetize every layer: distribution margin, specialty services, data services, logistics, and financing. McKesson, Cencora, and Cardinal Health are among the most durable businesses in healthcare because they sit at the chokepoint.

The tension

The percentage-of-price model is broken by high-volume, thin-margin drugs like GLP-1s. Distributors must either raise prices (unlikely in a competitive market), reduce costs (subject to logistics constraints), or shift into higher-margin channels. The shift to specialty/oncology/services increases complexity and risk, and depends on sustained pricing power in those channels.

The surprising part

Despite years of industry warnings about thin margins and volume pressure, all three distributors remain extraordinarily profitable. This suggests that the structural chokehold is stronger than cost pressure—manufacturers and providers have no choice but to use them.

Why it matters

Three companies control access to medicines for the entire U.S. healthcare system. Any disruption in their operations (strikes, cyber attacks, supply issues) cascades through the entire pharmacy and hospital network. The dependence is absolute, which means cost pressure from any direction (regulation, consolidation, geopolitics) can propagate through drug availability and pricing.

Pharmaceutical distributors control the one mandatory chokepoint between every drug and every patient, collecting tolls regardless of their actual value-add.
