The Crack Widens: Disintermediation Threatens Every Distribution Model

C.H. Robinson has outgrown freight markets for 13 consecutive quarters using AI automation, but it is still a broker competing with platforms built to eliminate brokers. Fastenal's 45% gross margin survives because it embedded vending machines in customer walls. McKesson and Cardinal face manufacturer-direct specialty pharma. Every spread machine's greatest strength—concentration in high-value customers or products—is also its greatest vulnerability.

Every distribution chokehold invites the same attack: someone builds a direct channel or a platform replaces the broker. The exposure is uneven. Asset-light brokers like C.H. Robinson face platform disintermediation. Healthcare distributors like McKesson and Cardinal face manufacturer-direct specialty pharma. The most defended players physically embedded themselves into customer operations (Fastenal vending, WESCO grid services). But embedding creates its own crack: customer concentration. The companies winning hardest in their segments are also the ones most exposed to disintermediation by customers large enough to self-supply. This is the paradox: strength and fragility are the same metric read two ways.

The mechanism

Disintermediation is not uniform; it maps precisely to how asset-light and substitutable each spread machine is. C.H. Robinson is most exposed by structure—a $16.2B freight brokerage whose entire value is matching shippers to carriers, exactly the function digital freight platforms are built to automate. The company's defensive response reveals the awareness: it has driven 'more than 60% productivity improvement since end of 2022' through 'Lean AI' and outgrown the Cass Freight Index for 13 consecutive quarters. That is a broker racing to become more efficient than the platforms attacking it—winning the sprint, but running on a track that is being paved over. The productivity gains extend the survival window but do not address the fundamental threat.

Healthcare distribution faces the manufacturer-direct vector, and it is most consequential because products have the highest value. McKesson moves $359.1B in revenue dominated by its U.S. Pharmaceutical engine ($327.7B)—exactly the flow that GLP-1 makers bypass by launching direct-to-patient programs. The defensive footprint is visible: McKesson is pruning its portfolio and pouring capital into Oncology & Multispecialty and Prescription Technology Solutions, building service moats around the distribution business it knows is attackable. Cardinal Health shows the defense is expensive and uncertain—its $184M goodwill impairment on Navista & ION physician-services platform proves the service-adjacency bet is risky. Both companies are betting that wrapping services around distribution creates stickiness, but manufacturers have strong incentives to keep the service margin themselves.

The best-defended spread machines physically embedded themselves into customer operations. Fastenal runs 61.4% of 2025 sales through its Digital Footprint—vending machines (FMI) and onsite staffing planted inside customer facilities—sustaining a 45.0% gross margin that commodity distribution cannot approach. You cannot easily disintermediate a supplier who owns the bin on your factory floor and the software embedded in your procurement. WESCO is attempting the same move upward, converting from selling parts into multi-year grid-services awards, with backlog up 60% to a record. Transactional supply becomes contracted infrastructure. But embedding creates its own vulnerability: customer concentration. WESCO's data-center sales hit $4.3B up 50%—a triumph that is also a vulnerability, because hyperscale data-center customers are large enough to self-supply at scale the moment the spread becomes worth internalizing. The very customers driving growth are the ones with buying power to become their own distributor. Concentration is the price of embedding.

The regulatory vector is the slowest but most structural. McKesson, Cardinal, and the drug-distribution oligopoly carry ongoing opioid-litigation cash demands and reimbursement-reform exposure that function as standing regulatory overhang. McKesson's portfolio pruning and the Apollo asset separation read as pre-positioning for a world where concentration itself becomes the liability. Regulators rarely break a spread machine outright, but they can force the fee structure open—and in healthcare, where the spread is politically visible, that pressure never fully recedes. C.H. Robinson faces its own regulatory pressure if gig-economy classification expands to freight platforms, changing the economic calculus of brokerage.

Why now

The 2025-2026 filings are the first where defensive spending against disintermediation is large enough to read as strategy rather than experiment. C.H. Robinson's 'Lean AI' productivity push (60%+ since 2022) is explicit response to digital freight platforms. McKesson and Cardinal are visibly reallocating capital toward specialty, oncology, and technology services and away from plain distribution—Cardinal took a $184M impairment learning the lesson. Meanwhile, GLP-1 manufacturers launching direct-to-patient channels have moved manufacturer-direct from theoretical to live threat in the highest-margin pharmaceutical category. The threat stopped being hypothetical the moment incumbents started paying to defend against it.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
In the near term, spread machines' own workers and shareholders bear cost of defense—C.H. Robinson's 60% productivity gain is partly automation displacing brokerage headcount. In the longer term, if disintermediation succeeds unevenly, the small operators who relied on distributor aggregation lose the intermediary that gave them scale-adjacent pricing. The independent pharmacy, small shipper, regional contractor lose the distributor that gave them power. Meanwhile, largest customers who can self-supply capture the freed spread.
Who profits
If the crack widens: manufacturers going direct (keeping distributor margins), digital platforms replacing brokers, and hyperscale customers large enough to self-supply (integrating production with distribution). If the defense holds: embedded spread machines—Fastenal at 45% margin inside customer walls, WESCO converting supply into contracted grid services—who prove physical entanglement, not just logistics, is the real moat.

The tension

The paradox is that the very success metrics—WESCO's $4.3B concentrated data-center sales, McKesson's dominance in specialty pharma—are simultaneously the vulnerability. Growth concentrated in few enormous customers or few high-value products is growth in exactly the segments most worth disintermediating. The spread machine's strength and fragility are the same number read from two directions. C.H. Robinson's productivity gains help it win for now but do not solve the fundamental competitive pressure. Fastenal's embedding is durable but only because the products are low-value and high-touch. WESCO's grid-services growth is precisely where hyperscale customers have the most incentive to integrate vertically.

The surprising part

The most disintermediation-proof business is not the biggest—it is Fastenal, whose defense is embarrassingly physical: it owns the vending machine bolted to the customer's factory wall. While C.H. Robinson races to out-automate platforms and McKesson buys service adjacencies, Fastenal's 45% gross margin rests on the un-sexy fact that ripping out an embedded FMI system and re-sourcing thousands of low-value consumables is more operational trouble than paying the spread. The lowest-tech moat is hardest to breach. Physical embeddedness and switching cost matter more than scale or automation.

Why it matters

Distribution consolidators sit between you and essentials: the drugs at your pharmacy, the parts that keep factories running, the freight that stocks the shelves. When manufacturers or platforms disintermediate them, the savings may reach you or may simply move to whoever is big enough to self-supply, while the small pharmacy or contractor that depended on distributor aggregation gets squeezed out. Who wins the disintermediation fight determines whether essential supply chains stay open to small players or consolidate around the largest.

Every spread machine's greatest success—concentration in high-value customers or products—is also its greatest vulnerability to disintermediation by customers large enough to self-supply.
