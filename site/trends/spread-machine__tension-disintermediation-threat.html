The Crack in the Machine: Disintermediation, Digital Competition, and Regulatory Pressure

Every distribution chokehold invites the same attack — someone builds a direct channel — and the question is not whether the crack appears but which spread machine it reaches first.

The durable spread machines all face the same existential threat from three vectors: manufacturers going direct, digital platforms replacing the broker, and regulators breaking up concentration. The exposure is uneven — asset-light brokers like C.H. Robinson are most vulnerable to platform disintermediation, healthcare distributors like McKesson and Cardinal are most exposed to manufacturer-direct specialty pharma, and the most defended players are those, like Fastenal and WESCO, who have physically embedded themselves into customer operations. The tell in the filings is what each company is spending to defend.

The mechanism

The disintermediation threat is not uniform; it maps precisely to how asset-light and how substitutable each spread machine is. C.H. Robinson is the most exposed by structure — a $16.2 billion freight brokerage whose entire value is matching shippers to carriers, exactly the function digital freight platforms are built to automate. The company's own defense reveals the fear: it has driven 'more than 60% productivity improvement since the end of 2022' through what it calls 'Lean AI,' and has now outgrown the Cass Freight index for 13 consecutive quarters. That is a broker racing to become more efficient than the platforms attacking it — winning the sprint, but running on a track that is being paved over.

Healthcare distribution faces the manufacturer-direct vector, and it is the most consequential because the products are the highest-value. McKesson moves $359.1 billion in revenue, dominated by its U.S. Pharmaceutical engine ($327.7 billion) — precisely the flow that a GLP-1 maker's direct-to-patient program routes around. The defensive footprint is visible: McKesson is pruning its portfolio and pouring capital into Oncology & Multispecialty and Prescription Technology Solutions, building service moats around the plain distribution business it knows is attackable. Cardinal Health tells the same story in reverse — its $184 million goodwill impairment on the Navista & ION physician-services platform shows the service-adjacency defense is expensive and not guaranteed to work.

The best-defended spread machines are the ones that stopped being pure intermediaries and physically embedded themselves in the customer's operations. Fastenal runs 61.4% of its 2025 sales through its 'Digital Footprint' — vending machines (FMI) and onsite staffing planted inside the customer's own facilities — which is why it sustains a 45.0% gross margin that commodity distribution cannot approach. You cannot easily disintermediate a supplier who owns the bin on your factory floor and the software embedded in your procurement. WESCO is attempting the same move upward, expanding from selling parts into multi-year grid-services awards, with backlog up 60% to a record — converting transactional supply into contracted infrastructure.

But embedding creates its own crack: customer concentration. WESCO's data-center sales hit $4.3 billion up 50%, a triumph that is also a vulnerability, because hyperscale data-center customers are large enough to self-supply at scale the moment the spread becomes worth internalizing. The very customers who make the growth story are the ones with the buying power to become their own distributor. Concentration is the price of embedding, and it is the vector that reaches the strongest players — not a platform or a regulator, but a single customer big enough to build the channel themselves.

The regulatory vector is the slowest but the most structural. McKesson, Cardinal, and the drug-distribution oligopoly carry ongoing opioid-litigation cash demands and reimbursement-reform exposure that function as a standing regulatory overhang; McKesson's own portfolio pruning and the Apollo asset separation read as pre-positioning for a world where concentration itself becomes the liability. Regulators rarely break a spread machine outright, but they can force the fee structure open — and in healthcare, where the spread is politically visible, that pressure never fully recedes.

Why now

The 2025-2026 filings are the first where the defensive spending against disintermediation is large enough to read as strategy rather than experiment. C.H. Robinson's 'Lean AI' productivity push (60%+ since 2022) is an explicit response to digital freight platforms. McKesson and Cardinal are visibly reallocating capital toward specialty, oncology, and technology services and away from plain distribution — Cardinal took a $184 million impairment learning that lesson. Meanwhile GLP-1 manufacturers launching direct-to-patient channels have moved manufacturer-direct from a theoretical risk to a live one in the single most profitable pharmaceutical category. The threat stopped being hypothetical the moment the incumbents started paying to defend against it.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
In the near term, the spread machines' own workers and shareholders bear the cost of the defense — C.H. Robinson's 60% productivity gain is partly automation displacing brokerage headcount. In the longer term, if disintermediation succeeds unevenly, the small operators who relied on the distributor's aggregation — the independent pharmacy, the small shipper, the regional contractor — lose the intermediary that gave them scale-adjacent pricing, while the largest customers who can self-supply capture the freed spread.
Who profits
If the crack widens: the manufacturers who go direct (a drugmaker keeping the distributor's margin), the digital platforms who replace the broker, and the hyperscale customers large enough to self-supply. If the defense holds: the embedded spread machines — Fastenal at 45% gross margin inside the customer's walls, WESCO converting supply into contracted grid services — who prove that physical entanglement, not just logistics, is the real moat.

The tension

The externalized cost is that the very success metrics — WESCO's $4.3 billion in concentrated data-center sales, McKesson's dominance in specialty pharma flow — are simultaneously the vulnerability. Growth concentrated in a few enormous customers or a few high-value products is growth in exactly the segments most worth disintermediating. The spread machine's strength and its fragility are the same number read from two directions.

The surprising part

The most disintermediation-proof business is not the biggest — it is Fastenal, whose defense is embarrassingly physical: it owns the vending machine bolted to the customer's factory wall. While C.H. Robinson races to out-automate the platforms and McKesson buys service adjacencies, Fastenal's 45% gross margin rests on the un-sexy fact that ripping out an embedded FMI system and re-sourcing thousands of low-value consumables is more trouble than paying the spread. The lowest-tech moat is the hardest to breach.

Why it matters

Distribution consolidators sit between you and essentials — the drugs at your pharmacy, the parts that keep factories running, the freight that stocks the shelves. When manufacturers or platforms disintermediate them, the savings may reach you or may simply move to whoever is big enough to self-supply, while the small pharmacy or contractor that depended on the distributor's scale gets squeezed out. Who wins the disintermediation fight determines whether essential supply chains stay open to small players or consolidate around the largest.

Every spread machine's growth is concentrated in exactly the customers and products worth disintermediating, so the strongest quarter is also the map of where the crack will open.
