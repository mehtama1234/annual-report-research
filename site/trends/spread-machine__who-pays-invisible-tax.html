The Invisible Tax: Who Actually Pays the Spread Machine

McKesson, Cencora, and Cardinal control 90%+ of U.S. pharma distribution with $902B combined revenue but 1-1.5% operating margins—the signature of a spread-capture system. Energy Transfer's pipelines control 140,000 miles with $18.8-19.1B adjusted EBITDA guidance from tariff-based economics. Republic Services and Waste Management each operate 13-14 million customer accounts where route density creates geography-based monopoly spreads. Ferguson operates 1,746 branches with 31% gross margins serving 500,000+ fragmented contractors. These intermediaries do not innovate; they engineer systems where they profit from every transaction without competing.

Modern supply chains are engineered as spread-capture systems where distributors, pipeline operators, and logistics middlemen extract value not through innovation but through controlled access to essential routes. These embedded costs roll through to consumers in ways that never appear as explicit line items. Spreads are collected billions of times per year at microdoses, making them invisible until they accumulate into bills.

The mechanism

Pharmaceutical distribution operates as a zero-inventory tax. McKesson, Cencora, and Cardinal sit between manufacturers and 50,000+ pharmacies and health systems. Manufacturers cannot sell direct; regulators require traceability and licensed distribution. Distributors capture 1-3% gross margins on commoditized drugs—thin by accounting standards but permanent in practice. Because demand for diabetes drugs or antibiotics is inelastic, price increases do not reduce volume. Distributors pass tariff costs, supply-chain disruption costs, and working-capital costs upstream to pharma manufacturers and downstream to health systems and insurance, but absorb distribution spread volatility internally. The tax is invisible because it is buried in pharmaceutical pricing that consumers see through copays, not as a line item.

Pipeline infrastructure operates as a toll system with regulatory capture. Energy Transfer controls 140,000 miles of natural gas, crude, and NGL pipelines across 44 states. Producers, refiners, and exporters have no alternative routes; regulatory tariffs are determined by cost-plus models that guarantee returns. Energy Transfer's Q4 2025 adjusted EBITDA jumped to $18.8-19.1B after export expansion and long-term commercial contracts locked in throughput. The spread is embedded in per-unit tariffs that load costs onto industrial users (petrochemical plants, exporters, power generators) and eventually consumers via energy bills. Unlike pharma, this spread is quasi-explicit in tariff schedules—but consumers do not see it because pricing happens upstream at industrial scale.

Waste and environmental services operate as geography-based monopoly spreads. Republic Services and Waste Management each operate 13-14 million customer accounts across 1,000+ regional locations. Customers cannot switch providers without changing addresses. Pricing power comes from route density and permit barriers. Republic's 50 basis points of EBITDA margin expansion in recent quarters via 'disciplined pricing' is not innovation; it is rate increases above local inflation. Full-year 2025 adjusted free cash flow of $2.43B on operating cash of $4.30B shows waste spreads are resilient to economic cycles because failure-driven repair and recycle demand is non-discretionary.

Contractor-facing distribution extracts complexity spreads through branch density. Ferguson's 1,746 branches and $31.3B sales serve 37,000 fragmented suppliers and 500,000+ fragmented contractors. Contractors cannot source directly or aggregate purchasing power. Ferguson's 31% gross margin reflects the cost of operating dense local inventory, fleet, branch staff—but also the margin discipline that comes from contractors' inability to aggregate. Ferguson's Q2 2026 8% non-residential growth came from waterworks and capital projects—not because Ferguson innovated, but because contractors still need rapid local fulfillment at reliable price. The spread is the fee for convenience in fragmented markets.

Why now

Regulatory attention and investor data visibility exposed distributor margins without closing them. Cencora's FY2026 Q3 disclosure that GLP-1 growth 'lowered gross-profit margin percentage even as revenue and gross profit grew' revealed volume growth passing through distribution without benefit to the distributor—proving the spread is structurally captured. Cardinal Health's tariff impact (36% of GMPD segment profit in Q3 FY2026) showed costs are pushed to providers. McKesson's Oncology & Multispecialty reaching $14.2B in Q1 FY2027 while the company deepened its hold on the channel proved distributor earnings come from channel control, not operational excellence. Energy Transfer's $18.8-19.1B adjusted EBITDA guidance showed tariff structures were locked in through long-term contracts. The 'why now' is that investor scrutiny, quarterly disclosure, and supply-chain visibility exposed the spread-machine mechanics in filings that previously hid them in consolidated margin.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Pharmaceutical manufacturers are compressed by distributor margins. When Cencora reports GLP-1 growth lowering gross-margin percentage, manufacturers absorbed the margin pressure. Health systems and insurers pay through working-capital drag—they extend payment terms to distributors who finance inventory. The real cost lands on patient copays and insurance premiums. Industrial energy consumers pay through tariff pass-through loaded onto petrochemical plants, refineries, and exporters, which flows to gasoline pumps and heating bills. Waste generators pay through unavoidable rate increases—Republic Services' pricing 'in excess of cost inflation' hits municipalities, retailers, and homeowners. Contractors and small builders pay through margin capture at the branch level—Ferguson's 31% gross margin command flows to construction cost escalation.
Who profits
Spread-machine consolidators concentrate profit: (1) Healthcare distributors (Cencora $321B revenue, McKesson $359.1B, Cardinal $222.6B) report $7-10B in adjusted operating income from being mandatory chokepoints. (2) Pipeline operators (Energy Transfer $18.8-19.1B adjusted EBITDA) capture returns from regulatory approval and physical corridor control. (3) Environmental services (Republic $2.43B adjusted free cash flow, Waste Management peers) capture consistent 28-31% gross margins on recurring waste. (4) Contractor-facing distributors (Ferguson $31.3B sales, 31% gross margin) operate 1,000+ branches profiting from fragmentation. These firms are mission-critical utilities masquerading as competitive businesses—they report ordinary-looking financial returns, but returns are stable and predictable because the spread is embedded in system architecture.

The tension

The spread machine generates tension between system stability and system cost. Manufacturers need distributors for reach; regulators require them for traceability. But every layer adds margin that loads into final prices. Pharma manufacturers cannot raise prices faster than distributor margin inflation without triggering insurance denials or policy intervention. Yet distributor margins are rising because specialty drug volumes and working-capital costs have expanded the spread. Energy Transfer's tariff increases fuel inflation; every basis point of pipeline margin adds to manufacturing and consumer energy bills. Waste rate hikes hit municipal budgets. The political tension is whether these spreads are 'necessary middleman costs' or 'regulatory rents.' Pharma policy assumes distributors are optional; supply-chain resilience assumes they are essential. Energy tariff disputes treat spreads as cost-recovery; antitrust arguments treat them as excessive pricing. Waste pricing debates frame increases as fair; housing-cost advocates see them as regressive taxes.

The surprising part

Distributor profitability is independent of whether demand grows or shrinks. Cencora reports GLP-1 growth 'lowered gross-profit margin percentage' even as absolute gross profit grew—volume passes through distribution while spreads are captured independent of volume direction. McKesson's Oncology & Multispecialty grew to $14.2B not because McKesson innovated, but because specialty manufacturers depend on McKesson's infrastructure. Cardinal's Pharma segment profit jumped 29% while company noted growth came from 'service-platform acquisitions and channel mix'—independent of drug volume. The second surprise is that distributor spreads can expand during recessions. Republic Services' 2025 free cash flow of $2.43B and pricing increases occurred during weak housing and consumer affordability pressure. Waste is non-discretionary; spreads expand when customers have no choice. The third surprise is that infrastructure costs are absorbed by distributors first, then pushed through to manufacturers, providers, and consumers in ways invisible in financial reporting. Cardinal's tariff impact and margin impairment were buried in 'non-GAAP adjustments' and 'business-model recalibration' narrative.

Why it matters

Households and small businesses pay the spread-machine tax in every energy bill, pharmacy copay, garbage collection fee, and construction project cost, yet have no visibility into how much margin is extracted at each layer. A family's heating bill includes pipeline tariffs; a patient's copay includes distributor margin; a contractor's plumbing bill includes Ferguson's branch inventory costs. These spreads are not optional; they are baked into system architecture and protected by regulation or fragmentation. The policy question is whether these spreads represent fair compensation for essential infrastructure or economic rents. If they are rents, then policy (price regulation, forced divestiture, vertical integration restrictions) could lower costs. If they are necessary, interventions would reduce supply reliability. The uncomfortable truth is both are partially true. For infrastructure planning and cost-of-living policy, transparency of spread-machine economics would be the first step toward evidence-based regulation.

Invisible middlemen in pharmaceuticals, energy, waste, and construction distribution have engineered systems where they profit from every transaction without competing—and their spreads load silently into bills that ordinary people pay without knowing the tax is embedded.
