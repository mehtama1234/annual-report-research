The mechanism

For decades, Nielsen was the arbiter of television truth. A network bought Nielsen ratings, advertisers trusted those ratings, and campaigns were built on Nielsen data. Nobody loved Nielsen, but everyone accepted it because there was no alternative. Digital media fragmented that monopoly: now audiences are spread across Google, Meta, Twitch, TikTok, YouTube, Netflix, hundreds of publisher sites, and streaming services. There is no single arbiter. Without a unified measurement standard, advertisers and publishers face a trust problem: How do we agree on value if we do not agree on what counts as a viewer? Comscore emerged to solve that problem by building a cross-platform measurement layer that attempts to count viewers consistently across digital, TV, OTT, and theatrical. FY2025 showed the value of that bet: cross-platform solutions grew 24% even though total revenue was flat and legacy syndicated products declined. Q1 2026 accelerated to 30% growth. Comscore is not growing because it has more data. It is growing because it has become the trusted way to measure in fragmentation.

The economics of measurement trust are unusual. Comscore's FY2025 revenue was $357.5M, flat year-over-year, yet the company raised prices in select areas and grew the cross-platform product at 24%. That should not be possible if demand were simply shifting—the company should be growing overall. Instead, what is happening is margin expansion on higher-value products. Advertisers and publishers are willing to pay more for Comscore measurement than for legacy Nielsen-style products because Comscore can connect the dots across platforms. Similarly, The Trade Desk ($2.896B FY2025 revenue) and Criteo ($1.945B FY2025 revenue) both sell the ability to measure and verify campaign performance across fragmented inventory. They are not selling ads; they are selling trust in measurement and execution. The Trade Desk controls $13.4B in gross platform spend through its decisioning layer, and maintains 95%+ customer retention over 12 consecutive years—proof that control of the arbitration layer is more valuable than controlling the inventory.

Local TV and Gannett offer a different angle on the same mechanism. Gannett manages 186M average unique visitors across local and national news properties, but the real monetization challenge is proving to local advertisers that those visitors matter. Without trusted measurement, a local business might doubt whether their ad actually reached anyone. Comscore's local-TV measurement (growing double digits in FY2025) solved that problem: local publishers can now offer advertisers transparent, credible proof of reach. That measurement layer becomes the basis for pricing and renewal. The winner is not the publisher with the most users—it is the publisher with credible proof of users to advertisers. Comscore's double-digit local TV growth proves that publishers are willing to pay for the ability to prove their value.

Why now

Three factors created the measurement-trust market between 2024 and Q1 2026. First, media fragmentation became mathematically unavoidable and urgent. Nielsen could audit TV ratings because TV was centralized and single-medium. Nobody can audit fragmented digital. By 2024-2025, advertisers faced a crisis: they were spending across 50+ platforms and had no agreed-upon way to measure ROI. Comscore's cross-platform growth (24% FY2025, 30% Q1 2026) reflects the urgency of solving that problem. Second, AI and algorithmic ad targeting changed the nature of proof. Advertisers need to know not just reach but also verification of user quality, fraud risk, and attribution across touchpoints. Traditional syndicated ratings do not answer those questions. Third, regulation around data, privacy, and measurement (GDPR, cookie deprecation, FLoC failure) forced advertisers and publishers to rely on trusted third parties rather than on first-party data alone. That regulatory squeeze created a moat for whoever could build credible cross-platform trust.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Advertisers pay higher costs for Comscore or Trade Desk measurement services because they believe credible measurement is worth the premium. Publishers pay for measurement tools to justify their traffic claims. Platforms like Meta and Google capture most ad spend despite not offering the same third-party validation—they win because they own both advertiser and audience, so they control measurement unilaterally. But for the rest of the ecosystem (independent publishers, DSPs, ad networks), the cost of measurement is now non-negotiable.
Who profits
Comscore profits by becoming the standard for cross-platform measurement. The Trade Desk profits by becoming the control plane for fragmented buying (95%+ retention, $13.4B in controlled spend). Criteo profits by owning commerce-media measurement. The three together demonstrate that measurement trust generates sustainable premium pricing and recurring revenue. In the background, The Trade Desk's lock-in is the strongest because it controls the decisioning layer—where advertiser money actually flows. That is more valuable than data ownership.

The tension

The tension is that third-party measurement layers are useful precisely when they are weak enough that buyers and sellers both need the arbitration. Once Comscore or The Trade Desk becomes too powerful, they risk becoming new monopolies that publishers and advertisers would like to escape. The second tension is that AI-native measurement (based on behavioral data and models) is replacing traditional impressions-based measurement. If Comscore does not evolve from counting impressions to modeling behavior, they become obsolete. The Trade Desk faces the opposite risk: if advertisers build their own decisioning models, the need for Trade Desk's layer diminishes. Both companies are in a race to evolve the measurement layer itself as the underlying media consumption changes.

The surprising part

The most underappreciated fact is that measurement layers are not about getting accurate numbers. They are about getting numbers that all parties trust enough to build transactions on. Comscore's measurement does not need to be more accurate than Nielsen's to be more valuable. It needs to be accepted. The premium customers pay for Comscore (growing 24-30% YoY despite flat total revenue) reflects not confidence in accuracy, but confidence in interoperability and standardization. The second surprise is The Trade Desk's 95%+ retention rate over 12 years—in a media environment that has fundamentally transformed three times over (rise of mobile, rise of video, rise of AI). That retention is only possible because the decisioning layer has become so integrated into workflow that switching costs are prohibitive. Measurement layers are not data tools; they are infrastructure.

Why it matters

Whoever controls measurement controls where advertising money flows. If Comscore or The Trade Desk become trusted enough, they can dictate terms to publishers, advertisers, and platforms. This concentration of measurement power can warp incentives: publishers optimize for what Comscore counts, not for what is best for readers. Advertisers optimize for measurable metrics, not for sustainable business outcomes. The societal stake is whether measurement infrastructure is competitive or concentrated, and whether the infrastructure serves publishers, advertisers, and audiences fairly or concentrates power in the hands of a few gatekeepers.

In a fragmented media environment, advertisers and publishers will pay a premium for measurement they trust, making the arbiter of trust more valuable than the media itself.
