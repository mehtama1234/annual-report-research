The Information Underclass: What Happens to People Who Cannot Afford the Trust Premium

As trusted information becomes premium, non-payers inhabit a low-trust information commons where AI-generated content, contested claims, and brand-unsafe environments dominate—and Gannett's economics show why.

The trust market creates a stratified information environment where those who pay for premium news and analysis get curated, fact-checked information, while everyone else navigates an algorithmically curated commons flooded with AI-generated content and unverified claims. Gannett's $2.3B FY2025 revenue, only 1.5M digital-only subscribers supporting 186M monthly unique visitors, illustrates the structural problem: the vast majority of news consumers are not paying for trust. They are receiving low-trust information subsidized by advertising that increasingly aligns with brand-unsafe content. The tension is epistemic—stratified access to trustworthy information is stratified access to shared reality.

The mechanism

The stratification mechanism works through resource asymmetry. Premium news organizations (Wall Street Journal, Financial Times, New York Times) employ journalists, fact-checkers, and editors who verify information—that costs money recovered through subscription fees ($9.94+ monthly). Non-paying consumers access news through social feeds, algorithmic recommendations, and free news sites relying on advertising. In low-trust commons, advertising dollars drive what content gets surfaced: sensational content, polarizing claims, AI-generated fill, and brand-unsafe material all perform better algorithmically because they drive engagement and clicks. The result is two-tiered information market: wealthy, engaged consumers with subscription access to fact-checked reporting; everyone else consuming algorithmic feeds designed to maximize engagement, not accuracy. Gannett's economics make this concrete. With 186M monthly visitors but only 1.5M digital-only paying subscribers, Gannett's operating model is entirely dependent on advertising. That means Gannett is incentivized to maximize engagement and attract high-value advertisers, not necessarily provide most trustworthy information.

Gannett is caught in structural spiral. Low subscriber penetration (1.5M of 186M visitors = 0.8% conversion) forces reliance on advertising. Advertising reliance forces engagement optimization. Engagement optimization attracts sensational, polarizing, or incomplete content. High-engagement content is low-trust content. Premium brands increasingly avoid brand-unsafe environments—they do not want ads appearing next to misinformation or conspiracy. That leaves Gannett monetizing lower-tier, brand-unsafe advertising, which is less lucrative. Lower ad pricing pushes toward cheaper, AI-generated or low-quality content. The spiral is: low subscriber penetration→advertising reliance→engagement optimization→sensational content→brand flight→lower CPMs→cheaper content→lower quality→lower brand value→inability to command subscription premium→impossible to grow subscribers. Subscribers paying $50-150 annually avoid that spiral entirely. They pay directly for quality; the business has no incentive to compromise.

The competitive displacement from AI-generated content accelerates the mechanism. If Gannett publishes 5 journalist articles and 20 AI-generated or low-cost freelance articles, algorithmic feeds treat them equally—both get surfaced based on engagement signals. The AI article costs 10x less and captures same click revenue. That creates instant pressure to cut journalist costs and increase AI content. Over time, the information commons becomes flooded with AI content that is plausible but unverified. Users cannot distinguish trustworthy journalism from engagement-optimized content. Subscription-paywalled journalism remains fact-checked because readers pay directly for quality. Free, advertising-supported journalism devolves into engagement optimization.

Why now

Three shifts created the trust-market stratification inflection in 2025-2026. First, AI-generated content matured to point where it could credibly fill news sites and social feeds at 1/10th journalist cost. Through 2024-2025, AI text volume made verification increasingly difficult. Second, advertising efficiency metrics shifted from reach to quality; advertisers demanded brand-safety guarantees, which low-tier news sites cannot provide. That pushed advertising dollars toward premium publishers (paywalled) and social platforms, leaving mid-tier news without reliable ad revenue. Third, economic pressure on news organizations (declining print, competition from platforms, falling CPMs) made transition to subscriber-based models necessary. But transition is incomplete—Gannett has only 1.5M subscribers despite 186M visitors. Most news remains in low-trust advertising commons. New York Times proves the model works at scale: 12.78M subscribers at $9.94+ ARPU in Q2 2026, up from $9.72 in Q4 2025, with digital advertising growing 20.7% on premium inventory. The gap between New York Times (premium subscription model) and Gannett (low-subscription advertising model) is the clearest signal of trust stratification.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Consumers who cannot afford or choose not to pay subscription fees ($9.94-150 annually per publication) are forced to consume advertising-supported news, increasingly low-trust. This hits lower-income consumers and younger audiences without established media subscriptions acutely. These consumers are paying not just through reduced information quality; they are paying through reduced political efficacy. If information diet is algorithmically optimized for engagement rather than accuracy, understanding of events is systematically biased toward sensationalism and polarization. Over time, that compounds into political disempowerment. Non-subscribers pay most acutely in jurisdictions where local news has collapsed. Communities without local journalists have no on-the-ground reporting on local government, schools, or civic infrastructure. That information void is filled by national-scale algorithmic feeds or no information at all.
Who profits
Meta, Google, and other social platforms profit because they capture advertising dollars supporting journalism previously. They host user-generated and AI-generated content at near-zero marginal cost. Premium news organizations (Financial Times, Wall Street Journal, New York Times) profit through subscription revenue by building paywalls that gate trusted information. New York Times' 12.78M subscribers at $9.94+ ARPU represent a profitable business model. Tech-enabled news aggregators and AI-generated content platforms profit by surfacing engaging content without paying journalists. Regional and mid-tier news organizations like Gannett are caught in middle—too large to be niche, too untrustworthy to command subscription premiums. Capital markets have essentially written off Gannett and similar mid-tier publishers because transition to subscriber economics is incomplete.

The tension

The tension is that trust market stratification damages shared reality. If wealthy and educated cohorts consume fact-checked information while lower-income and younger cohorts consume engagement-optimized information, they are not operating from same baseline understanding of events. Over time, that divergence makes democratic deliberation increasingly difficult. Second, the tension is regulatory: should access to trustworthy information be a public good or a private good subject to market pricing? Historically, trusted information was subsidized through advertising and education. Now it is priced as luxury subscription. Third, the tension is existential for mid-tier news: they cannot simultaneously serve audiences for free (requiring low-trust, high-engagement content) and maintain advertiser trust (requiring brand-safe, low-engagement content). That contradiction is tearing organizations apart.

The surprising part

The non-obvious part is that the stratification is not primarily a result of paywalls; it is a result of advertising dynamics. Gannett does not have large paywall because business model economics do not support one. The company needed mass reach to justify advertising inventory, but mass reach in algorithmic environment means competing on engagement, which means low-trust content, which means advertisers flee. The paywall cannot save Gannett because only 1.5M of 186M visitors would pay. This is structural economics problem, not strategy problem. Company could execute paywalls perfectly and still fail because advertising-supported commons is so large that paywalled premium cannot compete on reach. The surprise is that stratification is systemic, not avoidable.

Why it matters

Stratified information access stratifies democratic deliberation and political efficacy. A society where wealthy cohorts have access to fact-checked information and lower-income cohorts consume algorithmically optimized engagement-driven content is a society with divergent understandings of shared reality. Over time, that divergence makes collective decision-making increasingly difficult and makes political polarization more likely. The social stake is whether information can remain a public good (publicly subsidized, universally accessible) or whether it necessarily becomes a luxury good (privately produced, priced for those who can afford it). History suggests societies with stratified information access tend toward authoritarian outcomes because shared reality collapses.

When trusted information requires paid subscription, those who cannot afford or choose not to pay are systematically relegated to a low-trust information commons dominated by algorithmic engagement optimization and AI-generated content.
