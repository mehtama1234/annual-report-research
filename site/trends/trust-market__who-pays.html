The Information Underclass: What Happens to People Who Cannot Afford the Trust Premium

As trusted information becomes premium, non-payers inhabit a low-trust information commons where AI-generated content, contested claims, and brand-unsafe environments dominate—and Gannett's economics show why.

The trust market creates a stratified information environment where those who pay for premium news and analysis get curated, fact-checked information, while everyone else navigates an algorithmically curated commons flooded with AI-generated content and unverified claims. Gannett's $2.3B FY2025 revenue, declining print subscriptions, and only 1.5M digital-only subscribers supporting 186M monthly unique visitors illustrates the business model: the vast majority of news consumers are not paying for trust; they are receiving low-trust information subsidized by advertising that increasingly aligns with brand-unsafe content. The tension is not just economic; it is epistemic—stratified access to trustworthy information is stratified access to shared reality.

The mechanism

The stratification mechanism works through resource asymmetry. Premium news organizations (Wall Street Journal, Financial Times, New York Times) employ journalists, fact-checkers, and editors who verify information—that costs money, and those costs are recovered through subscription fees ($50-150 annually). Non-paying consumers access news through social feeds, algorithmic recommendations, and free news sites that rely on advertising. In the low-trust commons, advertising dollars drive what content gets surfaced: sensational content, polarizing claims, AI-generated fill, and brand-unsafe material all perform better in algorithmic feeds because they drive engagement and clicks. The result is a two-tiered information market: wealthy, engaged consumers with subscription access to fact-checked reporting; everyone else consuming algorithmic feeds designed to maximize engagement, not accuracy.

Gannett's economics make this concrete. With 186M monthly unique visitors but only 1.5M digital-only paying subscribers, Gannett's operating model is entirely dependent on advertising. That means Gannett is incentivized to maximize engagement and attract high-value advertisers, not necessarily to provide the most trustworthy information. High-engagement content is sensational, polarizing, or incomplete. High-value advertising is premium-brand advertising. But premium brands increasingly avoid brand-unsafe environments—they do not want their ads appearing next to misinformation or conspiracy content. That leaves Gannett monetizing lower-tier, brand-unsafe advertising, which is less lucrative and drives content toward even lower-trust coverage. The spiral is structural: low subscriber penetration (1.5M) forces reliance on advertising; advertising reliance forces engagement optimization; engagement optimization attracts brand-unsafe audiences; brand-unsafe audiences drive down ad pricing; lower ad pricing pushes toward cheaper, AI-generated or low-quality content. Subscribers paying $50-150 annually avoid that spiral entirely.

The competitive displacement from AI-generated content accelerates the mechanism. If Gannett publishes 5 articles written by journalists and 20 articles generated by AI or low-cost freelancers, algorithmic feeds treat them equally—both get surfaced based on engagement signals. But the AI-generated article costs 10x less and captures the same click revenue. That creates instant pressure to cut journalist costs and increase AI content. Over time, the information commons becomes flooded with AI content that is plausible but unverified, and users cannot easily distinguish between trustworthy journalism and content generated to maximize engagement. Subscription-paywalled journalism remains fact-checked because paywalled readers pay directly for quality; free, advertising-supported journalism devolves into engagement optimization.

Why now

Three shifts created the trust-market stratification inflection. First, AI-generated content matured to the point where it could credibly fill news sites and social feeds at 1/10th the cost of journalism. Through 2024-2025, the volume of AI-generated text made verification increasingly difficult—if 50% of news-site content is AI-generated and indistinguishable from human-written copy, trust signals fail. Second, advertising efficiency metrics shifted from reach to quality; advertisers began demanding brand-safety guarantees, which low-tier news sites cannot provide. That pushed advertising dollars toward premium publishers (paywalled) and social platforms, leaving Gannett-style mid-tier news without reliable ad revenue. Third, economic pressure on news organizations (declining print, competition from social platforms, falling ad CPMs) made the transition to subscriber-based models necessary for survival. But the transition is incomplete—Gannett still only has 1.5M subscribers despite 186M monthly visitors—which means most news remains in the low-trust advertising commons.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Consumers who cannot afford or choose not to pay subscription fees ($50-150 annually per publication) are forced to consume advertising-supported news, which is increasingly low-trust. That is most acutely felt by lower-income consumers and younger audiences without established media subscriptions. These consumers are not just paying through reduced information quality; they are paying through reduced political efficacy—if your information diet is algorithmically optimized for engagement rather than accuracy, your understanding of events is systematically biased toward sensationalism and polarization. Over time, that compounds into political disempowerment. Non-subscribers are paying the most acutely in jurisdictions where local news has collapsed: communities without local journalists have no on-the-ground reporting on local government, schools, or civic infrastructure. That information void is filled by national-scale algorithmic feeds or by no information at all.
Who profits
Meta, Google, and other social platforms profit because they capture advertising dollars that used to support news organizations; they host user-generated and AI-generated content at near-zero marginal cost. Premium news organizations (Financial Times, Wall Street Journal, New York Times) profit through subscription revenue by building paywalls that gate trusted information. Tech-enabled news aggregators and AI-generated content platforms profit by surfacing engaging content without paying journalists. Regional and mid-tier news organizations like Gannett are caught in the middle—too large to be niche, too untrustworthy (due to advertising-driven content) to command subscription premiums. Capital markets have essentially written off Gannett and similar mid-tier publishers because the transition to subscriber economics is incomplete.

The tension

The tension is that the trust market's stratification damages shared reality. If wealthy and educated cohorts consume fact-checked information while lower-income and younger cohorts consume engagement-optimized information, they are not operating from the same baseline understanding of events. Over time, that divergence makes democratic deliberation increasingly difficult—voting populations cannot agree on basic facts if they are consuming from stratified information systems. Second, the tension is regulatory: should access to trustworthy information be a public good (like roads or schools) or a private good subject to market pricing? Historically, trusted information was subsidized through advertising and education; now it is priced as a luxury subscription. Third, the tension is existential for mid-tier news organizations: they cannot simultaneously serve audiences for free (which requires low-trust, high-engagement content) and maintain advertiser trust (which requires brand-safe, low-engagement content). That contradiction is tearing organizations apart.

The surprising part

The non-obvious part is that the stratification is not primarily a result of paywalls; it is a result of advertising dynamics. Gannett does not have a large paywall because the business model economics do not support one—the company needed mass reach to justify advertising inventory, but mass reach in an algorithmic environment means competing on engagement, which means low-trust content, which means advertisers flee. The paywall cannot save Gannett because only 1.5M of 186M visitors would pay. This is a structural economics problem, not a strategy problem. The company could execute paywalls perfectly and still fail because the advertising-supported commons is so large that paywalled premium cannot compete on reach. The surprise is that the stratification is systemic, not avoidable.

Why it matters

Stratified information access stratifies democratic deliberation and political efficacy. A society where wealthy cohorts have access to fact-checked, curated information and lower-income cohorts consume algorithmically optimized, engagement-driven content is a society with divergent understandings of shared reality. Over time, that divergence makes collective decision-making increasingly difficult and makes political polarization more likely. The social stake is whether information can remain a public good (publicly subsidized, universally accessible) or whether it necessarily becomes a luxury good (privately produced, priced for those who can afford it). History suggests that societies with stratified information access tend toward authoritarian outcomes because shared reality collapses.

When trusted information requires paid subscription, those who cannot afford or choose not to pay are systematically relegated to a low-trust information commons dominated by algorithmic engagement optimization and AI-generated content.
