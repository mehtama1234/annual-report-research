Distribution as Infrastructure

Distribution density becomes structural moat and access rent. Cencora, Cardinal Health, DNOW, and Ferguson control the physical and logistical infrastructure that connects upstream supply to downstream users. That control generates recurring fees that scale with the network, not with market growth.

Cencora's $321.3B FY2025 revenue, $74.9B U.S. Healthcare Solutions revenue, and specialty-services expansion show how pharmaceutical distribution is becoming infrastructure—not a commodity pass-through. The company sits between manufacturers and pharmacy chains, physician practices and patients, controlling timing, access, pricing, and information flow. That infrastructure generates recurring revenue streams independent of underlying drug consumption growth. Specialty depth (oncology, complex therapies) and services (logistics, consulting, data) all increase the rent collected per unit of physical distribution.

The mechanism

Distribution-as-infrastructure operates through four reinforcing mechanisms. First, network density moat: Cencora has distribution centers, pharmacies, physician practices, and patient relationships across the entire U.S. healthcare system. That density cannot be replicated—it is a sunk-cost barrier. Second, switching costs and embedment: Cencora's systems are embedded in pharmacy workflows, physician EHRs, payer networks, and patient information flows. Switching distribution partners requires operational disruption that costs pharmacies and practices more than staying with Cencora. Third, information layer monopoly: Cencora sits at the chokepoint where patient demand, pharmacy inventory, physician prescribing, and payer claims all flow. That information is valuable; Cencora captures it and uses it for consulting, forecasting, and targeted services. Fourth, specialty and services expansion: as Cencora moves into specialty pharmacy, oncology (OneOncology), and consulting services, it captures higher margins and locks customers into a broader relationship. Specialty products require specialized handling and logistics; general distribution competitors cannot easily enter that space.

The rent extraction shows in the economics. Cencora's U.S. Healthcare Solutions segment grew 4.9% in Q3 FY2026 and 2.9% in Q2, which is modest growth. Yet adjusted EPS was $4.48 and $4.75 in those same quarters, implying healthy profit margins. That margin comes not from growth, but from infrastructure: Cencora moves products across a network it owns, collects fees from manufacturers, pharmacies, and payers for access and logistics, and captures information rent through consulting. The business works regardless of whether drug consumption grows or shrinks.

The tension emerges in commoditization and regulatory pressure. If distribution becomes pure logistics (moving products cheaper and faster), margins compress and the business becomes commodity. GLP-1 volume in Cencora's figures shows this risk: GLP-1 sales grew topline, but gross margins were pressured because GLP-1 is relatively low-margin and high-volume. If GLP-1 becomes a larger share of business, margin compression follows. Regulatory risk is also present: drug-pricing regulation could constrain manufacturer profitability or payer willingness to pay, reducing the total rent available for distributors. Opioid litigation created similar headwinds for earlier generations of distributors.

Why now

Distribution-as-infrastructure is apparent now because drug pipelines have matured and growth is slowing. The previous playbook was: new drug launches drive volume, distributors grow with it. That playbook worked in the 2000s-2010s. Now, pipeline growth is slower, and competition (biosimilars, generics) erodes pricing power. Distributors responded by deepening integration and moving up the value chain: Cencora acquired OneOncology to embed physician relationships; expanded specialty services to capture higher margins; built consulting and data products to create recurring revenue independent of product volume. The infrastructure strategy only becomes apparent when volume growth slows enough that pricing and rent extraction matter more than topline expansion.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Manufacturers pay for access to distribution channels (shelf space, customer relationships, data insights). Pharmacies and physician practices pay for logistics, supply-chain reliability, information systems, and consulting. Payers absorb some distribution costs through drug pricing. Patients may face higher drug prices if distribution markups are passed through.
Who profits
Scale distribution operators (Cencora, Cardinal, McKesson) that control network density and information flow. Specialty and services providers embedded in those networks. Private equity investors who consolidate regional distributors into national platforms. Healthcare systems that own their own distribution infrastructure.

The tension

The tension is between infrastructure monopoly and regulatory risk. As Cencora consolidates more of the distribution network and specialty services, it becomes powerful enough to trigger regulatory scrutiny. If drug-pricing regulation limits manufacturer profitability or payer willingness to pay, the total rent available to distributors shrinks. If Amazon or other tech companies enter distribution (as they have begun with pharmacy), the incumbents lose network moat. Cencora's OneOncology acquisition and specialty expansion are defensive moves—deepening moat before disruption happens.

The surprising part

The surprising element is how invisible the distribution infrastructure has been to investors. Cencora is one of the largest healthcare companies by revenue, yet it is less visible culturally than drug makers or health plans. The surprise is that this invisible infrastructure is now the more durable business model than visible-brand drug manufacturing. As drug innovation slows and pipelines commoditize, the winners are companies that control access, logistics, and information, not companies that own manufacturing or intellectual property.

Why it matters

An economy where distribution infrastructure is concentrated in a few companies creates dependency and potential for rent extraction. If Cencora controls drug access and pricing across the nation, it has leverage over consumers, providers, and manufacturers. That concentration could drive prices higher if not regulated, or create fragility if the infrastructure fails. The positive: Cencora's infrastructure enables efficient drug delivery; the negative: that efficiency comes with concentrated power.
