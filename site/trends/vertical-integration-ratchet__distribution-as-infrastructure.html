Trends · The Vertical Integration Ratchet

The Middleman Who Became the System

Distribution operators have embedded themselves so deeply into customer workflows that they have stopped being replaceable and started charging access rents—converting what was once a service margin into a structural moat.

Cencora, DNOW, Ferguson, and Cardinal Health have each followed the same playbook: acquire enough density, embed deeply enough into customer workflows through branch networks and service attachments, and the middleman stops being a commodity service and becomes critical infrastructure that customers cannot unwind. Cencora's acquisition of Retina Consultants of America and moves into specialty healthcare services. DNOW's transformation of MRC Global into an integrated PVF and energy-infrastructure distributor. Ferguson's build of 1,729 branches across plumbing, HVAC, and waterworks that make it operationally impossible to leave. Cardinal Health's expansion into specialty pharmaceuticals and at-home solutions. Each case shows that once a distributor achieves critical density and embeds its systems into customer operations, it stops competing on price and starts collecting rent on structural dependence.

The mechanism

Distribution has historically been treated as a thin-margin commodity business: buy from manufacturers, transport to customers, take whatever spread the market allows. But modern distribution has become something different—a deeply embedded system that controls access, timing, inventory, technical support, and workflow integration for customers who have no viable alternative supplier. Cencora operates 321 billion dollars of pharmaceutical distribution—not because it is cheaper than competitors, but because hospitals, health systems, and pharmacies cannot unwind from it without disrupting care. The company has moved beyond moving boxes to providing specialty services, oncology support, market-access solutions, and provider-facing platforms. Cardinal Health reports $222.6 billion in revenue and has embedded itself into hospital procurement, pharmacy operations, lab logistics, and home-care supply chains. These are not services customers shop for quarterly. They are operational systems customers depend on for continuity.

The density creates lock-in. Ferguson operates 1,729 branches across North America, serving about 37,000 suppliers to fragmented contractor bases. A plumber or HVAC contractor does not shop for distribution. They buy from Ferguson because Ferguson is local, carries inventory, offers technical support, and can get a replacement valve or pump in hours rather than days. Switching to another distributor would mean rebuilding relationships, reconfiguring procurement systems, and finding alternative sources for hundreds of SKUs. DNOW's acquisition of MRC Global created a 300-location platform that controls access to PVF, valves, pumps, instrumentation, and automation systems across upstream, midstream, gas utilities, data centers, and industrial process markets. Neither a gas utility nor a petrochemical plant can easily shop around. The distributor's local presence and integrated systems have become infrastructure.

The structural moat manifests as pricing power and service attachment. Cencora can charge premium fees for specialty pharmaceutical services, oncology programs, and provider-facing tools because alternatives do not exist at equivalent scale. Cardinal Health's non-GAAP operating earnings increased 38% in Q2 FY2026 as the company expanded MSO platform acquisitions, specialized pharma margins, and broader service channels. Ferguson raised mid-quarter 2026 guidance to mid-single-digit sales growth while maintaining adjusted operating margin of 9.5-9.8% despite residential weakness, precisely because its non-residential and waterworks channels—where it holds dense infrastructure—remained pricing-disciplined. The distributor is not fighting for each order. It is collecting rent on customer dependence.

Why now

Distribution is consolidating into larger, more integrated platforms at the same time that customers are becoming more fragmented and technically specialized. A contractor, a health system, or a gas utility can no longer support in-house procurement, logistics, and technical support infrastructure. They need a third party to do it. Simultaneously, the distributors that own the densest networks and the most integrated systems are acquiring competitors and adjacent services to make themselves more entrenched. Cencora's acquisition of Retina Consultants extends its reach into physician services. DNOW's $1.763B acquisition of MRC Global in November 2025 created a combined platform with immediate synergies of $23M (35% above target) and $70M expected over three years. Ferguson completed eight acquisitions with annualized revenue above $300M in calendar 2025 and signed a deal for FloWorks, a technical valve-and-flow-control distributor, in Q2 2026. Cardinal Health is expanding at-home solutions, nuclear and precision health services, and logistics. Each acquisition makes it harder for customers to leave and easier for the distributor to cross-sell services within its ecosystem. The lock-in is accelerating. The timing also reflects capital discipline among the largest operators. Cencora generates $3.0B of adjusted free cash flow, returning $900M to shareholders while investing in growth. Ferguson maintains net debt leverage of 1.1x while completing multiple acquisitions per quarter. DNOW is repurchasing stock at $75M year to date despite a massive merger integration. Cardinal Health deployed capital toward debt repayment and buybacks even while absorbing tariff pressure and service-platform integration. These are companies with sufficient structural moats to self-fund expansion and return capital simultaneously—the mark of companies extracting significant rents from their position.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
End customers—hospitals, pharmacies, contractors, gas utilities, petrochemical plants, and industrial operators—pay for the distribution moat through higher service costs and limited alternatives. A hospital cannot renegotiate terms with Cencora because Cencora connects them to pharmaceutical supply, specialty oncology services, provider IT support, and logistics systems that have become operationally indispensable. A contractor cannot leave Ferguson without losing local inventory availability, technical support, and integrated procurement systems. A gas utility cannot bypass DNOW/MRC Global without rebuilding supplier relationships and losing access to engineered solutions and automation capabilities. The customers have absorbed the costs of switching so thoroughly that negotiating room has evaporated. Smaller competitors and new entrants cannot afford the capital intensity and density required to compete, so the market has sorted into a handful of large integrated platforms. Manufacturers also pay. Pharmaceutical companies, pump makers, and valve manufacturers depend on Cencora, Cardinal Health, DNOW, and Ferguson to reach their end customers. Once the distributor achieves critical density, it has pricing power over manufacturers too. It can demand rebates, co-op funding, marketing support, and favorable terms because manufacturers have no alternative channel to reach hospitals, contractors, and utilities efficiently.
Who profits
The distribution platform owners—Cencora, Cardinal Health, DNOW, Ferguson, and similar consolidated distributors—capture the rent from structural dependence. Cencora's adjusted operating income of $4.2B on $321B revenue represents durable margin locked in through network effects and switching costs. Ferguson's 9.6% adjusted operating margin on $31.3B of sales, maintained even during residential weakness, proves that pricing power is insulated from commodity cycles. Cardinal Health's non-GAAP operating earnings growth of 38% in Q2 FY2026 shows how service attachment and margin mix shift value toward the platform. DNOW's first-year MRC synergies of $23M, with $70M expected cumulatively, demonstrate that larger scale and integrated operations compound value. All four companies are returning capital to shareholders while self-funding growth, which is the hallmark of operators that have moved beyond competition into rent extraction. The secondary winner is any company that can attach higher-value services to the distribution base—whether that is specialty pharmaceutical support (Cencora), at-home healthcare solutions (Cardinal Health), automated valve and flow-control systems (DNOW), or technical contractor tools (Ferguson). Once the density exists, layering on services multiplies the moat.

The tension

The model assumes that customers cannot defect and manufacturers cannot build alternative channels. But both assumptions face pressure. Manufacturers increasingly want direct relationships with large end-users to capture more margin and control brand positioning. Digital and e-commerce channels threaten to disintermediate traditional distribution if customers can order directly and accept longer lead times. Tariffs and supply-chain disruption can shift the balance of power toward customers who have alternative sources or can absorb longer fulfillment cycles. Additionally, the profitability attracts new competition. Amazon, regional supply chains, and specialized digital players are investing in distribution infrastructure. Consolidation also reduces diversity of suppliers, which creates regulatory risk. Cencora's sheer size ($321B revenue) and healthcare criticality invite antitrust scrutiny. DNOW's rapid integration after a massive acquisition creates execution risk. If any of these large distributors stumble on integration, margin quality, or customer service, the structural moat weakens because customers suddenly have windows to explore alternatives. Finally, the model assumes that density forever requires physical branches and local presence. But technology and automation could reshape that equation. If fulfillment can be handled by regional hubs and drones rather than by 1,729 branches, Ferguson's physical density becomes less valuable. The moat is structural until it isn't.

The surprising part

The most non-obvious insight is that these companies are not competing on efficiency or cost. Ferguson's operating margin did not improve because it cut costs; it improved because pricing power increased. Cencora's operating income grew faster than revenue because it was able to mix higher-margin services (specialty pharma, physician support, data) into the distribution base. DNOW is paying multiples on the MRC Global acquisition despite integration chaos because the combined platform will have higher pricing power. These are monopolistic moats dressed up as logistics companies. The surprise is that distribution has become not just a low-margin intermediary but a high-moat, high-margin-expansion story that rivals technology companies in pricing discipline. A healthcare system or contractor cannot defect from their distributor any more easily than a software customer can defect from enterprise software they have built their workflow around. The underlying economics of physical distribution should favor thin margins and commodity competition. Instead, consolidation and density have inverted that: the largest distributors are extracting rents more like platform monopolies than like service providers.

Why it matters

This matters because it reshapes the economics of entire downstream industries. Hospitals pay higher drug costs when Cencora controls access. Contractors' profit margins compress when Ferguson controls supply timing and availability. Gas utilities' capital planning gets constrained when DNOW controls critical infrastructure. Industrial plants face higher operational costs when distributors control spare parts and technical support. The consolidation of distribution into a handful of platforms means that cost pressures flow downward to end-users rather than being absorbed by competitive pressure on the middleman. If healthcare systems, contractors, or industrial operators want to challenge prices, they have nowhere else to turn. For households, the impact is indirect but real: higher healthcare costs driven by pharmaceutical distribution rents, higher construction and repair costs driven by contractor supply constraints, higher energy bills driven by midstream infrastructure control. Additionally, the consolidation reduces industry dynamism. Smaller distributors cannot compete with large integrated platforms, which limits job creation and innovation in the distribution layer itself. For workers, consolidation means fewer independent opportunities and more exposure to large corporate operators with less local autonomy.

Distribution has stopped being a commodity service and become a structural moat by embedding itself so deeply into customer workflows that leaving is operationally impossible and economically irrational.
