Trends · The Vertical Integration Ratchet

The Middleman Who Became the System

Distribution operators have stopped being replaceable service providers and become structural moats by embedding so deeply into customer operations that leaving is operationally impossible. They now charge access rents instead of competing on margin.

Cencora, Cardinal Health, Ferguson, and DNOW have each followed the same playbook: acquire enough density, embed deeply into customer workflows through branch networks and service attachments, and the middleman stops being a commodity and becomes critical infrastructure that customers cannot unwind without operational collapse. Cencora's pharmaceutical distribution ($321B revenue) plus specialty services and physician-practice acquisitions. Cardinal Health's $222.6B platform spanning pharma, medical products, and at-home solutions. Ferguson's 1,729 North American branches making it operationally impossible for contractors to leave. DNOW's November 2025 acquisition of MRC Global creating a 300-location PVF and energy-infrastructure platform. Each case proves that once a distributor achieves critical density and embeds its systems into customer procurement and logistics, it stops competing on price and starts collecting rent on structural dependence.

The mechanism

Distribution historically meant thin margins: buy from manufacturers, move inventory, take whatever spread the market allows. Modern consolidation has inverted that equation. Cencora operates $321 billion of pharmaceutical distribution annually not because it costs less to use them, but because hospitals and pharmacies have built their entire procurement, specialty-access, and oncology-support systems inside Cencora's platform. Unwinding means disrupting patient care continuity. The company has embedded itself by layering specialty pharmaceutical services, physician-practice acquisitions (Retina Consultants of America, OneOncology), market-access solutions, and provider-facing platforms on top of commodity distribution.

Cardinal Health's $222.6 billion platform shows the same pattern across pharma, medical products, and logistics. The company's Q2 FY2026 earnings increased 38% to $877 million not because Cardinal moved more volume, but because it was able to mix higher-margin specialty drugs, MSO acquisitions, and service-platform revenue into a base that hospitals and pharmacies had already embedded into their operations. A health system cannot renegotiate procurement with Cardinal without rebuilding supplier relationships, retraining staff, and disrupting workflows that depend on integrated systems for pharmacy operations, lab logistics, and home-care supply chains. Switching costs are not financial; they are operational and existential.

Ferguson's 1,729 branches across North America prove that density itself becomes a moat. A contractor does not shop for distribution quarterly. They buy from Ferguson because Ferguson is local, carries inventory for 37,000 different suppliers, offers technical support, and can deliver a replacement valve or pump in hours rather than days. Switching means rebuilding hundreds of supplier relationships, reconfiguring procurement systems, and losing access to integrated technical support. Ferguson maintained a 9.6% adjusted operating margin on $31.3 billion of calendar 2025 sales despite residential weakness precisely because pricing power on non-residential and waterworks channels is insulated from commodity cycles. The branch network is not a cost center; it is a pricing weapon.

DNOW's November 2025 acquisition of MRC Global for $1.763 billion created a 300-location platform controlling access to PVF, valves, pumps, automation systems, and instrumentation across upstream oil, gas utilities, data centers, and industrial process markets. Gas utilities and petrochemical plants cannot easily shop around. The distributor's local presence and integrated sourcing systems have become critical infrastructure. DNOW's first-year synergies of $23 million came in 35% above target and proved that integrating a massive acquisition was working despite early ERP transition chaos. By Q2 2026, the combined platform had U.S. midstream revenue at a $1B annualized run rate for the first time, showing that pricing power extended across the merged portfolio.

The structural moat manifests as pricing discipline divorced from cost. Cencora's fiscal 2025 adjusted operating income of $4.2 billion on $321 billion revenue represents a durably locked-in margin protected by network effects and switching costs. Cardinal Health's service-attachment strategy—MSO platforms, specialty pharma, logistics channels—is explicitly multiplying margin mix rather than competing on low-cost volume. Ferguson raised Q2 2026 guidance to mid-single-digit sales growth while simultaneously maintaining adjusted operating margin guidance of 9.5-9.8% because contractors have nowhere else to turn. DNOW is not fighting for each order; it is collecting rent on customer dependence built into operations.

Why now

Distribution consolidation accelerated into a visible inflection point between 2024 and mid-2026. The MRC Global acquisition (November 2025, $1.763B) followed years of strategic bolt-on deals by Ferguson (eight acquisitions above $300M annualized revenue in calendar 2025 alone) and Cardinal Health's accelerating MSO and specialty-pharma strategy (non-GAAP operating earnings +38% in Q2 FY2026). Simultaneously, customers—hospitals, contractors, gas utilities—became more fragmented technically and operationally. A modern health system cannot manage specialty-drug supply, oncology access, and home-care logistics without a third-party infrastructure partner. A gas utility cannot maintain internal PVF sourcing at the scale and speed DNOW now provides. Contractors increasingly depend on local branch inventory and technical support rather than building procurement capacity in-house. As customer complexity grew, the distributors that achieved the densest networks and most integrated service platforms became not just vendors but operational necessities. Cencora's acquisition of Retina Consultants (January 2025) and OneOncology embedded it into physician services and oncology supply chains, making the platform more entrenched with each add-on. Ferguson's pattern of eight major acquisitions in 2025 plus the FloWorks deal in Q2 2026 shows management escalating density into higher-complexity channels (technical valves, flow control, instrumentation) where switching costs compound. The timing also reflects capital discipline among the largest operators. Cencora generates $3.0B of adjusted free cash flow while returning $900M to shareholders and funding growth. Ferguson maintains net debt leverage of 1.1x while completing multiple acquisitions per quarter. DNOW repurchased $75M in stock year to date despite integration complexity. These are companies with sufficient structural moats to simultaneously self-fund expansion, return capital, and absorb massive mergers—the unmistakable mark of operators extracting significant rents from structural dependence.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
End customers pay through limited alternatives and higher service costs. Hospitals renegotiate poorly with Cencora because Cencora connects them to pharmaceutical supply, specialty oncology services, provider IT support, and logistics systems that have become operationally indispensable. Contractors cannot leave Ferguson without losing local inventory availability, technical support, and procurement integration that serve 37,000 suppliers across 1,729 branches—reconstructing that network would take years. Gas utilities cannot bypass DNOW's 300-location platform without rebuilding PVF, automation, and instrumentation supplier relationships and losing access to integrated midstream and gas-utility solutions that hit $1B in annualized run rate. Smaller competitors cannot afford the capital intensity and density required to compete. The market has sorted into a handful of large integrated platforms with no viable alternatives. Manufacturers also pay pricing pressure. Pharmaceutical companies, pump makers, valve manufacturers, and component suppliers depend on Cencora, Cardinal Health, DNOW, and Ferguson to reach end customers efficiently. Once the distributor achieves critical density, it has negotiating leverage over manufacturers for rebates, co-op funding, and favorable terms because manufacturers have no alternative channel to reach hospitals, contractors, and utilities at equivalent scale.
Who profits
The distribution platform owners capture the rent from structural dependence. Cencora's adjusted operating income of $4.2B on $321B revenue (1.3% margin, durably locked in through network effects and switching costs) proves the model. Ferguson's 9.6% adjusted operating margin on $31.3B of calendar 2025 sales—maintained even during residential weakness—shows that pricing power is insulated from commodity cycles. Cardinal Health's non-GAAP operating earnings increased 38% in Q2 FY2026 to $877 million, demonstrating how service attachment and margin-mix shift value toward the platform owner rather than improving returns to manufacturers or hospitals. DNOW's first-year MRC synergies of $23M, with $70M expected cumulatively, prove that integrating a massive acquisition is compounding value precisely because the combined platform has higher pricing power. All four companies are returning capital to shareholders (Cencora $900M, Ferguson $236M repurchase plus $2B authorization, DNOW $75M year to date) while self-funding growth—the hallmark of operators that have moved beyond competition into rent extraction. Secondary winners are companies that attach higher-value services to the distribution base: Cencora's specialty pharmaceutical support, Cardinal Health's at-home healthcare and MSO acquisitions, DNOW's automated valve and controls (Edge Controls, $46M acquisition February 2026), Ferguson's technical valve and flow-control expansion (FloWorks deal Q2 2026). Once the density exists, layering on specialized services multiplies the moat and margin.

The tension

The model assumes customers cannot defect and manufacturers cannot build alternative channels, but both assumptions face real pressure. Manufacturers increasingly want direct relationships with large end-users to capture more margin and control brand positioning. E-commerce and digital sourcing threaten traditional distribution if customers can order online and accept longer lead times. Amazon is investing in distribution infrastructure. Regional supply chains and specialized digital players are testing whether they can bypass the incumbents. Tariffs and supply-chain disruption can shift power toward customers with alternative sources. Additionally, profitability attracts new competition. Consolidation also reduces supplier diversity, creating regulatory risk. Cencora's sheer size ($321B revenue) and healthcare criticality invite antitrust scrutiny. DNOW's rapid integration after a massive acquisition creates execution risk. If any of these large distributors stumble on integration, margin quality, or service levels, the structural moat weakens because customers suddenly have windows to explore alternatives. Finally, the model assumes that density forever requires physical branches and local presence. Technology and automation could reshape that equation. If fulfillment can be handled by regional hubs and drones rather than by 1,729 branches, Ferguson's physical density becomes less valuable. The moat is structural until technology or capital pressure breaks it.

The surprising part

These companies are not competing on efficiency or cost—they are competing on lack of alternatives and pricing power. Ferguson's operating margin did not improve because it cut costs; it improved because its pricing power increased on channels (non-residential, waterworks, large capital projects) where customers have no alternative supplier and need rapid fulfillment. Cencora's operating income grew faster than revenue because it mixed higher-margin specialty pharma, physician support, and data services into the distribution base, not because it became more efficient at moving boxes. DNOW is paying acquisition multiples on MRC Global despite integration chaos because the combined platform will have higher pricing power over gas utilities and petrochemical plants that depend on integrated PVF, automation, and instrumentation sourcing. Cardinal Health's 38% operating earnings growth in Q2 FY2026 came from service attachment and margin mix, not volume leverage. These are monopolistic moats dressed up as logistics companies. The surprise is that distribution has become not just a low-margin intermediary but a high-moat, high-margin-expansion story that rivals technology companies in pricing discipline. A healthcare system or contractor cannot defect from their distributor any more easily than a software customer can defect from enterprise software they built their operations around. The underlying economics of physical distribution should favor thin margins and commodity competition. Instead, consolidation and density have inverted that: the largest distributors are extracting rents more like platform monopolies than like service providers competing on cost.

Why it matters

This reshapes the economics of entire downstream industries. Hospitals pay higher drug costs when Cencora controls access to specialty pharmaceuticals and physician services. Contractors' profit margins compress when Ferguson controls supply timing, branch availability, and technical support across 1,729 locations. Gas utilities' capital planning gets constrained when DNOW controls access to integrated PVF, automation, and instrumentation sourcing. Industrial operators face higher operational costs when distributors control spare parts and technical support. The consolidation of distribution into a handful of platforms means cost pressures flow downward to end-users rather than being absorbed by competitive pressure on the middleman. If healthcare systems, contractors, or industrial operators want to challenge prices, they have nowhere else to turn. For households, the impact is indirect but real: higher healthcare costs driven by pharmaceutical distribution rents, higher construction and repair costs driven by contractor supply constraints, higher energy bills driven by midstream infrastructure control. Additionally, consolidation reduces industry dynamism. Smaller distributors cannot compete with large integrated platforms, which limits job creation and innovation in the distribution layer. For workers, consolidation means fewer independent opportunities and more exposure to large corporate operators with less local autonomy.

Distribution has stopped being a commodity service and become a structural moat by embedding itself so deeply into customer workflows that leaving is operationally impossible and economically irrational.
