What Breaks: When Vertical Integration Complexity Overwhelms Execution

Vertical integration that promised margin resilience and cost control proved fragile when integration execution stumbled or commodity cycles squeezed margins simultaneously. Cleveland-Cliffs, DNOW, Cenovus, and Cencora all faced mid-2025 to 2026 pressure points where combining upstream/downstream or manufacturing/distribution proved more operationally complex than the standalone business. Cleveland-Cliffs reported a net loss of $1.4B in 2025 despite $18.6B revenue, partly because fixed-price automotive contracts lagged commodities and integration costs climbed. DNOW's merger with MRC Global closed November 2025 but hit ERP integration issues; Q1 2026 adjusted EBITDA was only $39M despite $1.183B revenue. Cenovus integrated MEG (November 2025) and divested WRB simultaneously, creating a transitional earnings trough. Cencora absorbed OneOncology and PharmaLex goodwill impairment (accounting strain) while managing GLP-1 margin pressure. The pattern showed that vertical integration creates structural cost leverage in upswings but operational fragility and earnings traction-loss in downturns or transition periods.

Vertical integration creates defensible margins and cost control only when execution and cycle timing align. Companies that cannot execute integration cleanly, or that merge/integrate during cyclical downturns, face compressed earnings and balance-sheet stress despite strategic merit of the combination.

The mechanism

Vertical integration (upstream-to-downstream, manufacturing-to-distribution) promises cost control, margin stability, and supply-chain resilience. In practice, the promise depends on: execution (successfully merging systems, reducing redundancy), cycle timing (integrating during upswings when margin recovery is visible), and operational discipline (managing fixed costs when volumes or prices decline).

Cleveland-Cliffs integrated iron ore mining, pellets, DRI, scrap processing, steelmaking, finishing, and automotive sales. In 2025, this system was pinned down by weak automotive production (down 6-8% in North America), fixed-price automotive contracts that lagged commodity spot prices, an expiring slab contract with unfavorable economics, and high fixed costs. The company could not shrink fast enough to offset lower throughput.

DNOW's acquisition of MRC Global (November 2025) added $1.763B of acquisition cost and significant ERP integration complexity. Q1 2026 showed the pain (EBITDA $39M, charges for integration and ERP), but Q2 2026 recovery (EBITDA $60M) suggested the worst of integration trough was passing. The combined entity faced receivables quality, inventory positioning, and system-merge friction simultaneously.

Cenovus completed MEG acquisition (November 2025) and WRB divestiture (September 2025) simultaneously, creating a transitional earnings shape: higher upstream production (from MEG) but lower downstream throughput (from WRB exit). Q1 2026 looked better (record upstream, healthy downstream capture), but the portfolio rebalancing created quarterly volatility.

Cencora absorbed OneOncology and PharmaLex (goodwill impairment) while managing GLP-1 volume growth (which depressed gross margins despite topline support). The company faced mix pressure (high-volume, low-margin GLP-1) and integration/impairment noise simultaneously.

Why now

The inflection was late 2024-early 2025. After commodity price normalization (oil in the $70-85 range, steel prices softer than 2021-2023 peaks), companies that had banked on margin recovery through integration found that costs were sticky and volumes were not. Cleveland-Cliffs' 2025 loss came as a shock to investors who expected 2024-2025 to be recovery years. DNOW's MRC acquisition close in November 2025 overlapped with macroeconomic caution and energy capex uncertainty. Cenovus integrated MEG in November 2025, a period when oil-sands synergies were hoped to offset weaker US refining capture. Cencora faced Q2-Q3 FY2026 pressure from GLP-1 margin compression and integration costs. By Q1-Q2 2026, all four companies were managing earnings traction-loss, guidance pressure, or balance-sheet challenges related to integration timing or mix shift. None reported simple 'integration going perfectly' stories.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Shareholders of companies executing poor integrations face valuation compression and dividend/buyback pressure. Customers of Cleveland-Cliffs face price pressure passed through or service-level deterioration. Employees of acquired companies may face redundancy or reorganization. Creditors of over-leveraged acquirers face refinancing risk.
Who profits
M&A advisors (fees). Operational-restructuring firms hired to optimize post-merger. Companies that acquire and execute cleanly still see value creation, but the window to prove integration viability is narrow (6-18 months).

The tension

The tension is between the strategic promise of integration and the operational reality of combining complex systems. Vertical integration looks better in PowerPoints than in execution. The tension also exists between near-term earnings pain (integration costs, one-time charges, mix shifts) and long-term value creation (promised cost synergies, margin improvement). Investors and boards must tolerate near-term pressure to realize long-term benefit.

The surprising part

Cleveland-Cliffs, despite reporting a $1.4B net loss, maintained $3.3B liquidity and managed to claim 'progress on higher-value products, multi-year automotive contracts, lower unit costs, reduced capex.' This inversion—massive GAAP loss but management tone of execution progress—showed that management was framing the loss as cyclical/transitory rather than structural. Yet the adjusted EBITDA was only $37M on $18.6B revenue, implying a 0.2% margin, which is non-sustaining. This disconnect between GAAP loss (negative equity story) and management's execution narrative (positive momentum story) suggests that vertically integrated steelmakers face perception and execution challenges that pure-commodity miners do not.

Why it matters

Industrial base resilience. Vertically integrated steelmakers, industrial distributors, and healthcare distributors are part of physical supply chains. If integration fails and companies shrink or exit, it can reduce supply redundancy and critical infrastructure.

Vertical integration faltered when execution stumbled, cycle timing misaligned, or cost inflexibility prevented rapid margin recovery, creating earnings troughs and balance-sheet pressure in 2025-2026.
