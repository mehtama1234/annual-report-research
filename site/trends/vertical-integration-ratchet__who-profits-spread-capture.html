Trends · The Vertical Integration Ratchet

Capturing the Spread the Intermediary Used to Take

Vertical integration succeeds by capturing the margin that an arm's-length counterparty would have charged, but only some forms of spread capture are durable across commodity cycles.

The economic logic of vertical integration is that the acquiring company captures the margin that an arm's-length intermediary would have extracted. Suncor's integrated oil-sands-to-refining spread, Freeport-McMoRan's copper-to-byproduct economics, Cencora's specialty-services attachment to drug distribution, and Commercial Metals' scrap-to-steel-product spread all prove that vertical integration can consolidate margin. But not all spread capture is permanent—some depends on temporary dislocations, commodity price disparities, or information advantages that decay over time.

The mechanism

Suncor integrates oil-sands production with downstream refining and retail. Oil-sands crude has a narrower marketing window and lower spreads than light sweet crude—refineries typically discount it. Suncor refines its own oil-sands production internally, capturing the normal refining spread that would otherwise go to an independent refiner. This eliminates a middleman and consolidates margin inside the integrated company. The integrated spread is durable because it is tied to physical co-location and processing efficiency, not market timing. However, if crude prices collapse, the entire value chain compresses, and the integrated spread offers no more protection than an independent refiner would.

Freeport-McMoRan operates mines that produce copper plus molybdenum, gold, and silver as byproducts. A pure copper miner would sell these byproducts at commodity prices or modest premiums. Freeport captures more value by integrating byproduct marketing, negotiating higher prices for byproducts into long-term contracts, and optimizing extraction sequencing to maximize byproduct yields. The spread capture here is more fragile because it depends on Freeport's negotiating power with buyers and the current commodity price environment for gold and molybdenum. If gold prices collapse, the byproduct premium shrinks dramatically.

Cencora's specialty pharmaceutical distribution shows that spread capture can also appear in services. Cencora (formerly AmerisourceBergen) acquired specialty distribution and specialty services businesses, consolidating pharmaceutical distribution, specialty pharmacies, and provider services into one platform. The spread capture occurs through cross-selling (selling multiple services to the same client) and avoiding wholesale margins that independent specialty pharmacies would pay. Commercial Metals operates a similar model: the company collects scrap steel, processes it, and sells finished steel products, capturing the spread between scrap-input costs and finished-product prices. This spread is durable as long as scrap collection and steel fabrication remain efficient, but vulnerable if raw-material costs or finished-product prices diverge sharply.

Why now

Vertical integration is becoming attractive in commodities and distribution because commodity prices have become more volatile and geographically fragmented. Integrated companies can arbitrage price dislocations across their supply chain that independent companies cannot. Additionally, supply-chain pressures post-COVID made companies realize that controlling intermediaries reduced execution risk. Cencora and Commercial Metals both expanded through acquisition during 2023-2025 as these pressures mounted. Simultaneously, energy companies like Suncor are using integrated structures to defend refining margins against industry consolidation and margin compression.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Independent refiners (who compete with integrated producers like Suncor), standalone specialty-pharmaceutical distributors (who compete with Cencora), independent scrap dealers and steel fabricators (who compete with Commercial Metals), and consumers of refined fuels, pharmaceuticals, and steel products (through higher prices if integrated companies can sustain premium pricing)
Who profits
Suncor, Freeport-McMoRan, Cencora, and Commercial Metals through consolidated margin across multiple steps of their value chains. The spread capture shows up as higher gross margins and lower distribution costs compared to pure-play competitors in a single segment.

The tension

Vertical integration creates operational complexity and capital intensity that can offset spread-capture benefits if execution falters. Suncor must manage both mining and refining operations, exposing it to operational risk on both sides. Freeport must optimize ore sequencing across multiple commodities, which is complex. Cencora must integrate incompatible IT systems and sales forces from acquired companies, which creates risk. Additionally, spread capture is vulnerable if the commodity price environment shifts sharply (collapse in gold prices, for example) or if antitrust enforcement constrains integrated companies' ability to reallocate margin internally. Finally, competitors could replicate vertical integration, which would erode the spread-capture advantage and make the complexity cost worse.

The surprising part

The most counterintuitive insight is that vertical integration does NOT always create durable competitive advantage. It only captures spread that a middleman would have extracted. If margins are determined by commodity prices or full competition, vertical integration just compresses the value chain without adding sustainable profit. The companies that win through vertical integration are those that control a scarce input (Freeport's ore body, Suncor's oil-sands reserves, Cencora's scale in specialty distribution) or have information/processing advantages (Commercial Metals' scrap-sourcing network, Suncor's refining efficiency). The spread capture is durable only when the integrated company has a competitive position that an independent intermediary does not.

Why it matters

If vertical integration concentrates industry structure, smaller competitors and independent middlemen are squeezed out, reducing industry dynamism and innovation. Integrated companies may also use their scale to extract uncompetitive pricing from suppliers or customers, embedding cost into supply chains. However, successful vertical integration can also improve efficiency and reduce wasteful middleman mark-ups that get socialized into consumer prices. The distribution effect is ambiguous—concentration can either worsen or improve outcomes depending on whether the integrated company uses its scale to improve efficiency or to extract rents.

Vertical integration captures margin from eliminated intermediaries, but that spread advantage is only durable if the integrating company controls a scarce input or has superior operational efficiencies that independent competitors cannot replicate.
