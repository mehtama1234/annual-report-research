The Workers' Invisible Tax

When companies face labor scarcity, the choice is wages or automation. Across mining, landscaping, construction trades, and packaging, profitable companies are choosing automation — and the gains flow to shareholders while workers and their communities absorb the cost without offset.

Post-pandemic labor shortages in extractive and service industries were real. The market response that textbooks predict — wages rising until labor supply catches up — did not happen at scale. Instead, companies with strong balance sheets accelerated capital deployment to reduce their dependence on labor. Nutrien automated 49% of its potash ore tonnage. Comfort Systems accumulated a $14.06 billion backlog while explicitly naming labor availability as the binding constraint preventing revenue conversion. BrightView replaced domestic seasonal workers with 1,500 H-2B visa holders. Graphic Packaging spent $1.67 billion on the Waco recycled-paperboard project, then cut 2026 capex to $450 million by closing the Lebanon, Tennessee facility and evaluating the Winsford, UK closure. In every case, the automation or consolidation investment captured margin, and those margins flowed to shareholders through buybacks and dividends. ConocoPhillips distributed $9.0 billion to shareholders in 2025. The workers whose jobs were automated away, or whose wage growth was suppressed by visa labor or consolidation, received no equivalent transfer.

The mechanism

The 2020-2022 supply chain disruption created genuine, documented labor shortages in sectors that run on physical presence: construction trades, home health aides, landscaping crews, underground mining, and facilities maintenance. The standard economic response would be wages rising until supply returns. That did not happen at scale in this corpus. Instead, companies with available capital made a different calculation: automation and consolidation preserve margin better than permanent wage increases do. Nutrien invested in automated mine systems across its 6 potash mines. By 2025, 49% of potash ore tonnes were extracted with automation. Comfort Systems kept backlog growing — $5.99 billion at end of 2024 to $11.94 billion at end of 2025 to $14.06 billion by Q2 2026 — while management consistently tied growth constraints to labor availability, not customer demand. ABM Industries used the WGNSTAR acquisition to shift its service mix from low-margin routine janitorial into semiconductor fabs and datacenter-facility operations, reducing its exposure to the same labor market dynamics while improving margin profile.

The H-2B visa program is a cleaner version of the same mechanism in service industries. BrightView deployed approximately 1,500 seasonal H-2B workers in fiscal 2025 to maintain its commercial landscaping operations across airports, hospitals, campuses, hotels, and municipalities. This is not growth labor — it is maintenance-of-service labor that the domestic market would provide at higher wages. The company reduced its H-2B count from 2,000 in 2024 to 1,500 in 2025, but the filing explicitly discloses the expectation to continue using the program. That expectation is itself the signal: domestic wage rates have not risen to the market-clearing level because companies can access cheaper temporary labor through the visa channel instead. The labor market never clears; the scarcity signal never produces the wage response; workers in the domestic pool never realize the scarcity premium.

Automation gains flow upward through two confirmed channels in this filing set. First, capital expenditure on automation increases operating leverage: fewer labor-hours per unit of output means higher operating margins on the same revenue. Nutrien's $2.30 billion in 2025 net earnings came partly from 49% automated potash extraction. Graphic Packaging's $1.67 billion Waco investment drove efficiency gains, then the company shifted to harvesting mode — cutting 2026 capex to $450 million, closing the Lebanon, Tennessee facility, and evaluating the Winsford, UK closure — realizing the automation return through workforce reduction and capital reallocation. Second, the freed cash flow is directed to shareholders rather than workers. ConocoPhillips returned $9.0 billion to shareholders in 2025 while workers at its extraction sites absorbed both the physical risk of oil and gas production and the ongoing job-elimination risk of continued automation investment.

The framing in corporate filings masks the underlying transfer. Nutrien calls 49% automated potash mining a 'competitive advantage.' ABM describes WGNSTAR as a 'tech-forward acquisition' expanding its mission-critical capabilities. Graphic Packaging frames the Waco investment as 'sustainable innovation' and the Lebanon closure as part of 'footprint optimization.' Comfort Systems describes record backlog as proof of 'unmatched execution.' None of these framings is false, but none of them names the mechanism clearly: labor became expensive or unreliable, automation capital was deployed, margins improved, capital was returned to shareholders, and workers were left with fewer jobs and no transition infrastructure. The speed of deployment — the Waco project at $1.67 billion, the Nutrien automation ramp to 49%, the ABM shift into technical facilities, all visible in 2023-2026 filings — outpaces any retraining or community-investment response by a decade.

Why now

Three overlapping conditions made 2023-2025 the inflection window, and all three are traceable to specific data points in this corpus. First, the 2021-2022 labor shock established new baselines: companies that had always described labor as manageable began disclosing it as a primary operating constraint. Comfort Systems' year-end backlog grew from $5.99 billion in 2024 to $11.94 billion at end of 2025, a trajectory that would not exist if demand were the bottleneck — demand was not the constraint; skilled-trades availability was. Second, capital cost was low enough through 2023-2024 that large automation investments cleared their hurdle rates. Graphic Packaging committed $1.67 billion to the Waco project in a rate environment that made a $5.6 billion debt load serviceable. Nutrien funded mine automation while returning cash to shareholders and executing divestitures. Third, automation technology in physical industries — remote mine operations, high-efficiency recycled-paperboard mills, robotics-adjacent facilities management — matured to the point where the capital-versus-labor tradeoff closed in automation's favor even at service wages below $25/hour. The competitive dynamic now self-reinforces: Nutrien's 49% automated potash mining is cited as a competitive advantage in its 2025 annual report, which means any potash producer without equivalent automation investment is, by definition, describing its lower-automation exposure as a risk. That creates a race where laggards must follow the leaders, and every round of following displaces more labor.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Workers in potash mining communities in remote Canada, where Nutrien's 6 mines operate and automation has no local replacement employment. Home health aides at Aveanna Healthcare and similar operators — workers providing pediatric and elder private duty care at home, absorbing physical and emotional risk, while wage pressure from labor shortage is cited as a cost risk to manage rather than a signal to raise pay. Domestic landscaping workers competing with 1,500 H-2B visa workers at BrightView, where the program's existence suppresses the wage that market scarcity would otherwise produce. Skilled trades workers at construction and HVAC contractors who see Comfort Systems' $14.06 billion backlog as proof that demand for their work is at record levels, but who cannot capture the implied scarcity premium because the backlog is a measure of unconverted work, not realized wages. Packaging and manufacturing workers in Lebanon, Tennessee, whose facility is being closed as Graphic Packaging shifts to the Waco model, with no local replacement employer of comparable scale. Routine facilities and janitorial workers at ABM as the company deliberately upgrades its service mix toward semiconductor fabs and technical environments, reducing its growth investment in the low-margin work those workers perform. Communities in all of these geographies lose tax base and institutional capacity when the primary employer automates or consolidates without any requirement to invest in transition.
Who profits
Shareholders through buybacks and dividends funded by the operating leverage gained when labor costs are controlled or reduced. ConocoPhillips returned $9.0 billion in 2025 on $19.8 billion of operating cash flow. Nutrien increased total cash returns to shareholders 30% in 2025 while delivering $2.30 billion in net earnings partly on the back of 49% automated potash extraction. Graphic Packaging distributed capital after the Waco buildout by shifting to lower capex, closures, and debt paydown. Specialized technical workers — automation engineers, mine systems operators, semiconductor-facility technicians, WGNSTAR integration specialists — who maintain, program, and expand the automation systems earn premium wages and face genuine competitive scarcity for their skills. Capital equipment vendors and system integrators who sell automation into mining, packaging, and facilities management. Large incumbents with the balance sheets to fund the automation capital cycle — Nutrien, Graphic Packaging, ConocoPhillips, ABM — versus smaller competitors that cannot afford equivalent investment and face widening productivity gaps that put cost pressure on their workers instead. H-2B visa intermediaries and seasonal labor brokers who monetize the gap between domestic wage expectations and visa program costs.

The tension

The labor shortage that multiple sectors describe as a constraint on growth is a market signal that wages are below the level needed to attract sufficient workers. If BrightView raised domestic landscaping wages from current levels to $25/hour, domestic labor supply would likely increase and H-2B dependency would decline — but that wage increase compresses the margin that makes BrightView's $56 million net income on $2.67 billion in revenue viable. If home health aides at Aveanna's care network earned $22/hour instead of current rates, staffing would improve, but adjusted EBITDA of $84.4 million on $647.9 million of revenue would shrink. The wage adjustment that would clear the market is precisely the adjustment that compresses returns to shareholders. Companies are not choosing automation over wages out of ignorance of the wage signal; they are choosing automation because they have the capital to do so and because automation is cheaper in the long run than permanently higher wages. The regulatory and collective-bargaining responses that would force alignment between who benefits and who pays have not materialized. There is no sectoral retraining fund for potash mining communities. There is no binding requirement for Graphic Packaging to invest in Lebanon, Tennessee before closing that facility. There is no automation tax that funds transition for displaced workers. The economic gains from the 2023-2025 automation acceleration are already distributed: they are in ConocoPhillips' $9.0 billion shareholder return, in Nutrien's $2.30 billion net earnings, in Comfort Systems' $1.02 billion net income on record backlog. The worker-transition cost is deferred, diffuse, and absorbed by people without the capital or political leverage to demand offset.

The surprising part

The most non-obvious element is that labor shortage and high profitability are not in tension in these filings — they are the same signal read from two different positions. Comfort Systems has a $14.06 billion backlog, which is unambiguous proof that customer demand at current prices far exceeds the company's ability to deliver. In a normal market, that gap would produce wage increases until enough skilled tradespeople appeared to close it. Instead, Comfort Systems grew backlog while growing net income to $1.02 billion on $9.10 billion in 2025 revenue, and management described the backlog as 'unmatched execution' rather than as a wage-market failure. The scarcity that should have driven wage growth instead drove pricing discipline — customers paid more, but workers did not capture that premium in wages because the company was not compelled to bid up labor. The second surprise is the scale of capital being returned to shareholders in the same industries citing labor scarcity as their primary operating risk. ConocoPhillips returned $9.0 billion to shareholders in 2025, equivalent to 45% of its $19.8 billion operating cash flow, while workers at its extraction sites were absorbing physical risk the company explicitly discloses. Nutrien increased shareholder cash returns 30% in 2025 while deploying automation at 49% of ore tonnage. These are not struggling industries paying workers poorly because they are desperate. They are highly profitable industries choosing automation because they have the capital to do so and because automation costs less over a ten-year horizon than the wage premium needed to attract and retain sufficient labor.

Why it matters

Industrial automation is proceeding faster than the social infrastructure — retraining programs, community investment, income support, sectoral wage agreements — that could buffer displaced workers. The filings in this corpus show every labor-intensive sector citing workforce availability as a primary operating constraint while simultaneously reporting strong profits and returning capital to shareholders. The gap between those two facts is where the social cost accumulates. Workers displaced by automation in Nutrien's potash mining communities, by facility closure in Graphic Packaging's Lebanon, Tennessee operation, or by the shift from domestic to H-2B landscaping labor in BrightView's national network, are not absorbed by any existing institutional mechanism. Their communities lose tax base and employment without advance notice or mandatory transition investment from the companies that captured the automation gains. The political risk of uncompensated transfer at this scale is visible in the same industries: when manufacturing workers see record profits and record backlog in their sector while their wages stagnate or their jobs are automated away, the political response is not calibrated — it targets immigration, capital, automation policy, and specific companies without precision. The economic case for intervention — wage insurance, retraining funds tied to automation investment, community-transition requirements on facility closures, taxes on automation gains that fund worker support — is that the cost of not intervening accumulates as political instability, geographic inequality, and social fragmentation at a pace that eventually costs more than redistribution would have.

Every labor shortage described in these annual reports is a choice by a profitable company to automate away workers rather than pay market-clearing wages — and the proof is that automation gains are showing up in $9.0 billion shareholder returns at ConocoPhillips and 49% automated ore extraction at Nutrien while no comparable infrastructure exists to absorb the workers displaced in mining towns, landscaping markets, and packaging facilities.

The case against this trend

What would falsify it: Evidence that profitable companies facing labor scarcity are choosing permanent wage increases over automation/consolidation, such as companies raising skilled-trades or home health wages substantially and successfully recruiting without visa programs or facility consolidation, or that labor supply actually increased to meet demand without wage premium.

Companies that don't fit:

  • Comfort Systems USA — Despite $14.06B record backlog (Q2 2026) cited as proof of labor constraint, management explicitly emphasizes 'growing teams' and 'organic revenue growth of 51%' with 'robust ongoing demand' rather than automation. Backlog growth itself proves the company is NOT automating to solve the constraint — it is hiring to convert demand into revenue. If the thesis were fully true, management would have deployed significant automation to eliminate the labor bottleneck; instead, they are scaling labor and maintaining pricing power.
  • Aveanna Healthcare Holdings — Explicitly cited as having 'wage pressure from labor shortage' as operating risk, yet the response is NOT automation reduction. Instead, Aveanna is growing aggressively through acquisition (Family First Homecare), raising guidance, and maintaining high margins while absorbing wage pressure. The company is choosing to compete for labor through scale and continuity rather than through labor reduction.
  • Applied Industrial Technologies — Entire business model inverts the thesis. Management explicitly states 'customer labor constraints matter' as VALUE DRIVER, not as spur to automation. The company is GROWING demand for technical labor and expertise precisely because customers cannot staff internal capabilities. Engineered Solutions is approaching 40% of sales (up from 18% ten years ago) by making their labor deeper, not shallower.
  • ABM Industries — Expanding into WGNSTAR and technical facilities precisely BECAUSE those markets need MORE labor, not less. While the thesis predicts automation displacement, ABM's growth strategy is to deepen technical-services labor and shift toward higher-skill, higher-margin work. The company is not consolidating labor out; it is consolidating labor IN.

Honest caveat: The trend captures a real and important pattern in extraction (Nutrien), packaging (Graphic Packaging), and certain consolidation plays (ConocoPhillips capital returns), but it does NOT capture the full labor-scarcity response across the corpus. In construction (Comfort Systems), home healthcare (Aveanna), industrial distribution (Applied Industrial), and facilities operations (ABM), the primary response to labor scarcity has been to compete for labor, raise technical service premiums, or acquire capacity — not to automate labor away. The trend's error is treating automation and wage response as the only two options when the data shows a third: market segmentation where high-wage, high-skill players grow by deepening expertise while low-cost, low-skill players either consolidate or face margin pressure. Nutrien's 49% automation works precisely because mining is geographically isolated and low-skill-variance; Comfort Systems' backlog grows because construction labor is geographically distributed and skill-specific, making automation harder. The thesis works better for commoditized labor in capital-intensive extractive industries and worse for distributed, skill-specific, or relationship-based labor. The political danger is not overstated, but the mechanism is more segmented than the trend suggests.

The Automation Inflection: Compressed Into Three Years

Nutrien disclosed 49% of potash ore tonnes mined using automation in 2025, up from lower percentages in prior years. MSC Industrial cut field sales associates from 2,723 to 2,496 while adding 3,000+ vending machines. PepsiCo emphasized 'productivity savings' in every quarterly earnings release to fund growth and affordability moves. These are not cyclical headcount reductions—they are permanent deletions of job categories triggered when post-pandemic labor scarcity made automation capital cost-justified.

Sub-pattern

Mines Without Miners

Nutrien automated 49% of potash production by 2025. Freeport runs autonomous haul systems 24/7. Mosaic idles phosphate facilities rather than pay human labor rates. Mining companies are not planning to automate—they have already done it. The towns built around these mines have zero transition infrastructure.

Sub-pattern

The Service Labor Squeeze

Rather than let pay rise until enough workers show up, service companies lean on temporary-visa labor, hold wages near the floor, or simply cap growth at the size of the crew they already have. The unpaid difference is the workers' invisible tax.

Sub-pattern

Machines Replacing Sales Reps

Labor scarcity is pushing companies to automate faster than any retraining infrastructure can absorb, shifting risk to workers while capital captures the returns. Industrial and service companies facing labor shortages are choosing capital expenditure on automation over wage increases. Automation gains are concentrated in shareholder returns while displaced workers and their communities absorb the cost without policy or investment to help them transition.
