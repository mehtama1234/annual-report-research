The Workers' Invisible Tax

Labor scarcity is pushing companies to automate faster than any retraining infrastructure can absorb, shifting risk to workers while capital captures the returns.

societaleconomiclaborindustrial
Industrial and service companies facing post-pandemic labor shortages are choosing capital expenditure on automation over wage increases. When workers leave industries due to low pay or poor conditions, companies treat the scarcity as a problem to engineer away rather than a signal to improve the work. The result: automation gains are concentrated in shareholder returns while displaced workers and their communities absorb the cost without policy or investment to help them transition.

The mechanism

The supply chain shock of 2020-2021 created genuine labor shortages in labor-intensive sectors: construction trades, home health aides, landscaping, mining, and facilities management. Post-pandemic, instead of wages adjusting upward permanently to reflect scarcity, companies accelerated capital deployment to reduce labor dependence. Nutrien invested in automated mine systems (49% of potash ore now extracted with automation in 2025). Comfort Systems faced a $14.06 billion backlog but explicitly named labor availability as the bottleneck preventing conversion to revenue. ABM Industries used acquisitions like WGNSTAR to shift from low-margin janitorial work into higher-value semiconductor and datacenter-facility automation services, leaving traditional cleaning roles exposed.

The automation wave has accelerated beyond traditional productivity measures. Companies no longer frame automation as incremental efficiency; they frame it as competitive necessity. When BrightView cannot hire enough domestic landscapers at existing wages, it applies for H-2B temporary visa workers—not to grow, but to maintain current service levels. When Comfort Systems has demand it cannot fulfill with skilled trades, it raises backlog (signaling to investors that growth is constrained, not by customer demand but by worker availability) while maintaining pricing discipline rather than raising wages aggressively. Automation becomes the 'solution' because it removes the human variable from the equation entirely.

The gains flow upward through two channels. Capital expenditure in automation increases asset bases and drives efficiency gains that expand operating margins. ConocoPhillips returned $9.0 billion to shareholders in 2025 while workers in potash mines and oil fields absorbed both the physical risk of extraction and the job-elimination risk of accelerated automation. Graphic Packaging's $1.67 billion Waco investment drove profitability, then the company shifted strategy to lower capex ($450M from $935M in 2025) by closing facilities and reducing the workforce, realizing those returns through shareholder distributions and debt paydown. The workers displaced by those closures and automation investments have no equivalent infrastructure to absorb the shift.

The mechanism is masked by corporate framing. Companies describe automation as 'competitive advantage' or 'operational excellence.' Nutrien highlights 49% automated potash mining as a strength. ABM describes WGNSTAR as a tech-forward acquisition. Graphic Packaging describes the Waco project as 'sustainable innovation.' But the underlying dynamic is consistent: labor becomes expensive or unreliable, automation capital is deployed, margins improve, capital is returned to shareholders, and workers are left with fewer jobs and no transition support. The speed of deployment (2023-2026 in this corpus) outpaces any retraining or community-investment response.

Why now

Post-pandemic labor scarcity was real: Aveanna Healthcare explicitly cites wage pressure from labor shortage in in-home services; Comfort Systems has demand it cannot fulfill; BrightView reduced H-2B usage in 2024 but maintains expectations to increase it again as domestic labor becomes scarcer and more expensive. At the same time, capital became available (strong cash flows, low cost of capital through much of 2023-2025) and automation technology matured. The corpus captures a moment when companies are making the simultaneous decision to automate because they believe wages are a cost to engineer away, not a signal to improve. The speed is accelerating because the competitive pressure on firms that do not match automation investment is visible in margin comparisons. Nutrien's 49% automated potash mining is now a stated competitive advantage; companies that have not invested at that pace are describing labor constraints as risks to growth. That creates a race-to-the-bottom dynamic where automation becomes not optional but mandatory to remain competitive.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Workers in mining towns and potash communities (Nutrien automation reduces demand for underground labor in remote Canadian operations with few alternative employment paths). Home health aides earning near-minimum wage while absorbing physical risk and emotional labor of elder and pediatric care (Aveanna and other home-health operators cite labor shortage but pass wage pressure to staffing rather than to worker incomes). Landscaping workers competing with H-2B visa labor for low-wage seasonal work (BrightView's 1,500 H-2B workers in 2025 constrain domestic wage growth in landscaping). Skilled trades workers at companies like Comfort Systems who see demand spike but labor constraints prevent them from capturing wage growth that demand signal would normally create (backlog is proof of demand, but if labor is binding, workers do not realize the scarcity value). Packaging and manufacturing workers in regions dependent on single facilities (Graphic Packaging's Lebanon, Tennessee closure eliminates jobs with no local replacement). Office and back-office workers at staffing firms as automation replaces intermediation. Communities whose primary employer automates or consolidates operations lose tax base and employment without advance notice or community investment. Finally, all low-wage workers absorb the risk that their industry will be automated away because they lack the capital to invest in retraining or geographic mobility.
Who profits
Shareholders through buybacks and dividends funded by operating leverage gained when labor costs are controlled. ConocoPhillips returned $9.0 billion in 2025 while simultaneously automating extraction. Nutrien's 49% automated mining reduces long-term labor costs and supports dividend sustainability. Graphic Packaging distributed capital after the Waco buildout by shifting to closure and consolidation. Specialized technical workers (engineers, systems integrators, automation specialists) who maintain, program, and integrate automation systems earn premium wages and benefit from competitive scarcity. Capital equipment vendors who sell automation systems into these industries. Companies with scale and balance sheets deep enough to fund automation capex (large incumbents) vs. smaller competitors that cannot afford equivalent automation and fall behind on margins. Staffing firms and visa-program intermediaries that monetize temporary labor scarcity (BrightView's continued H-2B reliance creates demand for visa services and recruitment). Private equity and acquirers who can buy automation-lagging incumbents, automate them, and extract margin improvement.

The tension

The labor shortage that multiple sectors describe as a constraint on growth is partially a market signal—that wages are below the level needed to attract sufficient workers. The policy response chosen by companies is based on what preserves margin, not what serves workers or communities. If wages in landscaping rose to $25/hour from $15/hour, domestic labor supply would likely increase and H-2B dependency would decline. If home health aides earned $22/hour instead of $16/hour, staffing would improve. But those wage adjustments compress margins, so companies instead automate, deploy visa programs, or consolidate operations. The regulatory and union responses to automation-driven displacement have not materialized at scale. There are no sectoral retraining funds comparable to Trade Adjustment Assistance. There is no binding requirement for companies to invest in community transition when they close facilities or automate labor away. Automation benefits are concentrated in capital-intensive sectors with scale (mining, datacenter services, large construction); small and mid-size manufacturers cannot afford equivalent automation capex and face a widening productivity gap, which puts wage pressure on their workers instead. The real tension is that automation is profitable for capital, retraining is expensive for workers and governments, and there is no mechanism forcing alignment between who benefits and who pays.

The surprising part

The most non-obvious element is that labor shortage and automation acceleration are not separate phenomena; they are two sides of the same choice. Comfort Systems has a $14.06 billion backlog (proof that demand exists and exceeds supply) but cannot convert it to revenue because labor is not available. The 'rational' capitalist response would be to raise wages until workers appear. Instead, Comfort Systems and other firms are automating and consolidating, treating labor scarcity as a technical problem to engineer away. That choice is accelerating because companies are competing on margin, and automating away labor-scarcity premiums is cheaper in the long run than matching market-clearing wages. The second surprise is that these dynamics are visible in premium-positioned companies with strong earnings (Nutrien's $2.30 billion net earnings, ConocoPhillips's $9.0 billion shareholder returns, Comfort Systems's record backlog). This is not a story about struggling industries paying workers poorly because they are desperate. It is a story about highly profitable industries choosing to automate because automation is cheaper than market-clearing wages, and capital has the power to make that choice.

Why it matters

The gap between who produces value and who captures it is one of the defining questions of the current economy. Industrial automation is proceeding faster than the social infrastructure—retraining programs, community investment, income support, sectoral agreements—that could buffer displaced workers. These filings, read together, show every sector citing labor as their key constraint and simultaneously deploying capital to reduce dependence on labor. If this pattern continues without policy response, the effect is a systematic transfer of wealth from workers to capital, concentrated in specific geographies (mining regions, manufacturing towns, landscaping markets), without offset. Workers who are displaced by automation in towns without alternative employment will absorb generational income loss. Their children will have fewer paths to stable middle-class work. Communities will lose tax base and institutional capacity. And the political risk of that uncompensated transfer is that it generates backlash against capital, immigration, automation, or targeted companies—none of which is inevitable but all of which becomes more likely if the transfer is perceived as unfair. The economic and social case for responding—whether through wage insurance, retraining funds, community investment requirements, or automation taxes that fund transition—is that the cost of not responding (political instability, social fragmentation, geographic inequality) is higher than the cost of redistribution.

Every labor shortage described in these annual reports is a deliberate choice by profitable companies to automate away workers rather than to pay market-clearing wages, while automation gains flow to shareholders and losses flow to workers and communities with no transition support.
