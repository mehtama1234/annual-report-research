Machines Replacing Sales Reps

Labor scarcity is pushing companies to automate faster than any retraining infrastructure can absorb, shifting risk to workers while capital captures the returns. Industrial and service companies facing labor shortages are choosing capital expenditure on automation over wage increases. Automation gains are concentrated in shareholder returns while displaced workers and their communities absorb the cost without policy or investment to help them transition.

The mechanism

The supply chain shock of 2020-2021 created genuine labor shortages in labor-intensive sectors: construction trades, home health aides, landscaping, mining, and facilities management. Post-pandemic, instead of wages adjusting upward permanently to reflect scarcity, companies accelerated capital deployment to reduce labor dependence. Nutrien invested in automated mine systems — 49% of potash ore is now extracted with automation in 2025. Comfort Systems faced a $14.06B backlog but explicitly named labor availability as the bottleneck preventing conversion to revenue. ABM Industries used acquisitions like WGNSTAR to shift from low-margin janitorial work into higher-value semiconductor and datacenter-facility automation services. The automation wave has accelerated beyond traditional productivity measures. Companies no longer frame automation as incremental efficiency; they frame it as competitive necessity.

When BrightView cannot hire enough domestic landscapers at existing wages, it applies for H-2B temporary visa workers — not to grow, but to maintain current service levels. When Comfort Systems has demand it cannot fulfill with skilled trades, it raises backlog while maintaining pricing discipline rather than raising wages aggressively. Automation becomes the 'solution' because it removes the human variable from the equation entirely. The gains flow upward through two channels. Capital expenditure in automation increases asset bases and drives efficiency gains that expand operating margins. ConocoPhillips returned $9.0B to shareholders in 2025 while workers in potash mines and oil fields absorbed both the physical risk of extraction and the job-elimination risk of accelerated automation. Graphic Packaging's $1.67B Waco investment drove profitability, then the company shifted strategy to lower capex ($450M from $935M in 2025) by closing facilities and reducing the workforce, realizing those returns through shareholder distributions.

This creates a race-to-the-bottom dynamic. Companies that invest in automation first gain a margin advantage over those that don't. That competitive pressure makes automation not optional but mandatory to remain competitive. Nutrien's 49% automated potash mining is now a stated competitive advantage. Companies that have not invested at that pace are describing labor constraints as risks to growth. The pace of automation is accelerating because companies believe they can permanently escape the labor-supply problem through capital substitution, and the first movers to achieve that substitution capture the margin gains that the broader sector eventually shares.

Why now

Post-pandemic labor scarcity was real and measurable. Aveanna Healthcare explicitly cites wage pressure from labor shortage in in-home services; Comfort Systems has demand it cannot fulfill; BrightView's H-2B dependence persists even as the company reduced H-2B usage in 2024 (from 2,000 to 1,500) but explicitly stated expectations to increase it again as domestic labor becomes scarcer and more expensive. At the same time, capital became available (strong cash flows, low cost of capital through much of 2023-2025) and automation technology matured. The inflection point was 2023-2024, when companies made the simultaneous decision to automate because they believed wages were a cost to engineer away, not a signal to improve. The speed is accelerating because competitive pressure on firms that do not match automation investment is visible in margin comparisons. Nutrien's 49% automated potash mining is now a competitive advantage; companies that have not invested at that pace are describing labor constraints as risks. That creates a race-to-the-bottom dynamic where automation becomes mandatory to remain competitive.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Workers in mining towns and potash communities (Nutrien automation reduces demand for underground labor in remote Canadian operations with few alternative employment paths). Home health aides earning near-minimum wage while absorbing physical risk and emotional labor of elder and pediatric care (Aveanna and other home-health operators cite labor shortage but pass wage pressure to staffing rather than to worker incomes). Landscaping workers competing with H-2B visa labor for low-wage seasonal work (BrightView's 1,500 H-2B workers in 2025 constrain domestic wage growth in landscaping). Skilled trades workers at companies like Comfort Systems who see demand spike but labor constraints prevent them from capturing wage growth that demand signal would normally create (backlog is proof of demand, but if labor is binding, workers do not realize the scarcity value). Packaging and manufacturing workers in regions dependent on single facilities (Graphic Packaging's facility closures eliminate jobs with no local replacement). Communities whose primary employer automates or consolidates operations lose tax base and employment without advance notice or community investment.
Who profits
Shareholders through buybacks and dividends funded by operating leverage gained when labor costs are controlled. ConocoPhillips returned $9.0B in 2025 while simultaneously automating extraction. Nutrien's 49% automated mining reduces long-term labor costs and supports dividend sustainability. Graphic Packaging distributed capital after the Waco buildout by shifting to closure and consolidation. Specialized technical workers (engineers, systems integrators, automation specialists) who maintain, program, and integrate automation systems earn premium wages and benefit from competitive scarcity. Capital equipment vendors who sell automation systems into these industries. Companies with scale and balance sheets deep enough to fund automation capex (large incumbents) versus smaller competitors that cannot afford equivalent automation.

The tension

The labor shortage that multiple sectors describe as a constraint on growth is partially a market signal — that wages are below the level needed to attract sufficient workers. The policy response chosen by companies is based on what preserves margin, not what serves workers or communities. If wages in landscaping rose to $25/hour from $15/hour, domestic labor supply would likely increase and H-2B dependency would decline. If home health aides earned $22/hour instead of $16/hour, staffing would improve. But those wage adjustments compress margins, so companies instead automate, deploy visa programs, or consolidate operations. There are no sectoral retraining funds comparable to Trade Adjustment Assistance. There is no binding requirement for companies to invest in community transition when they close facilities or automate labor away. Automation benefits are concentrated in capital-intensive sectors with scale (mining, datacenter services, large construction); small and mid-size manufacturers cannot afford equivalent automation capex and face a widening productivity gap, which puts wage pressure on their workers instead.

The surprising part

The most non-obvious element is that labor shortage and automation acceleration are not separate phenomena; they are two sides of the same choice. Comfort Systems has a $14.06B backlog (proof that demand exists and exceeds supply) but cannot convert it to revenue because labor is not available. The 'rational' capitalist response would be to raise wages until workers appear. Instead, Comfort Systems and other firms are automating and consolidating, treating labor scarcity as a technical problem to engineer away. That choice is accelerating because companies are competing on margin, and automating away labor-scarcity premiums is cheaper in the long run than matching market-clearing wages. The second surprise is that these dynamics are visible in profitable companies with strong earnings (Nutrien's $2.30B net earnings, ConocoPhillips' $9.0B shareholder returns, Comfort Systems' record backlog). This is not a story about struggling industries paying workers poorly because they are desperate. It is a story about highly profitable industries choosing to automate because automation is cheaper than market-clearing wages, and capital has the power to make that choice.

Why it matters

The gap between who produces value and who captures it is one of the defining questions of the current economy. Industrial automation is proceeding faster than the social infrastructure — retraining programs, community investment, income support, sectoral agreements — that could buffer displaced workers. These filings, read together, show every sector citing labor as their key constraint and simultaneously deploying capital to reduce dependence on it. If this pattern continues without policy response, the effect is a systematic transfer of wealth from workers to capital, concentrated in specific geographies (mining regions, manufacturing towns, landscaping markets), without offset. Workers who are displaced by automation in towns without alternative employment will absorb generational income loss. Communities will lose tax base and institutional capacity. The economic and social case for responding — whether through wage insurance, retraining funds, community investment requirements, or automation taxes that fund transition — is that the cost of not responding (political instability, social fragmentation, geographic inequality) is higher than the cost of redistribution.

Every labor shortage described in these annual reports is a deliberate choice by profitable companies to automate away workers rather than to pay market-clearing wages, while automation gains flow to shareholders and losses flow to workers and communities with no transition support.
