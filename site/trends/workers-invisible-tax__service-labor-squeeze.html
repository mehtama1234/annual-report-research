The Service Sector's Quiet Squeeze

In services, companies do not automate work away — they suppress wages, rely on visa labor pools, or cap growth at what existing workforce can absorb. BrightView's H-2B dependency, Aveanna's home-care aide wages near minimum, Comfort Systems' $14B backlog constrained by technician availability — each is a structural refusal to let wages clear the market.

The service sector is choosing labor suppression over wage increases or automation. BrightView explicitly depends on H-2B visa workers (1,500 in fiscal 2025); Aveanna is scaling home-care delivery at wage points that would be uneconomical at market-clearing rates; Comfort Systems has a $14B backlog but cannot grow faster because skilled HVAC technician labor is not available at current wages. The pattern is consistent: demand exceeds available labor at offered wages, but companies would rather constrain growth, suppress wages, or import workers than raise compensation to market-clearing levels.

The mechanism

BrightView shows the visa-labor strategy explicitly. The company employed 1,500 seasonal workers through the H-2B visa program in fiscal 2025 (down from 2,000 in 2024). That decline is not due to reduced labor demand — BrightView's 2026 guidance has been constructive, and landscaping remains a strong recurring service. The decline is likely due to tighter visa availability and the company potentially shifting some work to lower-wage direct hire. Either way, the company's 10-K makes clear it expects to continue relying on the H-2B program. That dependence reveals the economic logic: if BrightView had to hire seasonal landscaping workers at market-clearing wages without visa access, either the business model would be uneconomical or prices would need to rise substantially. Instead, the company is structured to depend on a wage-suppressed visa labor pool.

Aveanna shows the wage-suppression path within home care. The company scaled adjusted EBITDA 25.2% in Q1 2026 while revenue grew 15.9%, suggesting margin improvement through operational leverage. But home-care aide wages are a known constraint in the home-care industry — many operators explicitly state they pay near minimum wage because the reimbursement rates don't support higher wages. Aveanna is growing by acquiring other home-care operators and consolidating them under lower cost structures. Scaling depends on the ability to employ caregivers at current wage levels; if those wages had to rise to market-clearing rates, the business model would compress significantly.

Comfort Systems shows the growth-constraint strategy. The company has a $14B backlog at Q2 2026, up from $8.12B a year earlier. Quarterly revenue was $3.27B with 51% organic growth in Q1 2026. Yet management keeps noting that execution depends on 'growing teams' and that the company can only grow as fast as it can source and retain skilled HVAC technicians. The backlog would be larger if the company could hire more technicians; the growth constraint is pure labor availability. Rather than raise wages aggressively to pull technicians into the market, Comfort Systems is accepting lower growth, accepting that some backlog will never convert to revenue, and moving at whatever pace the existing technician pool allows.

Why now

The service squeeze is acute now because demand growth from AI infrastructure, building automation, healthcare scaling, and commercial property maintenance is outpacing labor-supply growth. Comfort Systems' massive backlog did not emerge overnight; it resulted from sustained demand growth hitting a fixed labor pool. Immigration policy tightness makes visa-dependent models (BrightView) more precarious. Healthcare reimbursement pressures keep Aveanna from raising wages even as demand for home care escalates. Companies across all three models are simultaneously facing higher demand and lower labor supply, and they are all choosing to suppress wages, import workers, or cap growth rather than let compensation rise to market-clearing levels.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
Home-care aides earning Aveanna wages. Landscaping workers on H-2B visas (who are cheaper to recruit internationally than paying domestic market rates). HVAC technicians and skilled trades who see wage growth lagging demand growth because companies prefer to constrain volume rather than raise wages. The final payment comes from consumers: nursing-home families paying Aveanna for home care, commercial property owners paying BrightView for landscaping, businesses paying Comfort Systems for HVAC at inflated prices due to execution scarcity. The worker pays through suppressed wages; the customer pays through higher prices; the company keeps the margin.
Who profits
Service operators who can maintain pricing power while suppressing wages or using cheaper labor sources. Comfort Systems can raise prices on HVAC work because capacity is scarce; the company pockets the margin differential between what it charges customers and what it pays technicians. Aveanna can scale home-care delivery at reimbursement rates that don't support market-clearing wages; growth and EBITDA expansion are profitable because labor is an input with suppressed pricing. BrightView has margin because visa workers earn less than domestic labor would demand. These are not high-margin businesses by manufacturing standards, but they are capturing spread by not allowing wages to clear the market.

The tension

The fragility sits in labor availability. H-2B visa programs can be restricted or eliminated by policy. Immigration barriers can tighten. If domestic workers have alternative opportunities (if other sectors start raising wages or if automation reduces pressure on non-service employment), visa-dependent models like BrightView become unsustainable. Home-care operators like Aveanna face continuous pressure: if healthcare reimbursement doesn't rise, wages stay suppressed, and caregiver burnout accelerates. Comfort Systems could face a shock if skilled trades simply become unavailable (though that seems unlikely given aging workforce demographics). The deepest fragility is political: if society decides that service workers deserve higher wages and enforces that through policy, the cost structure of these businesses breaks.

The surprising part

The non-obvious pattern is that these companies have rationally chosen constraint over wage competition. From a capital-allocation perspective, Comfort Systems with a $14B backlog could theoretically hire more technicians by offering higher wages — but that would compress margins more than accepting slower growth. Aveanna could expand faster if it paid home-care aides more, but reimbursement rates don't support it, so the company scales consolidation instead. BrightView relies on visa labor because paying domestic market wages would make the business uncompetitive. All three are making rational decisions within their constraints, but those decisions collectively suppress wages and limit the access to service-sector jobs at market-clearing rates. The system-level consequence is that service-sector wage growth lags broader employment growth, creating a structural tax on workers in those industries.

Why it matters

This pattern cascades into inequality and household economics. Workers in service sectors (landscaping, home care, technical services) are facing suppressed wage growth while demand for those services is rising. Households relying on those services see prices rising (because operators are raising customer prices while suppressing worker wages) while the workers providing the services see stagnant compensation. Immigration policy designed to restrict labor supply benefits operators like BrightView more than it protects domestic workers — instead of forcing wage increases, it just constrains growth and enables visa programs. The reimbursement crisis in home care means Aveanna and other operators can scale only by finding lower-wage workers or consolidating at lower cost, not by paying existing workers more. The aggregate effect is that service-sector employment is becoming increasingly unequal: capital owners and operators capturing spread while workers absorb wage suppression.

Service companies suppress wages, import visa labor, and constrain growth rather than allowing compensation to clear labor markets, creating a hidden tax on service workers and extracting spread for operators.
