The Service Labor Squeeze

Rather than let pay rise until enough workers show up, service companies lean on temporary-visa labor, hold wages near the floor, or simply cap growth at the size of the crew they already have. The unpaid difference is the workers' invisible tax.

Labor-heavy service firms are betting they can keep margins by managing around wage pressure instead of clearing it: BrightView rations seasonal work through H-2B visas, Aveanna scales home care staffed near the wage floor, and Comfort Systems lets a record backlog pile up rather than bid up scarce technicians. The market signal that pay should rise gets absorbed by the workforce instead of paid out.

The mechanism

When demand for a service outruns the local labor supply, the market answer is higher pay until enough workers show up. These firms route around that instead.

BrightView satisfies seasonal peaks with H-2B temporary-visa workers (~1,500 in FY2025) rather than raising wages to attract domestic seasonal labor, keeping a labor-heavy platform profitable at $245.5M maintenance segment adjusted EBITDA.

Aveanna scales pediatric private-duty home care staffed by aides paid near minimum, growing revenue 15.9% and adjusted EBITDA 25.2% in Q1 2026 while the underlying labor stays cheap and hard to retain.

Comfort Systems lets backlog balloon to $14.06B and grows organically 51% but frames the binding constraint as skilled-trades availability, meaning demand it can't staff simply waits rather than clearing through higher wages.

In each case the gap between what labor could command in a clearing market and what it's actually paid is retained as margin, and the adjustment burden falls on the workforce.

Why now

The 2025-2026 window makes the squeeze visible in the filings. Comfort Systems' backlog jumped from $8.12B a year earlier to $14.06B at Q2 2026 on $3.27B quarterly revenue and 51% organic growth in Q1 2026, with management repeatedly naming technician availability, not demand, as the limit. In the same period BrightView disclosed cutting H-2B seasonal workers from ~2,000 to ~1,500 while running an 18,600-person, $2.67B service platform, and Aveanna grew Q1 2026 revenue 15.9% to $647.9M on low-wage home-care labor. Demand that these firms literally cannot staff, alongside continued reliance on visa and near-floor labor, is the dated inflection.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
The workers: H-2B seasonal laborers tied to a single employer with limited bargaining power, home-care aides paid near the minimum wage, and skilled tradespeople whose scarcity should be pushing pay far higher than it is. Customers and communities also pay in the form of work that waits, because capacity is capped at the crew a firm already has.
Who profits
BrightView, which keeps a labor-intensive $2.67B maintenance-and-development business profitable partly on visa-mediated seasonal labor; Aveanna, which grows adjusted EBITDA 25.2% on aide wages near the floor; and Comfort Systems, whose $1.02B FY2025 net income and $14.06B backlog rest on technicians it pays less than a truly clearing market would demand.

The tension

Firms will say H-2B fills genuine seasonal gaps, home-care rates are constrained by Medicaid reimbursement, and skilled trades take years to train so wages can't fix scarcity overnight. All partly true, yet each is also a way to avoid letting compensation rise to the level the demand implies, and the workforce carries the difference.

The surprising part

Comfort Systems' backlog more than tripled over two years to $14.06B and it is still turning down or delaying work, not because customers won't pay but because it can't find enough technicians and won't bid pay up fast enough to conjure them. In a genuinely clearing market that scarcity would be the biggest raise skilled trades have seen in a generation; instead it shows up as a growing backlog and record margins.

Why it matters

When service firms cap growth, lean on visa labor, or hold wages near the floor rather than let pay clear the market, the productivity gains flow to margins and the adjustment cost lands on workers with the least leverage. It suppresses wages in exactly the essential, hard-to-automate jobs, keeping frontline pay disconnected from the real scarcity of the labor and quietly transferring value from workers to employers and shareholders.

Across landscaping, home care, and the building trades, companies are managing around a labor shortage instead of paying to end it, and the difference between what the work is worth and what the worker is paid is the invisible tax.
