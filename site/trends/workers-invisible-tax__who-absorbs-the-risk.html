The Invisible Carriers

Workers in labor-intensive industries absorb physical, scheduling, and reimbursement risk that companies systematically shift downstream.

The most profitable operating models in healthcare, landscaping, and packaging work by pushing occupational hazards, wage compression, and staffing instability onto workers and their families. This is not accidental. It is structural. Companies optimize operations by treating worker risk absorption as a cost-free input to the business model, even as that risk accumulates in unseen ways—through preventable injuries, career instability, and family dependence on uncertain work schedules.

The mechanism

Labor-intensive companies achieve profitability by separating worker cost control from worker risk exposure. In healthcare home care (Aveanna), workers provide private duty support to medically complex families for Medicaid and insurance reimbursement rates that compress wages. In landscaping (BrightView), seasonal work and visa-dependent employment create schedules and job security that shift scheduling risk entirely to workers. In packaging (Graphic Packaging) and distribution (MSC Industrial), workforce reduction is a direct cost-cutting lever while service density expectations on remaining workers increase without proportional wage growth.

The companies explicitly frame their operating models around labor efficiency gains that do not translate to worker compensation. BrightView reports that approximately 1,500 seasonal workers through the H-2B visa program satisfied part of its labor needs in 2025, down from 2,000 in 2024—a reduction that reflects cost optimization, not genuine labor-market stabilization. MSC Industrial simultaneously reduces field-sales associate headcount from 2,721 to 2,496 while expanding vending machines and In-Plant service locations, explicitly moving toward 'service intensity being software-enabled rather than purely labor-added.' The load shifts to remaining workers.

In elder care, Brookdale's profitability improvement is built on occupancy recovery while wage and care-intensity costs remain elevated. The company achieves this through operational discipline rather than genuine wage growth, meaning workers absorb more residents per caregiver or more complex care acuity without corresponding compensation increases. Aveanna's home-care model similarly scales private duty revenue by expanding the caregiver deployment base, not by improving per-worker economics. The business grows by deploying more caregivers into uncertain home-based work, which creates scheduling unpredictability and dependence on employer-controlled benefit structures.

The physical risk component is concentrated and hidden. Landscaping workers at BrightView face equipment operation, chemical exposure, and seasonal employment instability. Home-care workers at Aveanna face needlestick risk, bloodborne pathogen exposure, and the physical demands of patient-handling in uncontrolled home settings. Elder-care workers at Brookdale face back injury, aggressive-resident incidents, and infectious-disease exposure intensified by rising resident acuity. Packaging workers at Graphic Packaging face machinery interaction and repetitive-motion injury from accelerated line speeds tied to productivity targets. None of these risks decline when companies optimize labor efficiency. They concentrate on fewer workers or shift to precarious employment cohorts (visa workers, contingent staff) who have less power to refuse dangerous conditions.

Why now

The profitability improvement across these five companies in 2025-2026 is coincident with deliberate labor-cost architecture. BrightView reduced seasonal visa workers while maintaining maintenance revenue growth, signaling cost optimization. Graphic Packaging committed to $60M cost reduction including workforce reductions as part of restructuring. MSC Industrial achieved margin expansion while reducing field headcount. Brookdale achieved highest adjusted-EBITDA growth in over a decade while wage pressures remained elevated. Aveanna scaled pediatric private duty revenue by 15.9% in Q1 2026 through expanded caregiver networks. The pattern is consistent: companies achieve earnings growth by making labor cost structures more efficient, which invariably means workers absorb more uncertainty, risk, and workload per dollar of compensation.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
The workers themselves. A BrightView seasonal landscaper on an H-2B visa faces job uncertainty tied to program quota changes, employer dependence for housing, and wage compression that reflects visa-program exploitation. An Aveanna pediatric-care aide faces unpredictable home visit schedules, physical demands of patient handling, and reimbursement-rate dependence that keeps her wage stagnant even as service volume grows. A Brookdale nursing aide works with higher acuity residents while her cost-per-resident hasn't risen proportionally. An MSC field-sales associate covers more territory because vending and digital reduce branch density. A Graphic Packaging line worker faces accelerated production targets after her facility cuts 10% of headcount. The common pattern: operational efficiency gains are extracted as reduced headcount or compressed wage growth, concentrating risk on remaining workers.
Who profits
The companies and their shareholders. Aveanna's private duty segment grows revenue faster than industry wage trends, expanding margin by deploying cheaper labor. BrightView's maintenance business stabilizes recurring revenue while seasonal workforce optimization reduces overall labor cost as a percentage of sales. Brookdale's adjusted EBITDA reached 19.1% growth in 2025 while care-intensity and wage costs remained elevated, meaning occupancy and rate gains compounded without commensurate wage adjustment. MSC Industrial's operating margin expanded to 10.2% in Q3 FY2026 while field associate headcount fell, proving that service density can be maintained with fewer (busier) workers. Graphic Packaging avoids $200M in future capital spending through workforce reduction and restructuring, protecting free cash flow and shareholder returns.

The tension

The model breaks when worker supply physically breaks. If Aveanna loses home-care workers to competing employers offering stability or higher wages, pediatric case continuity fails and reimbursement risk rises. If BrightView cannot fill seasonal H-2B quotas due to visa policy or worker refusal, maintenance revenue stalls. If Brookdale's occupancy gains depend on care quality and worker burnout erodes that quality, RevPAR improvement reverses. If MSC's field associates, carrying higher territory loads, produce more errors or customer-service failures, margin gains evaporate. If Graphic Packaging's line speeds exceed ergonomic safety tolerances, injury rates spike and workers comp costs explode. The entire optimization rests on workers absorbing risk without breaking. That tension is not acknowledged in earnings calls or SEC filings.

The surprising part

The companies do not see this as a labor problem at all. They frame it as operational efficiency, cost discipline, and productivity. Aveanna does not say 'we deploy more caregivers into uncertain schedules.' It says 'private duty revenue growth of $75.7M driven by family-centered home-care scale.' BrightView does not say 'we reduced visa workers, cutting job security.' It says 'seasonal labor optimization.' MSC does not say 'we expect the same revenue from fewer field people.' It says 'service intensity being software-enabled rather than labor-added.' Brookdale does not say 'we improve margins by extracting more care work per dollar spent.' It says 'occupancy recovery and RevPAR growth.' The language completely severs the financial gain from the worker-side burden. That separation is not a side effect—it is the entire architecture of the model.

Why it matters

This pattern matters because it normalizes a growth model where worker wellbeing is treated as a cost to minimize rather than a constraint on acceptable profit. When major publicly-traded companies optimize earnings by reducing worker headcount, compressing wages, or concentrating risk on visa-dependent cohorts, they are signaling that worker-side sustainability is not a business constraint. That message cascades: smaller employers follow. Entire industries adopt the same efficiency logic. Workers in healthcare, landscaping, packaging, and distribution work become progressively less secure, less compensated, and more at-risk even as the companies serving those sectors grow more profitable. The hidden tax is paid by workers, their families, and the public systems (emergency care, disability, social services) that absorb the downstream consequences.

Profitable growth in labor-intensive industries is engineered by systematically shifting operational risk, scheduling uncertainty, physical hazard, and wage compression onto workers whose alternatives are limited by employment scarcity, visa dependence, or lack of skill portability.
