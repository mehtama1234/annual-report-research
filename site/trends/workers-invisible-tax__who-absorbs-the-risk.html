The Invisible Carriers

Workers in labor-intensive industries absorb physical, scheduling, and reimbursement risk that companies systematically shift downstream.

The most profitable operating models in healthcare, landscaping, and industrial services work by pushing occupational hazards, wage compression, and staffing instability onto workers. This is structural. Companies optimize by treating worker risk absorption as a cost-free input to the business model. Aveanna's private duty revenue grew $75.7M in Q1 2026 while filings do not disclose caregiver wage changes. BrightView employed 1,500 seasonal H-2B workers in 2025 (down from 2,000 in 2024), showing visa-dependent cost optimization. MSC reduced field sales associates from 2,721 to 2,496 while expanding vending and In-Plant locations, moving service density away from employed staff. These are not efficiency gains—they are risk transfers.

The mechanism

Labor-intensive companies achieve profitability by separating worker cost control from worker risk exposure. In healthcare home care (Aveanna), workers provide private duty support to medically complex families for Medicaid and insurance reimbursement rates that compress wages. Aveanna's Q1 2026 private duty revenue increased $75.7M but filings do not disclose caregiver wage trends or turnover rates. In landscaping (BrightView), seasonal work and visa-dependent employment create schedules and job security that shift entirely to workers. BrightView reported 1,500 H-2B workers in 2025 (down from 2,000 in 2024)—a reduction that reflects cost optimization. In industrial services (MSC), field headcount fell from 2,721 to 2,496 while vending machines rose to 30,790 and operating margin expanded to 10.2%.

Companies explicitly frame labor efficiency gains as cost optimization that does not translate to worker compensation. MSC is most transparent: the company is moving toward 'service intensity being software-enabled rather than purely labor-added,' meaning fewer field people managing the same or higher territory. BrightView's reduction from 2,000 to 1,500 H-2B workers while maintaining maintenance revenue shows cost efficiency prioritized over labor continuity. Aveanna's private duty revenue growth of $75.7M (Q1 2026) is framed as 'scaling a home-care model,' not as addressing caregiver wage pressure or retention.

In elder and home care, Aveanna's model achieves profitability improvement through operational discipline rather than wage growth. Q1 2026 adjusted EBITDA grew 25.2% to $84.4M while the company does not disclose whether caregiver compensation kept pace with revenue growth. BrightSpring's home health census grew 54% year-over-year with operating leverage, suggesting that throughput is improving faster than headcount. The load shifts to remaining workers: more patients per caregiver, more complex care acuity without compensation increases.

The physical and occupational risk component is concentrated and hidden. Landscaping workers at BrightView face equipment operation, chemical exposure, and seasonal employment instability. Home-care workers at Aveanna face needlestick risk, bloodborne pathogen exposure, and physical demands of patient handling in uncontrolled home settings. MSC field associates face machinery interaction and occupational hazards from industrial customer environments. None of these risks decline when companies optimize labor efficiency. They concentrate on fewer workers or shift to precarious employment cohorts (visa workers, contingent staff) who have less power to refuse dangerous conditions.

Why now

The profitability improvement across these companies in 2025-2026 is coincident with deliberate labor-cost architecture. BrightView reduced seasonal visa workers from 2,000 to 1,500 while maintaining revenue growth, signaling cost optimization. Aveanna scaled private duty revenue by $75.7M in Q1 2026 through expanded caregiver networks (not wage increases). MSC achieved operating margin of 10.2% in Q3 FY2026 while reducing field headcount by 225 people and expanding vending machines by over 2,000 units. BrightSpring achieved home health census growth of 54% YoY without proportional staffing increases. The pattern is consistent: companies achieve earnings growth by making labor cost structures more efficient, which invariably means workers absorb more uncertainty, risk, and workload per dollar of compensation.

Who pays · who profits

Who pays
The workers themselves. A BrightView seasonal landscaper on an H-2B visa faces job uncertainty tied to program quota changes, employer dependence for housing, and wage compression reflecting visa-program exploitation. An Aveanna pediatric-care aide faces unpredictable home visit schedules, physical demands of patient handling, and reimbursement-rate dependence that keeps wages stagnant even as service volume grows. A BrightSpring home-health worker works with higher acuity residents while cost-per-resident hasn't risen proportionally. An MSC field-sales associate covers more territory because vending and digital reduce branch density. Earnings pressure flows downward: operational efficiency gains are extracted as reduced headcount or compressed wage growth, concentrating risk on remaining workers.
Who profits
The companies and their shareholders. Aveanna's adjusted EBITDA grew 25.2% in Q1 2026 by deploying cheaper labor; BrightView's net income reached $56M (2.1% margin) on a $2.67B revenue base, showing that despite large labor deployment, profit margins stay thin for the company but profit-per-worker is negative. MSC expanded operating margin to 10.2% while reducing field headcount by 225 people, proving that service density can be maintained with fewer (busier) workers. Earnings growth in all three companies is achieved not through wage gains but through operational leverage extraction from constrained labor.

The tension

The model breaks when worker supply physically breaks. If Aveanna loses home-care workers to competing employers offering stability or higher wages, case continuity fails and reimbursement risk rises. If BrightView cannot fill seasonal H-2B quotas due to visa policy or worker refusal, maintenance revenue stalls. If MSC's field associates carrying higher territory loads produce more errors or customer-service failures, margin gains evaporate. If BrightSpring's home-health census grows faster than staff can manage, care quality deteriorates and regulatory risk emerges. The entire optimization rests on workers absorbing risk without breaking. That tension is not acknowledged in earnings calls or SEC filings.

The surprising part

The companies do not see this as a labor problem at all. They frame it as operational efficiency, cost discipline, and productivity. Aveanna does not say 'we deploy more caregivers into uncertain schedules'; it says 'private duty revenue growth of $75.7M driven by family-centered home-care scale.' BrightView does not say 'we reduced visa workers, cutting job security'; it says 'seasonal labor optimization.' MSC does not say 'we expect the same revenue from fewer field people'; it says 'service intensity being software-enabled rather than labor-added.' Brookdale does not say 'we improve margins by extracting more care work per dollar spent'; it says 'occupancy recovery and RevPAR growth.' The language completely severs the financial gain from the worker-side burden. That separation is not a side effect—it is the entire architecture of the model.

Why it matters

This pattern matters because it normalizes a growth model where worker wellbeing is treated as a cost to minimize rather than a constraint on acceptable profit. When major publicly-traded companies optimize earnings by reducing worker headcount, compressing wages, or concentrating risk on visa-dependent cohorts, they signal that worker-side sustainability is not a business constraint. That message cascades: smaller employers follow. Entire industries adopt the same efficiency logic. Workers in healthcare, landscaping, and industrial services become progressively less secure, less compensated, and more at-risk even as companies serving those sectors grow more profitable. The hidden tax is paid by workers, their families, and public systems (emergency care, disability, social services) that absorb downstream consequences.

Profitable growth in labor-intensive industries is engineered by systematically shifting operational risk, scheduling uncertainty, physical hazard, and wage compression onto workers whose alternatives are limited by employment scarcity, visa dependence, or lack of skill portability.
